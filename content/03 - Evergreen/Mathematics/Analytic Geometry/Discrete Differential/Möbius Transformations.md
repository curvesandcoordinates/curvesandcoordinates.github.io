Given geometric considerations in $\mathbb{CP}^{1}$ it is always interesting to consider the natural transformation in this space. These are Möbius transformations and their existence is naturally given by multiple different constructions of the complex projective space.

### Construction via Stereographic Projection

A natural way to think of the [[Complex Projective Line|complex projective line]] is to use the stereographic projection given by
$\pi_{0}: S^{2}\setminus \set{(0, 0,1)} \to \C, \quad (a, c, d) \mapsto \frac{a}{1-c} + i \frac{b}{1-c}$
which maps the complex plane to all points of the sphere except for one. Idenfitfying $\infty \in \mathbb{CP}^{1}$ to the north pole gives us a natual isomorphism of the complex projective line. We can then think of Möbius transformations as transformations of the complex projective line corresponding to rigid motions of the sphere. Without calculations we can see that this set of trasnformations might look wild in the context of the projective line but in reality is very restrictive.

### Construction Via Inverting Circles

Let us consider the group generated by inversion s on circles, namely by maps
$z \mapsto r^{2} \frac{z-c}{\norm{z-c}^{2}} + c$
Which describes the inversion on a circle with center $c$ and radius $r$. Trying to construct the map from scratch consider that we can take a general circle, scale it down to be a unit circle, do usual inversion and scale it back again.

These maps produce some interesting special cases.

- Consider for instance $c = 0$ and $r = a$ which gives us $z \mapsto \frac{a^{2}}{\bar{z}}$
- Choosing $c = i \cdot r$ gives in the limit as $r \to \infty$ that the map describes the inversion on the real axis since $z \mapsto r^{2} \frac{z-ir}{\norm{z-ir}^{2}} + ir = \frac{r^{2}}{\bar{z} + ir} + ir = \frac{r^{2} + \bar{z}ir - r^{2}}{\bar{z} + ir} \overset{r \to \infty}{\longrightarrow} \bar{z}$
- By a similar procedure we obtain the inversion on any straight line by this. The inversion along two lines gives a rotation around their intersection or if they are parallel a translation

By combining more and more inversions we can obtain a general form for the maps in this group. They are called fractional linear maps or Möbius Transformations.
$z \mapsto \frac{az + b}{cz + d} \quad z \mapsto \frac{a\bar{z} + b}{c \bar{z} + d}$
with $ad - bc \neq 0$.
Note that the zero of the denominator produces a pole and likewise $\frac{a}{c}$ can be viewed as the image of $\infty$. One should think of this as maps acting on the one-point compactification $\C \cup \set{\infty} = \C^{*}$ which in turn can be viewd as $\C P^{1}$

## General Fractional Linear Maps

Möbius transformations are often introduced as maps
$z \mapsto \frac{az +b}{cz + d}$
with $ad - bc \neq 0$. We can naturally see that this has a pole and we can see $\frac{a}{c}$ as the image of $\infty$ of the map. Meaning this is a natural map to consider in $\C^{*} = \C \cup \set{\infty} \cong \mathbb{CP}^{1}$

## Connecting the Möbius transformations to Cross-Ratio

An important statement to consider when looking at Möbius geometry is how we can generate them. Given a set of four points gives us a Möbius transformation. This is connected to the relevant invariant under this transformation, the [[Cross-Ratio]]. We can then see that the following is true

> [!lemma|\*] Characterization fo Möbius Transformations via Cross-Ratios
> Let $a, b\in \mathbb{CP}^{1}$ in general position be given and choose $\mu \in \C$. Then for every $d \in \mathbb{CP}^1$ we can find a point $c \in \mathbb{CP}^1$ such that $\mathrm{cr}(a, b, c, d) = \mu$. This correspondence gives us a Möbius transformation
> $M: \mathbb{CP}^{1} \to \mathbb{CP}^{1}, \quad d \mapsto c$
> such that $\mathrm{cr}(a, b, M(d), d) = \mu$.

`\begin{proof}`
We consider that the map $M$ is implicitly defined by
$\mathrm{cr}(a, b, c, d) = \mu$
with $c = M(d)$.
Calculating explicitly and treating the crossratio as a function of $d$ we get

$$$\begin{align*}
\mu &= \mathrm{cr}(a, b, c, d) \\
&= \frac{(a - c)(b - d)}{(a-d)(b-c)} \\
&= \frac{ab - ad - bc + cd}{ab - ac - db + dc} \\
\implies (ab - ac - db + dc) \mu &= ab - ad - bc + cd \\
ab\mu - ac\mu - db\mu + dc\mu &= ab - ad - bc + cd \\
 bc - ac\mu + dc\mu - cd &= ab - ad -ab\mu + db\mu
\end{align*}$$
This in the end gives us
$$c (b - a \mu + d \mu - d) = db \mu - ad + ab - ab \mu$$
which in the end describes our function as
$$M(d) = c = \frac{db \mu - ad + ab - ab \mu}{b - a \mu + d \mu - d} = \frac{(b \mu - a) d + (ab - ab \mu)}{(\mu - 1) d + (b - a \mu)}$$
which is a function in $d$ and actually is a Möbius function since
$$(b \mu - a) (b - a \mu) - (ab - ab \mu) (\mu - 1) = b^{2} \mu - ab \mu^{2} - ab + a^{2} \mu - 2ab \mu + ab + ab \mu^{2} = a^{2} \mu + b^{2} \mu - 2ab \mu = \mu (a - b)^{2}$$
which is non zero in general. This means our map is Möbius.
`\end{proof}`
$$$
