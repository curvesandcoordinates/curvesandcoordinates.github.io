The multiplication in our [[Algebras]] is very freely defined. We can specify it a little more by forcing it to be anti-commutative in a certain sense. We will specialise this to constructing $\R$-Vector spaces since that is the way we use it when considering the wedge product of [[Differential Forms on Manifolds|differential forms]] but this can all be generalised.

> [!definition|\*] Exterior Algebra over $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$
> We construct a free $\R$-Vector Space with Basis
> $B = \set{\alpha_{i_1} \cdot \ldots \cdot \alpha_{i_k} \,\middle|\, i_1 < \ldots < i_k, k \in \set{1, 2, \ldots, n}}$
> for some $n \in \mathbb{N}$. We denote this space as
> $\Lambda[\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n] := \Span_\R(B)$
> This space will be considered an Algebra, given the definition of an associative and distributive multiplication satisfying
>
> $$$\begin{aligned}
>         \alpha_i \cdot \alpha_j &= - \alpha_j \cdot \alpha_i \\
>         \alpha_i^2 &= 0    
> \end{aligned}$$
> We call this space the *exterior algebra* over $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$. The *k-homogeneous elements* of this algebra $\Lambda^k[\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n] := \Span_\R(C)$ where
> $$C = \set{b \in B \,\middle|\, \deg(b) = k}$$
> Specifically we define the *degree* of a basis element as $\deg(\alpha_{i_1} \cdot \ldots \cdot \alpha_{i_k}) = \deg(\alpha_{i_1})\ldots \deg(\alpha_{i_k})$
> $$$

We get many freedoms in this definition. The most important one is that the degree of every generating element is not predetermined. This means we can construct spaces, like $\Lambda[x_2, x_3]$ with $\deg(x_2) = 2$ and $\deg(x_3) = 3$ and get a space whose Basis is given by $B = \set{x_2, x_3, x_2 \cdot x_3}$ where the homogeneous elements are given by

$$$\begin{aligned}
    \Lambda^2[x_2, x_3] &= \Span_\R (x_2) \\
    \Lambda^3[x_2, x_3] &= \Span_\R (x_3) \\
    \Lambda^6[x_2, x_3] &= \Span_\R (x_2 \cdot x_3)
\end{aligned}$$
> [!remark|*]
> In this example, we have used the notation that every generator $x_i$ is of degree $i$. We will keep using this convention as a shorthand, but it should be noted that this is not necessary.

## Tensor Algebra Contex
In the contex of exterior algebras we can generally develop this theory and consider the objects in a similar way



> [!definition|*] Exterior Algebra
> Let $T(M)$ be the tensor algebra over some $R$-Module $M$. The exterior algebra of $M$ is then again a quotient
> $$\Lambda(M) = \faktor{T(M)}{I}$$
> where $I$ is the ideal generated by $\set{m \otimes m \midline m \in M}$.
> The multiplication in $\Lambda(M)$ is denoted by $u\wedge v$ and is called the wedge or exterior product.


> [!remark|*]
> We can create a similar look to the definition of symmetric algebras by considering
> $$I_{k} = \span_{R} \set{x \otimes (m \otimes m) \otimes y \midline x \in M^{\otimes i}, y \in M^{\otimes j}, m \in M,  i + j + 2 = k}$$
> with $I_{0} = 0 \in R$ and $I_{1} = 0 \in M$.
> We can then write
> $$\Lambda(M) = \bigoplus_{k \in \N} \Lambda^{k}(M)$$
> with $\Lambda^{k}(M) = \faktor{M^{\otimes k}}{I_{k}}$.
> This structure also is a graded algebra and we add the slightly new notation that
> $$m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{n}$$
> is the image of $m_{1} \otimes m_{2} \otimes \ldots \otimes m_{n}$ under the quotient map.


Similar to what we had before we can consider elements of $\Lambda(M)$ as in one of the submodules $\Lambda^{n}(M)$ which we will call homogeneous of degree $n$. We will call them elementary exterior products if they have the form of a simple product
$$m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{n}$$
with $m_{i} \in M$

> [!corollary|*]
> The map $M^{n} \to \Lambda^{n}(M), (m_{1}, \ldots, m_{n}) \mapsto m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{n}$ is alternating (and thus antisymmetric)

`\begin{proof}`
First note that interchanging two consecutive entries in $m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{n}$ and multiplying by $-1$ does not change $m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{n} \in \Lambda^{n}(M)$ as
$$m_{1} \otimes m_{i+1} = - m_{i+1} \otimes m_{1} + \left((m_{i+1} + m_{i}) \otimes (m_{i+1} + m_{i}) - m_{i+1} \otimes m_{i+1} - m_{i} \otimes m_{i}\right)$$
and since the bracket is an element of the ideal we know that
$$m_{i} \wedge m_{i+1} = - m_{i+1} \wedge m_{i}$$

Hence whenever $m_{i} = m_{j}$ for some $i \neq j$ we can make them consequtive without leaving the coset. The resulting elementary tensor then becomes an element of $I$ and is thus $0$ when mapped to $\Lambda^{n}(M)$.
`\end{proof}`

Swapping factors does not always lead to a sign change: if the elements are not homogeneous there is no relation between $u \wedge v$ and $v \wedge u$. For homgeneous elements it depends on the degrees:

> [!corollary|*]
> Let $M$ be an $R$-module and $u \in \Lambda^{k}(M), v \in \Lambda^{l}(M)$ then the following is true
> 1. $u \wedge v = (-1)^{kl} v \wedge u$
> 2. If $k$ is odd and $\frac{1}{2} \in R$, then $u \wedge u = 0$

`\begin{proof}`
For $1.$ consider that by linearity it suffices to consider elementary exterior products. To this end let $u = u_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge u_{k}$ and $v = v_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge v_{l}$. Then by swapping neighbours we get
$$\begin{align*}
u \wedge v &= u_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge u_{k} \wedge v \\
&= (-1)^{l} \cdot u_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge v \wedge u_{k} \\
&\vdots \\
&={(-1)^{l}}^{k} \cdot v \wedge u_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge u_{k} \\
&= (-1)^{kl} v \wedge u
\end{align*}$$

For $2.$ consider that we know that under the assumptions that $$u \wedge u = - u \wedge u$$ meaning $2 u \wedge u = 0$
`\end{proof}`

> [!theorem|*] Universal Properties of the Exterior Algebra
> Let $M$ be an $R$-module, $\Lambda^{n}(M)$ its $n-th$ exterior power and $\Lambda(M)$ its exterior algebra.
> 1. If $A$ is an associative unital $R$-algebra and $\phi \in \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, A)$ such that $\phi(m)^{2} = 0$ for all $m \in M$, then there is a unique $R$-algebra homomorphism $\Phi: \Lambda(M) \to A$ such that $\at{\Phi}{M} = \phi$
> 2. If $\phi: M^{k} \to N$ is an alternating multilinear map, then there is a unique $\Phi \in \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(\Lambda^{k}(M), N)$ such that $\phi = \Phi \circ \iota$ where $\iota: M^{k} \to \Lambda^{k}(M)$ maps $\iota(m_{1}, \ldots, m_{k}) = m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{k}$

`\begin{proof}`
For $1.$ the uniqueness follows from the fact that $\mathbb{1}$ and $M$ already generate $\Lambda(M)$ by algebra operations. To show existences not that there is an $R$-algebra homomorphism $\tilde{\Phi}: T(M) \to A$ such that $\at{\tilde{\Phi}}{M} = \phi$. This satisfies that for all $m \in M$ we get
$$\tilde{\Phi}(m \otimes m) = \tilde{\Phi}(m) \tilde{\Phi}(m) = \phi(m)^{2} = 0$$
which implies $\tilde{\Phi}(I) = 0$. This means we can extend this homomorphism to one defined on equivalence classes giving us a map on exterior algebras.

For $2.$ consider the unique map $\tilde{\Phi}: M^{\otimes k} \to N$ such that for all $m_{i} \in M$ we have
$$\phi(m_{1}, \ldots, m_{k}) = \tilde{\Phi}(m_{1} \otimes \ldots \otimes m_{k})$$
As $\phi$ is alternating, this is zero whenever $m_{i} = m_{j}$ for $i \neq j$, meaning that $\tilde{\Phi}(I_{k}) = 0$ and thus extending the map to equivalence classes gives us a map of the $k$-th exterior algebra elements.
`\end{proof}`

> [!theorem|*] Functoriality of the Exterior Algebra
> Let $M, N$ be $R$-modules and $\phi \in \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N)$. There is a $R$-algebra homomorphism $\Lambda(\phi): \Lambda(M) \to \Lambda(N)$ such that
> $$ \begin{CD} M   @>\phi>> N \\ @V\psi_{M} VV @VV\psi_{N}V\\  \Lambda(M) @> \Lambda(\phi)>> \Lambda(N)\end{CD}$$
> commutes, where $\Psi_{X}: X \to T(X) \to \faktor{X}{I}$ is the. canonical embedding followed by the quotient map.
> If $P$ is another $R$-Module and $\phi' \in \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(N, P)$ then
> $$\Lambda(\phi' \circ \phi) = \Lambda(\phi') \circ \Lambda(\phi)$$

`\begin{proof}`
Existences follows from the universal property applied to the map 
$$\psi_{N} \circ \phi: M \to \Lambda(N)$$
Uniqueness follows from the fact that $M$ and $1$ generate $\Lambda(M)$ by algebra operations. The composition identity follows from the uniqueness of $\Lambda(\phi' \circ \phi)$ together with the fact that $\Lambda(\phi') \circ \Lambda(\phi)$ also leads to a commuting diagram:
$$ \begin{CD} M   @>\phi>> N @>\phi'>> P \\ @V\psi_{M} VV @VV\psi_{N}V @VV\psi_{P}V\\  \Lambda(M) @> \Lambda(\phi)>> \Lambda(N) @>>\Lambda(\phi')>\Lambda(P)\end{CD}$$
`\end{proof}`

Now let us get to some remarks
> [!remark|*]
> - $\Lambda(\mathrm{id}_{M}) = \mathrm{id}_{\Lambda(M)}$
> - From $\Lambda(\phi)(m) = \phi(m)$ together with the fact that $\Lambda(\phi)$ is an $R$-Algebra homomorphism we obtain $$\Lambda(\phi): m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{n} \mapsto \phi(m_{1}) \wedge \ldots \wedge \phi(m_{n}) =: \Lambda^{n}(\phi)(m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{n})$$
> - We denote the restriction of $\Lambda(\phi)$ to homogeneous elements of degree $n$ by $\Lambda^{n}(\phi): \Lambda^{n}(M) \to \Lambda^{n}(N)$


> [!proposition|*] Antisymmetrization /Alternation
> Let $M$ be an $R$-Module and $n! \in R^{\times}$. Then $\mathrm{alt}: M^{\otimes n} \to \Lambda_{n}(M)$ defined by $$\mathrm{alt}(t) = \frac{1}{n!} \sum\limits_{\sigma \in S_{n}} \sgn(\sigma) \widehat{\sigma}(t)$$
> is a surjective $R$-module homomorphism with $\mathrm{alt}^{2} = \mathrm{alt}$ and $\ker(\mathrm{alt}) = I_{n}$

`\begin{proof}`
We first verify, that the $\mathrm{alt}$ map indeed mapts into the right space. For any $\pi \in S_{n}$:
$$\begin{align*}
\widehat{\pi} \circ \mathrm{alt}(t) = \frac{1}{n!} \sum\limits_{\sigma \in S_{n}} \sgn(\sigma) \widehat{\pi} \circ \widehat{\sigma}(t) = \frac{\sgn(\pi)}{n!} \sum\limits_{\sigma \in S_{n}} \sgn(\pi \circ \sigma) \widehat{\pi \circ \sigma}(t) = \sgn(\pi) \mathrm{alt}(t)
\end{align*}$$
Moreover, for any $t \in \Lambda_{n}(M)$ we have that
$$\mathrm{alt}(t) = \frac{1}{n!} \sum\limits_{\sigma} \sgn(\sigma) \widehat{\sigma}(t) = \frac{1}{n!} \sum\limits_{\sigma} \sgn(\sigma)^{2} t = t$$
so $\mathrm{alt}$ is a surjective projection.
Similar to the surjective case one can show that $I_{n}$ can be expressed as
$$I_{n} = \span_{\R} \set{t - \sgn(\sigma) \widehat{\sigma}(t) \midline \sigma \in S_{n}, t \in M^{\otimes n}}$$
This means that 
$I_{n} \subset \ker(\mathrm{alt})$ since $$\mathrm{alt}(t - \sgn(\sigma) \widehat{\sigma}) = \frac{1}{n!} \sum\limits_{\pi \in S_{n}} \left( \sgn(\pi) \widehat{\pi}(t) - \sgn(\pi \circ \sigma) \widehat{\pi \circ \sigma}(t)\right) = 0$$
Conversely, if $\mathrm{alt}(t) = 0$ then $t = t - \mathrm{alt}(t) = \frac{1}{n!} \sum\limits_{\sigma \in S_{n}}\left(t - \sgn(\sigma) \widehat{\sigma}(t)\right) \in I_{n}$ so $\ker(\mathrm{alt}) \subset I_{n}$
`\end{proof}`

> [!corollary|*]
> Let $M$ be an $R$-Module and $n! \in R^{\times}$. Then 
> 1. $M^{\otimes n} = \Lambda_{n}(M) \oplus I_{n}$
> 2. $\Lambda_{n}(M) \to \Lambda^{n}(M)$ given by the quotient map $M^{\otimes n} \to \faktor{M^{\otimes n}}{I_{n}}$ is an $R$-Module isomorphism.

`\begin{proof}`
Concerning $1.$ we can consider that for any $t \in M^{\otimes n}$ we have $\mathrm{alt}(t) \in \Lambda_{n}(M)$ and $t - \mathrm{alt}(t) \in I_{n}$. So $M^{\otimes n} = \lambda_{n}(M) + I_{n}$. The directness of the sum follows from $t \in \Lambda_{n}(M) \cap I_{n} = \im \mathrm{alt} \cap \ker(\mathrm{alt}) = \set{0}$

Concerning $2.$ we can simply apply Noether’s first isomorphism theorem and note that for any $f \in \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N)$ that $\faktor{M}{\ker(f)} \cong \im(f)$ with the quotient map being the isomorphism. Applied to $f = \mathrm{alt}$ we get the result.
`\end{proof}`

> [!proposition|*]
> Let $M$ be a free $R$-module with finite basis $v_{1}, \ldots, v_{d}$. If $1 \leq n \leq d$ then $$\set{v_{i_{1}} \wedge v_{i_{2}} \wedge \ldots \wedge v_{i_{n}}}_{1 \leq i_{1} \leq \ldots \leq i_{n} \leq d}$$
> is a basis of $\Lambda^{n}(M)$. For $n > d$ we get $\Lambda^{n}(M) = 0$.
> Consequently
> $$\dim_{\R}(\Lambda^{n}(M)) = \binom{d}{n} \text{ and } \dim_{\R}(\Lambda(M)) = 2^{d}$$

`\begin{proof}`
As we know that $\set{v_{i_{1}} \otimes \ldots \otimes v_{i_{n}}}$ form a basis of $M^{\otimes n}$, their image unter the quotient map $\set{v_{i_{1}} \wedge \ldots \wedge v_{i_{n}}}$ must then span the space $\Lambda^{n}(M)$. If we restrict to $1 \leq i_{1} < \ldots < i_{n} \leq d$ since all other cases are either zero for $i_{k} = i_{l}$ with $k \neq l$ or merely differy by a sign. It remains to to prove that this set is linearly independent (since it is by previous argument generating). To this end, consider the alternating multilinear map
$$M^{n} \ni (m_{1}, \ldots, m_{n}) \mapsto \sum\limits_{\sigma \in S_{n}} \sgn(\sigma) m_{\sigma(1)} \otimes \ldots \otimes m_{\sigma(n)}$$
By the second universal property of exterior powers we know there exists a map $\alpha: \Lambda^{n}(M) \to M^{\otimes n}$ such that
$$\alpha(m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{n}) = \sum\limits_{\sigma \in S_{n}} \sgn(\sigma) m_{\sigma(1)} \otimes \ldots \otimes m_{\sigma(n)}$$
Now suppose that $\sum\limits_{I} c_{I} v_{I} = 0$ where $I =(i_{1}, \ldots, i_{n})$ runs over all strictly increasing sequences and $v_{I}:= v_{i_{1}} \wedge \ldots \wedge v_{i_{n}}$. Then
$$0 = \alpha\left( \sum\limits_{I} c_{I} v_{I} \right) = \sum\limits_{I} \sum\limits_{\sigma} c_{I} \sgn(\sigma) v_{\sigma(i_{1})} \otimes \ldots \otimes v_{\sigma(i_{n})}$$
but since the tensor product is part of our basis of $M^{\otimes n}$ which implies $c_{I} = 0$ and thus we have linear independence.

Any $v \in \Lambda^{n}(M)$ can be decomposed as $v = \sum\limits_{I} c_{I} v_{I}$ where now $I = \set{1, \ldots, d}^{n}$. If $n > d$ then every $I$ has to contain an equal pair $v_{i_{k}} = v_{i_{l}}$ for $k \neq l$. So $v_{I} = 0$ and thus $\Lambda^{n}(M) = 0$. Then $$\dim(\Lambda(M)) = \sum\limits_{n = 0}^{d} \binom{d}{n} = 2^{d}$$
`\end{proof}`

> [!remark|*]
> Note that in particular that for $\dim_{R}(M) = d$ we have that
> $$\dim_{R}\Lambda^{d}(M) = 1 \quad \dim_{R} \Lambda^{k}(M) = \dim_{R} \Lambda^{d-k}(M)$$

> [!definition|*] Determinant
> Let $M$ be a free $R$-Module with $\dim_{R}(M) =: \in \N$ and $f \in \mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$. Then the determinant of $f$ is dined as $\det(f) \in R$ such that
> $$\Lambda^{d}(f): \Lambda^{d}(M) \to \Lambda^{d}(M), \quad x \mapsto \det(f) x$$

Note that this is well-defined sicne $\Lambda^{d}$ is one-dimensional and therefore 
$$\Lambda^{d}(f): \Lambda^{d}(M) \to \Lambda^{d}(M) $$
a scalar multiplication. Note that $x = m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{d}$ and $\Lambda^{d}(f)(x) = f(m_{1}) \wedge \ldots \wedge f(m_{d})$
This means that
$$f(m_{1}) \wedge \ldots \wedge f(m_{d}) = \det(f) m_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge m_{d}$$


> [!corollary|*]
> For $f, g \in \mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$ we get $\det(f \circ g) = \det(f) \cdot \det(g)$

`\begin{proof}`
This follows from the functoriality of $\Lambda$
`\end{proof}`

> [!corollary|*] Leibniz Formula of Endomorphisms
> If $f \in \mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$ is represented as $f(e_{j}) = \sum\limits_{i} A_{ij} e_{i}$ with $A_{ij} \in R$ and $e_{1}, \ldots, e_{d}$ a basis of $M$, then
> $$\det(f) = \sum\limits_{\sigma \in S_{d}} \sgn(\sigma) \left(\prod_{k=1}^{d} A_{\sigma(k), k}\right)$$
> 

`\begin{proof}`
By using the definition of our determinant we consider
$$\begin{align*}
f(e_{1}) \wedge \ldots \wedge f(e_{d}) &= \left(\sum\limits_{i_{1} = 1}^{d} A_{i_{1}, 1} e_{i_{1}}\right) \wedge \ldots \wedge \left(\sum\limits_{i_{d} = 1}^{d} A_{i_{d}, 1} e_{i_{d}}\right) \\
&= \sum\limits_{i_{1}, \ldots, i_{d}} \left(\prod_{k = 1}^{d} A_{i_{k}, k}\right) e_{i_{1}} \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{i_{d}} \\
&= \sum\limits_{\sigma \in S_{d}} \sgn(\sigma) \left(\prod_{k=1}^{d} A_{\sigma(k), k}\right) e_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{d}
\end{align*}$$
giving us our formula.
`\end{proof}`


We will now try to work towards Laplace Expansion int this situation. We need to note that when considering Laplace expansion in the vector space case we consider submatrices of a specific. We will generalise this generation of submatrices and see their role in the exterior algebra construction by using compund matrices.

> [!definition|*] Compound Matrix
> For $A \in R^{n \times m}$ and $k \in \set{1, \ldots, \min \set{n, m}}$ the $k$-th compound matrix $C_{k}(A) \in R^{\binom{n}{k} \times \binom{m}{k} }$ is defined as
> $$(C_{k}(A))_{i, j} := \det(A_{I, J})$$
> Where $A_{I,J}$ are $k \times k$ minors of $A$. Where we consider $I \subset [n]$ and $J \subset [m]$ run over all $k$-element subsets and are orderd lexicographically

The lexicographic Ordering part can seem tricky at first so let us consider some examples
> [!example|*]
> - $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3\\4 & 5 & 6\end{pmatrix}$ then $C_{1}(A) = A, C_{2}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}\det\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\4 & 5\end{pmatrix} & \det \begin{pmatrix}1 & 3\\4 & 6\end{pmatrix}  & \det \begin{pmatrix}2 & 3\\5 & 6\end{pmatrix}  \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-3 & -6 & -3\end{pmatrix}$
> - $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9\end{pmatrix}$ then $C_{1}(A) = A$ and $C_{2} = \begin{pmatrix} \det \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\4 & 5\end{pmatrix}  & \det \begin{pmatrix}1 & 3\\4 & 6\end{pmatrix}  & \det \begin{pmatrix}2 & 3\\5 & 6\end{pmatrix} \\ \det \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\7 & 8\end{pmatrix}  &  \det \begin{pmatrix}1 & 3\\7 & 9\end{pmatrix}  &  \det \begin{pmatrix}2 & 3\\8 & 9\end{pmatrix} \\ \det \begin{pmatrix}4 & 5\\7 & 8\end{pmatrix}  & \det \begin{pmatrix}4 & 6\\7 & 9\end{pmatrix}  & \det \begin{pmatrix}5 & 6\\8 & 9\end{pmatrix}  \end{pmatrix}$ and $C_{3} = \det(A)$

Let us now give the compunt matrices a more intentional reason to be defined

> [!proposition|*]
> If $A \in R^{n \times m}$ represents $f \in \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(R^{m}, R^{n})$ in standard basis, then $C_{k}(A)$ represents $\Lambda^{k}(f)$ in standard basis $\set{e_{i_{1}} \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{i_{k}}}_{1 \leq i_{1} < \ldots < i_{k} \leq m} = e_{I}$

`\begin{proof}`
For $I = (i_{1}, \ldots, i_{k})$ with $1 \leq i_{1} < \ldots < i_{k} \leq m$ we can write
$$\begin{align*}
\Lambda^{k}(f)(e_{I}) &= f(e_{i_{1}}) \wedge \ldots \wedge f(e_{i_{k}}) \\
&= \left(\sum\limits_{j_{1} = 1}^{n} A_{j_{1} i_{1}} e_{j_{1}}\right) \wedge \ldots \wedge \left(\sum\limits_{j_{k} = 1}^{n} A_{j_{k} i_{k}} e_{j_{k}}\right) \\
&= \sum\limits_{j_{1}, \ldots, j_{k} = 1}^{n} \left(\prod_{r = 1}^{k} A_{j_{k} i_{k}}\right) e_{j_{1}} \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{j_{k}} \\
&= \sum\limits_{j_{1} < \ldots < j_{k}} \left(\sum\limits_{\sigma \in S_{k}} \sgn(\sigma) \prod_{r = 1}^{k} A_{j_{\sigma(r)} i_{r}}\right) e_{j_{1}} \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{j_{k}} \\
&= \sum\limits_{j_{1} < \ldots < j_{k}} \det(A_{J,I}) e_{j_{1}} \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{j_{k}}
\end{align*}$$
Identifying $A$ with $f$ we get:
$$\Lambda^{k}(A) e_{I} = \sum\limits_{J \subset [n], |J| = k} \det(A_{J, I}) e_{J}$$
which is the same as saying
$$\Lambda^{k}(A) = C_{k}(A)$$
`\end{proof}`

Transfering the functoriality of the Exteror Product space we get
> [!corollary|*]
> For all $A \in R^{n \times m}, B \in R^{m \times l}$ we have that
> $$C_{k}(AB) = C_{k}(A) \cdot C_{k}(B)$$

`\begin{proof}`
The proof is simply the previous statement with the functoriality of $\Lambda^{k}$
`\end{proof}`


Now we go towards using these to prove a generalized Laplace equation. First we consider the previous corollary in terms of determinants

> [!proposition|*] Cauchy Binet
> For $A \in R^{n \times m}, B \in R^{m \times l}, I \subset [n], L \subset [l], |I| = |L| = k$
> $$\det((AB)_{I,L}) = \sum\limits_{J \subset [m], |J| = k} \det(A_{I,J}) \det(B_{J,L})$$

`\begin{proof}`
This is just the porevious statement expressed in terms of matrix elements:
$$C_{k}(AB)_{I,L} = \sum\limits_{J} C_{k}(A)_{I,J} C_{k}(B)_{J,L}$$
`\end{proof}`

> [!proposition|*] Generalized Laplace-Expansion
> For $A \in R^{n \times m}, I \subset [n], |I| = k$ :
> $$\det(A) = \sum\limits_{J \subset [n], |J| = k} \frac{\epsilon(J)}{\epsilon(I)} \det(A_{J,I}) \det(A_{J^{c}, I^{c}})$$
> where $I^{c} := [n] \setminus I$ and $\epsilon(I) \in \set{\pm 1}$ is such that $e_{I} \wedge e_{I^{c}} = \epsilon(I) e_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{n}$

`\begin{proof}`
Let $\omega := e_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{n}$ and consider that
$$\begin{align*}
\det(A) \omega &= \Lambda^{n}(A)(\omega) \\
&= \frac{1}{\epsilon(I)} \Lambda^{n}(A)(e_{I} \wedge e_{I^{c}}) \\
&= \frac{1}{\epsilon(I)} \left(\Lambda^{k}(A)(e_{I})\right) \wedge \left(\Lambda^{n-k}(A) e_{I^{c}}\right) \\
\end{align*}$$

By the previous theorem we know that $\Lambda^{k}(A)$ acting on the basis vectors $e_{I}$ amounts to applying $C_{k}(A)$ which then gives us
$$\det(A) \omega = \frac{1}{\epsilon(I)}\sum\limits_{|J| = k, |L| = n-k} C_{k}(A)_{J, I} C_{n-k}(A)_{L, I^{c}} e_{J} \wedge e_{L}$$
since we know that $e_{J} \wedge e_{L}$ is zero except for when $L = J^{c}$ we can simplify the sum
$$\det(A) \omega = \frac{1}{\epsilon(I)} \sum\limits_{|J| = k} \det(A_{J,I}) \det(A_{J^{c}, I^{c}}) e_{J} \wedge e_{J^{c}}$$
using the definition of $\epsilon(J)$ we get our answer.
`\end{proof}`

This already is super useful to see that most of our theory from linear spaces transfers nicely to Modules in the exterior algebra. Let us now quickly consider some additional properties of compund matrices:

> [!proposition|*] Properties of Compunt Matrices
> If $A \in R^{n \times m}, k \leq \min \set{n, m}$, then 
> 1. $C_{k}(A)^{T} = C_{k}(A^{T})$

Now let us get into a nice corollary of all this, namely Majorization Lemmas

> [!corollary|*] Horn's Log-Majorization
> For $\K \in \set{\R, \C}$ and $A \in \K^{n \times m}, B \in \K^{m \times l}$ we denote the singular values ordered decreasingly by $s_{i}(A)$ and $s_{i}(B)$. Then
> $$\forall k \leq \min \set{n, l}: \prod_{i=1}^{k} s_{i}(AB) \leq \prod_{i=1}^{k} s_{i}(A) s_{i}(B)$$

`\begin{proof}`
The proof is given by noting that the products of singular values are in the compund matrices. Using the operator Norm of a matrix which gives the product of singular values.

$$\prod_{i=1}^{k} s_{i}(AB) = \norm{C_{k}(AB)} = \norm{C_{k}(A) C_{k}(B)} \leq \norm{C_{k}(A)} \norm{C_{k}(B)} = \prod_{i,j = 1}^{k} s_{i}(A) s_{j}(B)$$
`\end{proof}`

> [!corollary|*] Weyl's Log Majoraization
> Let $(\lambda_{i}(A))_{i=1}^{n}$ and $(s_{i}(A))_{i = 1}^{n}$ be eigenvalues and singular vlaues of $A \in \C^{n \times n}$. Then $\forall k \in [n]$:
> $$\prod_{i =1}^{k} |\lambda_{i}(A)| \leq \prod_{i=1}^{k} s_{i}(A)$$

`\begin{proof}`
We use that the spectral radius is not larger than the largest singular value, meaning:
$$\max_{I \subset [n], |I| = k} | \prod_{i \in I} \lambda_{i}(A)| = \max_{\alpha}|\lambda_{\alpha}(\Lambda^{k}A)| \leq s_{1}(\Lambda^{k}A) = \prod_{i = 1}^{k} s_{i}(A)$$
`\end{proof}`


> [!theorem|*] Useful Trick to show Equality of Polynomials
> If $g, f \in \Z[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]$ is such that $g(z) = f(z)$ holds for all $z \in U \subset \C^{n}$ then
> $$f(r) = g(r)$$
> for all $r \in R^{n}$ in any commutative unital ring $R$

`\begin{proof}`
The idea is to use the equality of $U$ to all of $\C^{n}$ by analyticity. This then implies that the identity also holds over $\Z^{n} \subset \C^{n}$. The final step is evaluating each of these polynomials $r \in R^{n}$ is a ring homomorphism 
$$\Z[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}] \to R$$
`\end{proof}`

A nice example where we ease our load a lot by using this trick.

> [!theorem|*]
> For $A \in R^{n \times n}$ the following holds
> $$\det(\mathbb{1} + tA) = \sum\limits_{k=0}^{n} t^{k} \tr[\Lambda^{k}A]$$

`\begin{proof}`
The simplest way to prove this is to note that this is a polynomial identity with integer coefficients in variables $t, A_{i,j}$ for $i, j \in [n]$. Now solving this over $\C$ gives us the freedom that for the eigenvalues $\lambda_{i}$ of $A$ we have
$$\det(\mathbb{1} + t A) = \prod_{i = 1}^{n} (1 + t \lambda_{i}) = 1 + \sum\limits_{k = 1}^{n} t^{k} \sum\limits_{i_{1} < \ldots < i_{k}} \prod _{r = 1}^{k} \lambda_{i_{r}}$$
By the previous threorem we know that $\prod _{r = 1}^{k} \lambda_{i_{r}}$ is simply the product of Eigenvalues of $\Lambda^{k}A$ and thus we know that our identity holds over $\C$ and thus also on any thing $R$
`\end{proof}`

### Hodge Duality

Now for the following we will consider Hodge duality which is motivated by the observation that if $\dim_{R}(M) = d \in \N$, then $\dim_{R}(\Lambda^{k}(M)) =\binom{d}{k} = \binom{d}{d-k} = \dim_{R}(\Lambda^{k}(M))$
> [!lemma|*]
> 1. **Wedge Duality:** The following map is an isomorphism of $R$-Modules $$\phi: \Lambda^{d-k}(M) \to \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(\Lambda^{k}M, \Lambda^{d}M), \quad \beta \mapsto \left(\alpha \mapsto \alpha \wedge \beta\right)$$
> 2. **Metric Duality:** Fix bilinear form $g: M \times M \to R$ representing a perfect pairing, meaning $M \to M^{*}, \omega \mapsto \left(v \mapsto g(v, \omega)\right)$ is an isomorphism, then $g_{k}(v_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge v_{k}, w_{1} \wedge \ldots \wedge w_{k}) := \det(g(v_{i}, w_{j})_{i,j})$ induces an isomorphism$$\psi: \Lambda^{k}M \to (\Lambda^{k}M)^{*}$$

`\begin{proof}`
The first duality can be proven by explicitly writing everything in a given basis. For the proof of $2$ use that if $$\phi: M \to M^{*}$$
is an isomorphism, then
$$\Lambda^{k} \phi: \Lambda^{k}M \to \Lambda^{k} (M^{*})$$
is as well and $\Lambda^{k}(M^{*}) \cong \Lambda^{k}(M)^{*}$
`\end{proof}`

Now using this Lemma we can compose these dualities and find that

where $\Lambda^{d}M \cong R$ by fixing an element $\mu \in \Lambda^{d} M$ and then stating that $\Lambda^{d}M = R \cdot \mu$
