A central avenue to go down when considering [[Ring Modules]] is to consider the canonical object of the Tensor Product as a product of Modules. This is mainly based on Multilinearity and has many application in physics and [[5.1 The Curvature Tensor MA3205|Differential Geometry and the Curvature Tensor]].

For this we note that since we will be discussing maps from $M \times N$ where both are $R$-Modules we need to be aware of the definition of [[Multilinear Maps of Modules]]

# General Construction

There are many ways to introduce the tensor product. We will try some on for size, but it can be useful to keep one specific example in mind to know what we want to go for.

Consider $\K[x] \otimes K[y]$, then this should somehow be isomorphic to $\K[x, y]$. Finding what structure gives us this property is what we want.
$\K[x] \times \K[y] \to \K[x] \otimes_{K} \K[y] \cong \K[x, y]$

There are four paths to the tensor product:

1. Construction from generators. This is hinted at in our pathological example since we think of $x, y$ being the generators of the polynomial Ring.
2. Construction as a quotient of a free module. This is can be thought of as imposing relations. Which we need since we can see in our example that $\frac{x}{2} \cdot 2y = x y$ which means these elements need to be the same in our tensor product.
3. Universal property of the Tensor Product which defines the Product via its abstract implication using Categories
4. Dual Spaces and Multilinear forms.

We will move from the abstract universal property and from there consider paths one and two and in the end talking about four.

## Path 3: Universal Property of the Tensor Product

We begin with a definition that could be empty. We simple impose some property on an object and later only prove that this object must exist.

> [!definition|\*] Tensor Product from Universal Property
> Given two $R$-Modules $M, N$, their tensor product over $R$ is an $R$-Module $M \otimes_{R} N$ with a bilinear map $\tau: M \times N \to M \otimes_{R} N$ with the following property
>
> For every $R$-Module $K$ and every $R$-bilinear map $\phi: M \times N \to K$ there exists a unique $R$-linear map $\Phi: M \otimes_{R} N \to K$ such that
> $\Phi \circ \tau = \phi$
> We also write $x \otimes y := \tau(x, y)$

This definition tells us that the tensor product is the unique way of making bilinear maps linear.

We now need to fill this concept with something, by showing existence and uniqueness.

> [!theorem|\*] Uniqueness of $\otimes$
> Given two tensor products, $\tau_{1}: M \times N \to T_{1}, \tau_{2}: M \times N \to T_{2}$ then there is a unique isomorphism $j: T_{1} \to T_{2}$ such that $j \circ \tau_{1} = \tau_{2}$

`\begin{proof}`
Consider the commutative diagram. Then every branch can be read like the universal property of the tensor product. Meaning there exists a unique $R$-linear maps
$i: T_{1} \to T_{2}$
such that $i \circ \tau_{1} = \tau_{2}$ and
$j: T_{2} \to T_{1}$
such that $j \circ \tau_{2} = \tau_{1}$

Then we can consider the $R$-linear map $j \circ i: T_{1} \to T_{1}$ and again consider the universal property of the tensor product and note that by it the map $j \circ i$ is unique and has
$j \circ i \circ t_{1} = t_{1}$
Since the map is unique but also $\mathrm{id}: T_{1} \to T_{1}$ does the job we know that
$j \circ i = \mathrm{id}$
`\end{proof}`

Now that we know that if a tensor product exists that it must be unique up to isomorphism we need to find existence

> [!theorem|\*] Existence of $\otimes$
> Under the given Assumtion a tensor product, meaning a module $M \otimes_{R} N$ together with a map
> $\tau: M \times N \to M \otimes_{R} N$
> exists.

`\begin{proof}`
We define the canonical embedding of a product into its free module by
$\iota: M \times N \to F(M \times N ), \quad (v, w) \mapsto \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} r_{i}(v_{i}, w_{i})$
for $n \in \N, r_{i} \in R, v_{i} \in M, w_{i} \in N$.
Let $S \subset F(M \times N)$ be the submodule generated by the relations

- $(av+bu, x) - a(v, x) - b (u, x)$
- $(v, ax + b y) - a(v, x) - b(v, y)$

We then define a Module
$M \otimes_{R} N := \faktor{F(M \times N)}{S}$
where we intepret
$v \otimes w := (v, w) + S \in M \otimes_{R} N$
We can then define a map
$\tau: M \times N \to M \otimes_{R} N, \quad (v, w) \mapsto v \otimes w$
of which we would like to show bilinearity. Consider
$(a v + bu) \otimes x = (av + bu, x) + S = a(v, x) + b(u, x) + S = a(v \otimes x) + b(u \otimes x)$
which we can simiarly show bilinearity in every other slot.

This means we now have defined a module $M \otimes_{R} N$ togehter with a bilinear map $\tau$. We now only need to show the universal property of the tensor product. To this end consider some bilinear map $\phi: M \times N \to K$ with $K$ some $R$-Module. We now by the universal property of the Free module that there exists some map $\Phi: F(M \times N) \to K$.

We can see that on $S$, $\Phi$ is trivial since
$\Phi((av+bu, x) - a(v, x) - b (u, x)) = \Phi((av+bu, x)) - a \Phi((a, x)) - b \Phi((u, x))$
since we know that $\Phi$ acts like $\phi$ on $M \times N$ and thus it is bilinear and we get
$\Phi((av+bu, x) - a(v, x) - b (u, x)) = 0$
This means that we can move this $\Phi$ to a map
$\Phi: \faktor{F(M \times N)}{S} \to K$
with the action that
$\Phi(v \otimes x) = \Phi(v, x) = \phi(v, x)$
`\end{proof}`

> [!proposition|\*] Generating Sets of Tensor Products
> Let $M, N$ be $R$-Modules and $A \subset M, B \subset N$ generating sets of the respective module. Then the set
> $C = \set{a \otimes b \midline a \in A, B \in B}$
> is a generating set of $M \otimes_{R} N$. If $A$ and $B$ are bases then $C$ is also a basis of $M \otimes_{R} N$
> and $\dim_{R}(M \otimes_{R} N) = \dim_{R}(M) \cdot \dim_{R}(N)$

Sometimes we call the set $C$ in this statement the “elementary Tensors”. Its important to note that not all tensors are elementary.

`\begin{proof}`
Consider some $p \in M \otimes_{R} N$ with the following decomposition
$p = \sum\limits_{i} r_{i} u_{i} \otimes v_{i} = \sum\limits_{i} r_{i} \left(\sum\limits_{j} \alpha_{j} a_{j}\right) \otimes \left(\sum\limits_{k} \beta_{k} b_{k}\right) = \cdots = \sum\limits_{ijk} (\ldots) a_{j} \otimes b_{k}$
Now suppose that $A, B$ are bases. Now for all $a \in A$ define $\phi_{a} \in \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, R)$ such that

$$$\forall a' \in A: \quad \phi_{a}(a') = \begin{cases}
1 \quad& a' = a \\ 0 
\end{cases}$$
Then we also define
$$\phi_{ab}(u, v) := \phi_{a}(v) \phi_{b}(v)$$ which is bilinear and has a unique extension to $\Phi_{ab}: M \otimes_{R} N \to R$. With
$$0 = \phi_{a_{k} b_{l}}\left(\sum\limits_{ij} \lambda_{ij} a_{i} \otimes b_{j}\right) = \sum\limits_{ij} \lambda_{ij} \phi_{a_{k}}(a_{i}) \phi_{b_{l}}(b_{j}) = \lambda_{kl}$$
Linearity of the Basis $C$ is then given by computation of elementary tensors
`\end{proof}`

> [!example] Kronecker Product as Tensor Products
> Consider $R^{n_{1} \times m_{1}} \otimes_R {R}^{n_{2} \times m_{2}} \cong R^{n_{1} n_{2} \times m_{1} m_{2}}$ and the set $A = \set{e_{kl} \in R^{n_{1} \times m_{1}}}$ where $e_{kl}$ is the matrix with one $1$ at the coordinate $(k,l)$
> Then we can multiply two matrices by blocks
> $$C \otimes D = \begin{pmatrix}C_{11} D & C_{12} D & \cdots \\
C_{21}D & C_{22} D \\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}$$
For two matrices is called the Kronecker product.

Some more examples are given by the following interesting construction:

> [!example|*] Integers Modulo N
> Consider by $\Z_{n} = \faktor{\Z}{n \Z}$ then we can consider the tensor product
> $$\Z_{n} \otimes_{\Z} \Z_{m}$$
> We can actually prove two statements about this
> 1. The tensor product only contains elementary tensors
> 2. $\Z_{n} \otimes_{\Z} \Z_{m} \cong 0$ if $\mathrm{gcd}(m,n) = 1$
>
>`\begin{proof}`
>For the first statement consider that we can write
>$$x \otimes y = xy (1 \otimes 1)$$
>which means that the tensor $1 \otimes 1$ is a basis of the product and thus any element is an elementary tensor.
>
>For the second statement consider that
>$$\begin{align*}
>n (x \otimes y) &= (nx) \otimes y = 0 \otimes y = 0 \\
>m(x \otimes y) &= x \otimes (my) = x \otimes y = 0
>\end{align*}$$
>By Bezout we know that we can find $a, b \in \Z$ such that $an + bm = \mathrm{gcd}(m,n)$ and thus
>$$x \otimes y = \mathrm{gcd}(n, m) (x\otimes y) = (an + bm)(x \otimes y) = a n(x \otimes y) + b m (x \otimes y) = a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 0 = 0$$
>`\end{proof}`


## Tensor Products of Function Spaces

A special case that we are interested in when considering our motivation for tensor products, is tensor products of function space. Since here we can also consider how the function changes and the inputs behave it is important to consider how tensor functions transform.
> [!proposition|*]
> A tensor product of any two $\K$-module $M = \K^{X}$ and $N = \K^{Y}$ where $X, Y$ are sets and thus $M, N$ function spaces, is given a bilinear map
> $$\phi: M \times N \to K^{X \times Y}, \tau(g, h)(x, y) = g(x) h(y)$$
> and the tensor product is given explicitly as
> $$M \otimes_{\K} N = \set{f \in \K^{X \times Y} \midline f(x, y) = \sum\limits_{i =1}^{n} g_{i}(x) h_{i}(y), n \in \N, g_{i} \in M, h_{i} \in N}$$

`\begin{proof}`
Let us define 
$$K := \set{f \in \K^{X \times Y} \midline f(x, y) = \sum\limits_{i =1}^{n} g_{i}(x) h_{i}(y), n \in \N, g_{i} \in M, h_{i} \in N}$$
and we know that $T$ is trivially a $\K$-module.

We know by the universal property of the tensor product that for $\phi: M \times N \to K$ there must exist a map
$$\Phi: M \otimes_{\K} N \to K$$
such that $\phi(f \times g)= \left((x, y) \mapsto g(x)(h(y))\right)$. As the the tensor product and $K$ have all linear combinations we know that $\Phi$ is surjective.

For injectivity consider $\mu \in M \otimes_{\K} N$, then $\mu = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} g_{i} \otimes h_{i}$ with $n$ minimal, meaning the elements $h_{i}$ all need to be linearly independent since otherwise we could reduce $n$.

Now consider the map
$$\phi_{x}: Y \to \K, \quad y \mapsto \Phi(\mu)(x, y) = \sum\limits_{i =1}^{n} g_{i}(x) h_{i}(y)$$
We know that by linear independence that
$$\Phi(\mu) = 0 \implies 0 = \phi_{x} = \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n} g_{i}(x) h_{i} \implies g_{i} = 0 \implies \mu = 0$$
and thus $\Phi$ is injective and thus an isomorphism
`\end{proof}`

> [!example|*]
> The canonical example for this is the tensor product of two polynomial rings
> $$R[x] \otimes_{R} R[y] = R[x, y]$$
> We here know that a generating set of the tensor product is given by
> $$\set{x^{k} \otimes y^{l} \midline k, l \in \N}$$
> which we can simply map via
> $$x^{k} \otimes y^{l} \mapsto x^{k}y^{l}$$
> Also note that something we can see in this example aswell is that not all tensor are elementary, consider for example
> $$x + y \neq p(x) q(y)$$


> [!example|*] Smooth Functions
> Consider the tensor product of the vector spaces of smooth function
> $$C^{\infty}(\R) \otimes C^{\infty}(\R) \subset C^{\infty}(\R \times \R)$$
> where equality never holds. This is easily given by the function
> $$f(x, y) = \sin(xy)$$
> Assume it were a finite linear combination of elementary products so
> $$f(x, y) = \sum\limits_{i} g_{i}(x) h_{i}(y)$$
> Then we could write for any $k \in \Z$
> $$\sin (kx) = \sum\limits_{i}g_{i}(x) h_{i}(k)$$
> This would tell us that the set $B = \set{x \mapsto \sin(kx)}$ is not linearly independent. However we know that
> $$\int_{\pi}^{\pi} \sin(kx) \sin(lx) = \pi \delta_{k,l}$$

## Properties of the Tensor Product

Now that we have a natural construction given by the tensor products and we think of them as either this general given space by the universal property or we think of them as the formal sum of elementary tensors we need to consider what rules we can develop for Tensor Products.

### Identity Element
> [!lemma|*] Ring as the Tensorproduct Identity
> $$M \otimes_{R} R \cong M$$

`\begin{proof}`
Consider the map
$$\phi: M \times R \to M, \quad (m, r) \mapsto rm$$
then we can see that this is a bilinear map and thus by the universal property we know there exists a linear map
$$\Phi: M \otimes_{R} R \to M, m \otimes r \mapsto r m$$

Now we define $\psi: M \to M \otimes_{R} R, \quad m \mapsto m \otimes 1$ then we can see that
$$\psi \circ \Phi(m \otimes r) = \psi(rm) = rm \otimes 1 = m \otimes r$$
and
$$\Phi \circ \psi(m) = \Phi(m \otimes 1) = m$$
`\end{proof}`


> [!example|*] Change of Scalars
> Let $\rho: R \to S$ be a ring homomorphism, then we canregard $S$ as an $R$-module with $r s = \phi(r) s$. 
> Similarly we can regard any $S$-Module $M$ as an $R$-Module via $r m = \phi(r) m$ which we call a restriction of scalars.
> 
> Conversely for every $R$-module $M$ we can construct an $S$-Module by
> $$S \otimes_{R} M$$
> by regarding $S$ and $M$ as $R$-Module. The multiplication is then given by
> $$s (t \otimes m) = st \otimes m$$
> 
> This construction allows for things like complexification where for some vector space $V$ we create
> $$V_{\C} := \C \otimes_{\R} V$$
> and then note that $\dim(V_{\C}) = \dim_{\R}(\C) \dim_{\R}(V) = 2 \dim_{\R}(V)$


> [!lemma|*]
> Consider $\Z \otimes_{\Z} \Q \cong \Q$ as a $\Q$-Mdoule


`\begin{proof}`
Consider
$$\phi: \Z \otimes_{\Z} \Q \to \Q, \quad n \otimes p \mapsto n p$$
then this is a $\Q$ homomorphism and also surjective since for any $p \in Q$ we can write
$$p = \phi(1 \otimes p)$$
it is also injective with
$$\phi^{-1}: q \mapsto 1 \otimes q$$
similarly to previous lemmata
`\end{proof}`

### Distributivity, Commutativity and Associativity



> [!proposition|*] Distributivity
> Let $N$ and $M_{\lambda}$ be $R$-Modules, then
> $$N \otimes_{R} \left(\bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda} M_{\lambda}\right) \cong \bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda} \left(N \otimes_{R} M_{\lambda}\right)$$

`\begin{proof}`
To prove this we again employ the universal property. First define a bilinear map
$$\tau:N \times \left(\bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda} M_{\lambda}\right) \to \bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda} \left(N \otimes_{R} M_{\lambda}\right), \quad\left(n, (m_{\lambda})_{\lambda \in \Lambda}\right) \mapsto (n \otimes m_{\lambda})_{\lambda \in \Lambda}$$
What we now want to show is that this mapping as the universal property of the tensor product, so consider some arbitrary $R$-Module $K$ and a bilinear map
$$\phi: N \times \left(\bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda} M_{\lambda}\right) \to K$$
also define $\psi_{\lambda}: N \times M_{\lambda} \to K$ with $\psi_{\lambda}(n, m_{\lambda}) = \phi(n, (0, \ldots, m_{\lambda}, 0 \ldots))$.

Lastly we can also define
$$\Psi: \bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda} N \otimes_{R} M_{\lambda} \to K, \quad (x_{\lambda}) \mapsto \sum\limits_{\lambda} \psi_{\lambda}(x_{\lambda})$$
Then the commutative Diagram holds
$$ \begin{CD} N \times \left(\bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda} M_{\lambda}\right)  @>\tau>> \bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda} \left(N \otimes_{R} M_{\lambda}\right)\\ @V\phi VV @VV\Psi V \\  K @> id>> K\end{CD}$$

This means that if $\Psi$ were unique we would know that $\bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda} \left(N \otimes_{R} M_{\lambda}\right)$ has the property that the tensor product of $N \times \left(\bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda} M_{\lambda}\right)$ has and thus is isomorphic to it.

Uniqueness still needs to be shown
`\end{proof}`

> $$\Q \otimes_{\Z} \Z_{q_{i}} = 0$$

> [!proposition|*] Commutativity
> Consider two $R$-Modules $M, N$ then 
> $$M \otimes_{R} N \cong N \otimes_{R} M$$
> with explicit map 
> $$m \otimes n \mapsto n \otimes m$$
> extended by linearity


In order to show associativity we will define the tensor products for multiple modules
> [!definition|*]
> Let $M_{1}, \ldots, M_{n}$ be $R$-Modules. Their tensor product is an $R$-Module
> $M_{1} \otimes_{R} \ldots \otimes_{R} M_{n}$ togehter with an $n$-multiplinear map $\tau: M_{1} \times \ldots \times M_{n} \to M_{1} \otimes_{R} \ldots \otimes_{R} M_{n}$ with the property that
> For any multiplinear map $\phi: M_{1} \times \ldots \times M_{n} \to Q$ there exists a map $\Phi: M_{1} \otimes_{R} \ldots \otimes_{R} M_{n} \to Q$ such that
> $$\phi = \Phi \circ \tau$$
> The generators of this tensor product are given by $m_{1} \otimes m_{2} \otimes \ldots \otimes m_{n}$

> [!corollary|*] Associativity
> Let $M_{1}, M_{2}, M_{3}$ be $R$-Modules. Then
> $$(M_{1} \otimes_{R} M_{2}) \otimes M_{3} \cong M_{1} \otimes_{R} M_{2} \otimes M_{3} \cong M_{1} \otimes_{R} (M_{2} \otimes M_{3})$$

`\begin{proof}`
Use the uniqueness of the tensor product.
`\end{proof}`

## Tensors as Compact Representation of Multilinear Maps
A useful application for tensors is that the space of tensors naturally has as much information as a linear map. In this way we can think of $$A^{*} \otimes B \in \phi \otimes b$$
as defining a map on $A$ by simply saying that evaluating $\phi$ at a point of $A$ gives us a Ring element and we can use this to scale $b$ giving us a vector. Thus we get
$$f: A \to B, f(a) = \phi(a) b$$
Informationally speaking this is useful, as the tensors seem to encode everything in a compact representation. We will work towards this reslult in the [[Tensor Products as Linear Maps]] chapter.
