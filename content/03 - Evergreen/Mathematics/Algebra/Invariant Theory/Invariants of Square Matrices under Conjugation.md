Consider the Invariants of a square matrix. Let $V = \K^{n \times n}$ and consider $G = \mathrm{GL}_{n}(K)$ which acts on $V$ by matrix conjugation
$\sigma(A) = \sigma A \sigma^{-1}$
we also call this action an action on similarity classes. From Linear Algebra we know that operations like the trace and determinant are invariants of this setup. Both of these come from the characteristic polynomial which seems to be the best invariant for our purpose. We will not consider the actual polynomial though because of it formally not fitting to our definitions but we can take the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial and consider those.

For some $A \in \K^{n \times n}$ set
$F_{A}(y) = \det(y I_{n} + A) = y^{n} + a_{1} y^{n-1} + \ldots + a_{n-1}y + a_{n}$
where we know that $a_{i} \in K[V]^{G}$ and also that $a_{1} = \tr A$ and $a_{n}  = \det A$

We want to apply the theorem, thus we need to find a good candidate for $L$. We can consider the second condition as wanting $L$ to be almost all matrices and we know that most matrices are diagonalizable. We thus consider
$L = \set{\text{diagonal matrices}} \subset V$
We can now ask if $G(L)$ is dense? This is true since we can consider the formal polynmial
$h(A) = \text{descrim}(F_{A}(y))$
From linear algebra we know that $h(A) \neq 0 \implies A \in G(L)$.
Now let $f \in \mathcal{I}(G(L))$ then for all $A \in V$ we have that $h(A) = 0$ or $f(A) = 0$ giving us that $h \cdot f = 0$ but since $h = 0$ as we can construct matrices with a non-zero descriminant of $F_{A}$ meaning that $f = 0$.
This gives us that $\mathcal{I}(G(L)) = \set{0}$ giving us that
$\overline{G(L)} = V$
the zariski closure of our orbit is all of $V$ which gives us our second condition.

For the first condition we consider the invariants we have though of $\set{a_{i}}$ and consider their restriction to $L$. For this consider for some $A = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}) \in L$ the polynomial
$F_{A} (y) = \det(\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1} + y, \ldots \lambda_{n} + y)) = \prod_{i = 1}^{n} (y + \lambda_{i}) = y^{n} + s_{1}(\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}) y^{n-1} + \ldots + s_{n}(\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n})$
where $s_{i}$ are the elementary symmetric polynomials from before. This gives the idea of using as our group $H$ something like the symmetric group.

We know that $H = S_{n}$ acts on $L$ by permuting the diagonal entries, which means w can also express $H$ as the set of permutation matrices
$H = \set{\text{permutation matrices}} \subset G$
Since permutation matrices permute the diagonal entries we know that $H \leq \mathcal{N}_{G}(L)$. We can thus express via [[Invariants of the Symmteric Group]]
$K[L]^{H} = K[s_{1}, \ldots, s_{n}] = K[\at{a_{1}}{L}, \ldots, \at{a_{n}}{L}]$
Applying the [[Normal-Form Principle]] gives us that
$K[V]^{G} = K[a_{1}, \ldots, a_{n}]$
meaning that any invariant we can consider on the above situation are generated by the coeffiecients of the characteristic polynomial.

In the above setup we can also ask a question about orbit seperation. It turns out that the reason why the Jordan Canonical Form works so well is because the Jordan Matrices correspond to the Orbits. When asking for orbit seperation we can find an example easily where the determinant doesnt seperate them, like

$$$\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix},  \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0 \
0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$$

The problem here is again something to do with closed orbits.
> [!proposition|2.3]
> Let $A \in \K^{n \times m}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_{1}, \lambda_{n}$ (not necessarily destinct) then
> - $G(A)$ is closed if and only if $A$ is diagonalizable
> - For $A$ non-diagonalizable we have $\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}) \in \overline{G(A)}$

`\begin{proof}`
For the first statement let us assume that $A$ is diagonalizable. Then we know that
$$G(A) = \set{\text{all diagonalizable matrices with eigenvalues } \lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}}$$
Form the minimal polynomial $G(x) = \prod_{\lambda \in \set{\lambda_{1} \ldots, \lambda_{n}}} (x - \lambda)$ then from lienar algebra we know that
$$B \in G(A) \iff F_{A}(y) = F_{B}(y)$$
and $G(B) = 0$ set of polynomial equations so $G(A)$ is closed.


The implication $\implies$ follows from the second statment

To prove the second we consider a Jordan canonical form of $A$
$$A = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}) + \mathrm{offdiag}(\delta_{1} \ldots \delta_{n-1})$$ with $\delta_{i} \in \set{0, 1}$.

For some $c \in \K$ form 
$$A_{c} =\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}) + c \cdot\mathrm{offdiag}(\delta_{1} \ldots \delta_{n-1})$$
effectively replacing $1$ in the off diagonal with a $c$. If we assume $c \neq 0$ then via linear algebra we can see that $A_{c}$ has the same jordan canonical form as $A$. This means that
$$A_{c} \in G(A)$$
If we were in the euclidean topology now we could simply consider the limit as $c$ goes to 0, since we then have a sequentially closed set which is closed. As we are in a more complex topology we need some other trick

Let $f \in \mathcal{I}(G(A))$ and set $g(c) := f(A_{c})$ for any $c \in \K$. Then $g \in \K[c]$ and we know that for $c \neq 0$ we get
$$g(c) = f(A_{c}) = 0 \implies g = 0$$
This now gives us that
$$0 = g(0) = f(\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}))$$
giving us that $\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}) \in \overline{G(A)}$
`\end{proof}`

> [!corollary|2.3]
> Given two matrices $A, B \in \K^{n \times n}$  such that for all $f \in K[V]^{G}$ we have
> $$f(A) = f(B)$$
> meaning they aren’t separated by invariants. Then they have the same eigenvalues (counting algebraic multiplicities) $\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}$ then the previous proposition tells us that
> $$\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}) \in \overline{G(A)} \cap \overline{G(B)} \implies \overline{G(A)} \cap \overline{G(B)} \neq \emptyset$$
> Conversely let $c \in \overline{G(A)} \cap \overline{G(B)}$ then we get that
> $$\forall f \in K[V]^{G}: f(A) = f(c) = f(B)$$

$$$
