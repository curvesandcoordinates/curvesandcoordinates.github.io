> [!definition|10.1] Hilbert Series
> Let $R$ be a noetherian graded $\K$-Algebra and $M$ a graded noetherian $R$-Module (for example $M = R$). Then
> $H(M, t) = \sum\limits_{d \in \Z} \dim_{\K}(M_{d}) t^{d} \in \Z[[t]][t^{-1}]$
> is a formal laurant series called the **hilbert series**

Keep in mind that the assumption of noetherianness gives us finiteness of every coefficient. We also immediately know that the minimal degree is bounded below, since by definition a degree needs to begin somewhere, thus this sum only has finitely many negative degree components.

We should think of this as a way to describe the relationship between how quickly the dimension grows in each degree. This is useful to understnad possibly the number of primary invariants of something similar.

> [!proposition|10.2] Hilbert Series of Polynomial Ring
> Let $R = \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{l}]$ with $f_{i}$ homogeneous of degree $d_{i}$ and algebraically independent, then
> $H(R, t) = \frac{1}{(1 - t^{d_{1}})\cdot \ldots \cdot (1 - t^{d_{l}})}$

`\begin{proof}`
We prove this by induction on $l$. The base case is trivial since $l = 0$ gives us that $R = \K$ and thus $H(R, t) = 1$ trivially.

Now assume that $l > 0$ and $R = \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{l}]$ then consider the map
$\phi: R \to \K[f_{2}, \ldots, f_{l}], \quad F(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{l}) \mapsto F(0, f_{2}, \ldots, f_{l})$
with $F \in \K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{l}]$. Due to the $f_{i}$’s being algebraically independent we know that $F$ must be well defined and unique. It is also trivially surjective and the kernel is given by
$\set{f_{1} \cdot g \midline g \in \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{l}]} = f_{1} R$
We can thus consider the isomorphism
$\K[f_{2}, \ldots, f_{l}] \cong \faktor{\K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{l}]}{f_{1} \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{l}]} \implies \dim_{\K}(\K[f_{2}, \ldots, f_{l}]_{d}) = \dim_{\K}(\K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{l}]_{d}) - \dim_{\K}( f_{1} \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{l}]_{d})$
this then gives us a nice simplification

$$$\begin{align*}
H(\K[f_{2}, \ldots, f_{l}], t) = H(R, t) - H(\ker \phi, t) = H(R, t) - t^{d_{1}} H(R, t) = H(R, t) (1 - t^{d_{1}}) 
\end{align*}$$
which then simplifies 
$$H(R, t) = \frac{H(\K[f_{2}, \ldots, f_{l}], t)}{1 - t^{d_{1}}}$$
`\end{proof}`

Now let $G$ be a finite group and $V$ a $G$-Module, $n = \dim(V)$.

> [!corollary|10.3]
> Let $\K[V]^{G}$ be Cohen Macaulay and let $f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}$ be primary invariants of degrees $d_{1}, \ldots, d_{n}$ and let $g_{1}, \ldots, g_{m}$ be minimal secondary invariants of degrees $e_{1}, \ldots, e_{m}$. Then:
> $$H(\K[V]^{G}, t) = \frac{{t^{e_{1}} + \ldots + t^{e_{m}}}}{(1 - t^{d_{1}}) \cdot \ldots \cdot (1 - t^{d_{n}})}$$

`\begin{proof}`
This proof is mostly book-keeping and calculation but we will do it anyway. By the definition of primary and secondary invariants we know that
$$\K[V]^{G} = \bigoplus_{i = 1}^{m} g_{i} \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}]$$
this means that
$$\dim_{\K}(\K[V]^{G}_{d}) = \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{m} \dim_{\K}(g_{i} \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}]_{d- e_{i}}) = \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{m} \dim_{\K}(\K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}]_{d- e_{i}})$$
Plugging this into the Hilbert series gives us
$$\begin{align*}
H(\K[V]^{G}, t) &= \sum\limits_{d \in \Z} \dim_{\K}(\K[V]^{G}_{d}) t^{d} \\
&= \sum\limits_{d \in \Z} \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{m} \dim_{\K}(\K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}]_{d- e_{i}}) t^{d-e_{i}} t^{e_{i}} \\
&= \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{m}t^{e_{i}} \sum\limits_{d \in \Z} \dim_{\K}(\K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}]_{d- e_{i}}) t^{d-e_{i}} \\
&= H(\K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}], t) \cdot \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{m} t^{e_{i}}
\end{align*}$$
which is exactly the statement.
`\end{proof}`

> [!remark|*] Dropping the Cohen Macaulay Assumption
> If we drop the Cohen Macaulay assumption on $\K[V]^{G}$ then also
> $$\left(\prod_{i} (1 - t^{d_{i}})\right) H(\K[V]^{G}, t) \in \Z[t]$$


We can now also consider the special situation in which $h_{1}, \ldots, h_{l}$ are minimal secondary “invariants” for the trivial group with respect to $f_{i} \in \K[V]^{G}$ then since $\K[V]$ is Cohen Macaulay with $c_{i} = \deg(h_{i})$ we can calculate that
$$\frac{{t^{c_{1}} + \ldots + t^{c_{l}}}}{(1 - t^{d_{1}}) \cdot \ldots \cdot (1 - t^{d_{n}})} = H(\K[V]^{G}, t) = H(\K[V], t) = \frac{1}{(1 - t)^{n}}$$
Simplifying this we get
$$\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l} t^{c_{i}} = \prod_{i = 1}^{n} \frac{1 - t^{d_{i}}}{1 - t} = \prod_{i = 1}^{n} (1 + t + t^{2} + \ldots + t^{d_{i}-1})$$
plugging in $t = 1$ gives us
$$l= \prod_{i = 1}^{n} d_{i}$$

meaning that in this special case we know exactly what the number of secondary invariants is depending on the primary invariant degrees chosen. > [!theorem|10.5] Molien’s Formula
>$$H(\K[V]^{G}, t) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{\sigma \in G} \frac{1}{\det(1 - t \sigma)}$$


> [!lemma|10.6]
> $$\dim(V^{G}) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{\sigma \in G} \mathrm{tr_{V}}(\sigma)$$

`\begin{proof}`

`\end{proof}`

> [!lemma|10.7]
> For $\sigma \in G$ we have
> $$\sum\limits_{d = 0}^{\infty} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[V]_{d}}(\sigma) t^{d} = \frac{1}{\det(1 - t \sigma^{-1})}$$
> where the trace is of $\sigma$ acting on $\K[V]_{d}$

`\begin{proof}`
Neither side changes when extending $\K$. We may assume $\K = \overline{\K}$. Substituting $\sigma$ into $X^{|G|} - 1$ which is square-free yields $0$.

This implies that the minimal polynomial of $\sigma$ is squarefree and also thus that $\sigma$ is diagonalizable. Choose a basis $B$ of $V$ such that 
$$D_{B}(\sigma) = \mathrm{diag}(a_{1}, \ldots, a_{n})$$
Now to show is only the actual formula in this case, meaning we want to show
$$\sum\limits_{d = 0}^{\infty} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[V]_{d}}(\sigma) t^{d} = \prod_{i = 1}^{n} \frac{1}{1 - a_{i}^{-1} t}$$
We know that $\sigma$ acts on $\K[V] = <x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}>_{\K}$ by the matrix $\mathrm{diag}(a_{1}^{-1}, \ldots , a_{n}^{-1})$. We now do induction on $n$.

For $n = 0$ we know that $\K[V] = \K$ and thus trivially $H(\K[V]^{G}, t) = 1$. Now assume that $n > 0$ we then know that
$$\K[V]_{d} = \K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]_{d} = \bigoplus_{i = 0}^{d} \K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n-1}] x_{n}^{d-i}$$
which gives us that
$$\mathrm{tr}_{\K[V]_{d}}(\sigma) = \sum\limits_{i = 0}^{d} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n-1}]_{i}} a_{n}^{i-d}$$
plugging this in gives us that

$$\begin{align*}
\sum\limits_{d = 0}^{\infty} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[V]_{d}}(\sigma) t^{d} &= \sum\limits_{d = 0}^{\infty} \sum\limits_{i = 0}^{d} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n-1}]_{i}} a_{n}^{i-d} t^{d} \\
&= \sum\limits_{i = 0}^{\infty} \sum_{d = i}^{\infty} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n-1}]_{i}} t^{i} a_{n}^{i-d} t^{d-i}
\end{align*}$$
we can ow change the summation parameter by replacing $d$ with $d - i$ which gives us.

$$\begin{align*}
\sum\limits_{d = 0}^{\infty} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[V]_{d}}(\sigma) t^{d} &= \sum\limits_{i = 0}^{\infty} \sum_{d = 0}^{\infty} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n-1}]_{i}} t^{i} a_{n}^{-d} t^{d} \\
&= \left( \sum\limits_{i = 0}^{\infty} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n-1}]_{i}} t^{i} \right) \left( \sum_{d = 0}^{\infty}  a_{n}^{-d} t^{d} \right)
\end{align*}$$
via the induction hypothesis we get that
$$\begin{align*}
\sum\limits_{d = 0}^{\infty} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[V]_{d}}(\sigma) t^{d} &= \left(\prod_{i = 1}^{n-1} \frac{1}{1 - t a_{i}^{-1}}\right) \left( \sum_{d = 0}^{\infty}  a_{n}^{-d} t^{d} \right) \\
&= \left(\prod_{i = 1}^{n-1} \frac{1}{1 - t a_{i}^{-1}}\right) \frac{1}{1 - a_{n}^{-1} t} \\
&= \prod_{i = 1}^{n} \frac{1}{1 - t a_{i}^{-1}}
\end{align*}$$
Where the sum is algebraically equal by being a deometric product (ignoring analytic convergence since that is not what we care about).
`\end{proof}`


using what we have now we can prove moliens formula

`\begin{proof}`
$$H(\K[V]^{G}, t) = \sum\limits_{d = 0}^{\infty} \dim(\K[V]_{d}^{G}) t^{d}$$
by our first lemma we get that
$$H(\K[V]^{G}, t) = \sum\limits_{d = 0}^{\infty} \dim(\K[V]_{d}^{G}) t^{d} = \sum\limits_{d = 0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{\sigma \in G} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[V]_{d}}(\sigma) t^{d}$$
and by out last lemma while regarding that a sum over all the inverses is the same as a sum over all group elements
$$H(\K[V]^{G}, t) = \sum\limits_{d = 0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{\sigma \in G} \mathrm{tr}_{\K[V]_{d}}(\sigma) t^{d} = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{\sigma \in G} \frac{1}{\det(1 - t \sigma)}$$
`\end{proof}`

> [!remark|*] The Formula actually depends on conjugacy classes
> First we consider a useful observation:
> $$\det(1 - t \sigma) = t^{n} \det(t^{-1} I_{n} - \sigma) = t^{n} \chi_{\sigma}(t^{-1})$$
> meaning Moliens formula isa slightly different form to the characteristic polynomial of our representation. Since the characterisitic polynomial is invariance under similarity we know that thus we know
> $$\sigma, \tau \text{ similar} \iff \det(1 - t\sigma) = \det (1 - t \tau)$$
> and thus this measure is invariant under the conjugacy clas of  $\sigma$.
> This simplifies computation since we can note that the sum simplifies.

> [!remark|*] Symonds Bound in the Modular Case
> From the theorem we get that
> $$\deg_{t}\left(H(\K[V]^{G}, t)\right) \leq - n$$
> since the degree of every element is $-n$. Using this we can state that if $d_{1}, \ldots, d_{n}$ are the degrees of primary invariants and $e_{1}, \ldots, e_{m}$ of the secondary invariants we already know that
> $$H(\K[V]^{G}, t) = \frac{t^{e_{1}} + \ldots + t^{e_{m}}}{(1 - t^{d_{1}}) \cdot \ldots \cdot (1 - t^{d_{n}})}$$
> which also tells us that $\deg(H(\K[V]^{G}, t)) = \max \set{e_{i}} - \sum\limits_{i =1}^{n} d_{i}$
> Combining the results gives us
> $$\max \set{e_{i}} \leq \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n} (d_{i} - 1)$$
> which we know in the non-modular case as Symond’s Bound

> [!remark|*] Extending to the Non Modular Case
> Let $p = \mathrm{char}(\K) \not\mid |G|$ we can obtain Molien’s Formula as follows: For each $\sigma \in G$ diagonalize the representation over a large enough Field $\overline{\K}$ then we can lift eigenvalues to roots of unity in $\C$ (this goes under the name Brauer-Lift). This then is in characteristic zero in which our usual Molien’s formula holds. 

> [!remark|*] Molien’s Formula in the Semi-Modular Case due to Hughes, Kemper
> Let $p = \mathrm{char}(\K) \mid |G|$ but $p^{2} \not\mid |G|$ (sometimes called the mildly-modular case) then there is a variant of Molien’s Formula computing $H(\K[V]^{G}, t)$ which is more complicated but computation is of similar complexity.

> [!example|*]
> Consider $G = \left< \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1  & 0\end{pmatrix}  \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1  & -1\end{pmatrix}\right> \cong S_{3}$ and $\mathrm{char}(\K) = 0$ then with $\tau = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1  & 0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1  & -1\end{pmatrix}$ we know that the only conjugacy classes are given by
> $$[\mathrm{id}] \quad [\sigma] \quad [\tau]$$
> calculating the Term from Moliens fomula for each class gives us
> $$\frac{1}{\det(1 - t \mathrm{id})} = \frac{1}{(1 - t)^{2}}, \quad \frac{1}{\det(1 - t \tau)} = \frac{1}{1 - t^{2}}, \frac{1}{\det(1 - t \sigma)} = \frac{1}{1 + t + t^{2}}$$
> then keeping in mind the amount of elements in the conjugacy classes we get a simple application of molien
> $$H(\K[V]^{G}, t) = \frac{1}{6} \left( \frac{1}{(1 - t)^{2}} + \frac{3}{1 - t^{2}} + \frac{2}{1 + t + t^{2}}\right) = \ldots = \frac{1}{(1 - t^{2}) (1 - t^{3})}$$
> which indicates (but does not prove) that the primary invariants should be two of degree $2$ and $3$ and no secondary invariant.
