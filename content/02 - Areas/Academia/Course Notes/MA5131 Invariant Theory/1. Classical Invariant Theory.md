We begin the analysis of invariants by considering for some algebraically closed field $\K$ a $\K$-vector space $V$. Note that importantly the algebraic closure is only chosen for convenience since most statements do not require it at all. Our aim is to analyze the space of polynomials on this given vector space $V$, namely
$\K[V] = \set{f: V \to \K \midline f\text{ polynomial}}$
We can then by assuming that $\dim_{\K} V = n$ we can write any polynomial very explicitly in terms of it acting on a basis and obtain an isomorphism
$\K[V] \cong \K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]$
Now we can consider some [[group action ]]from a group $G \subset \mathrm{GL}(V)$ naturally acting on $V$ by matrix multiplication. We can also extend this action to an action of the polynomials:
$G \times \K[V] \to \K[V], \quad \sigma f(v) = f(\sigma^{-1}(v))$
this is a well-defined action.

> [!remark|\*] Why we need to consider the Pullback
> It seems to me a natural question to ask why we need to precompose with $\sigma^{-1}$ in order to describe the action on $\K[V]$. This choice is natural though since in order to obtain a valid action the group multiplication has to correspond to compositional multiplication.
> Assume that $\sigma f(v) = f(\sigma(v))$ then we can easily see that for some $\sigma, \tau \in G$ we have
> $(\sigma \cdot \tau) f (v) = f((\sigma \cdot \tau)(v)) =f \circ \sigma \circ \tau(v) =\tau (\sigma(f))(v)$
> we flip the order of our group elements. This is just a consequence of precomposition. The natural way to fix this is similarly seen in the pullback of differential forms is using the inverse of our objects.
> We can check that with our correct definition of the action $\K[V]$ we get
> $(\sigma \cdot \tau)f(v) = f((\sigma \cdot \tau)^{-1}(v)) = f(\tau^{-1}(\sigma^{-1}(v))) = \sigma(\tau(f(v)))$

This setting leads us to a simplified case of defining the [[Invariant Ring]], which is explicitly also shown in [[Invariant Ring#Classical Case of Invariant Theory]].

In this classical setting the invariant is explicitly given by

> [!definition|\*] Invariant Ring
> For our current setup we define the Ring
> $K[V]^G = \set{f \in K[V] \midline \forall \sigma \in G: \sigma f = f} = \set{f \in K[V] \midline \forall \sigma \in G, v \in V: f(\sigma v) = f(v)}$

### Some Tools of the Trade

When calculating these examples explicitly simpler calculations allow for tedious exploration. We can use some of the structure of the setup to develop some simple tools. A rudimentary idea that motivates the general invariant theory is to define the [[Zariski Topology]] on the space of polynomials $\K[V]$ which essentially is the [[ring of regular functions]] of $V$. This topology allows us to talk about closedness and denseness of solutions.

> [!proposition|\*] Invariants are Constant on Closures of Orbits
> Given the situation of classical invariant theory and some map $f \in \K[V]^{G}$ then for all $v \in V$ we have that
> $\at{f}{\overline{G(v)}} = \text{const}$

`\begin{proof}`
We know already that due to invariance the polynomial $f$ that $\at{f}{G(v)} = \text{const}$. Now note that in the Zariski Topology the map $f: V \to \K$ must be continous trivially. A topological property that can easily be shown is that if a continous function is constant on some set, that is must also be constant on the closure of that set

`\end{proof}`

### What do we analyse?

What do we try to find out when calculating these invariant rings? We care about invariants morally because the geometry of the 21st century already showed that geometry can be seen as the study of invariances of space. In this respect we mainly aim to find geometric properties of some group action via algebraic means. Some important questions arise from this which have had big historical relevance:

1. Is $K[V]^G$ finitely generated? ($\approx$ Hilbert’s 13th Problem)
2. If $K[V]^G$ is finitely generated, can we compute generating invariants?
3. What does $K[V]^G$ look like?
4. Orbit Separation: If $x, y \in V$ such that $G(x) \neq G(y)$ meaning they have distinct Orbits, does there exist an invariant $f \in K[V]^G$ such that $f(x) \neq f(y)$

The Orbit Separation problem is in some way the reason for invariant theory. It geometrically states that if orbits are different can we find a global invariant that seperates two points. A canonical example to consider here is the dimension of a vector space. If two vector spaces have the same dimension we know that they must be isomorphic. This isnt always true.

## Further Topics in Classical Invariant Theory

Some further topics in classical invariant theory, assuming that $\mathrm{Char}(K) = 0$. Also no proofs will be given here

1. We can consider the action of $G = \mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)$ acting on $k^{n \times m}$ by $\sigma(A) = \sigma A$.

It turns out that in this case the invariants are the $n \times n$ minors of our matrices. For $1 \leq j_{1} < j_{2} < \ldots < j_{n} \leq m$ then we denote the determinant of a submatrix to be
$f_{j_{1}, \ldots, j_{n}} = [j_{1}, \ldots, j_{n}]$
where the notation gives us the name that these subdeterminants are called brackets. In this case the invariant ring is also generated by the brackets.

> [!theorem|\*] First Fundamental Theorem of $\mathrm{SL}_{n}$
> $K[V]^{G}$ is generated by the brackets from before

`\begin{proof}`
Uses the Theory of Polarization (whatever that is) but we dont discuss that
`\end{proof}`

> [!theorem|\*] Second Fundamental Theorem of $\mathrm{SL}_{n}$
> There is a specific type of relation between the brackets, namely the Plücker relations. So for example in the case of $m = 4, n = 2$ we have
> $[12][34] + [13][24]+[14][23] = 0$
> The Plücker relations generate the relation ideal.

The projective variety given by the plücker relations is called the grassmannian which is in a 1-to-1 bijection as follows:
$Gr(m, n) \iff \set{n \text{-dimensional subspace of } K^{m}}$

2. Consider the action of $G = O_{n}(k)$ acting on $V = K^{n \times m}$ as above. We can view this action not on matrices but on multiple vectors at once $K^{n \times m} \cong (K^{n})^{m}$ thus a matrix is technically acting on a configuration of points.

Comming up with invariants we think of the characterising property of $O_{n}$ which is that is leaves the scalar product invariant. Thus we can define the invariant to be for $1 \leq i \leq j \leq m$ then
$f_{ij}: V \to K, \quad (v_{1}, \ldots, v_{m}) \mapsto \innprod{v_{i}}{v_{j}}$
We also denote $f_{ij} = (i,j)$Now as it turns out

> [!theorem|\*] First Fundamental Theorem of $O_{n}$
> The scalar products $(i,j)$ generate the invariant ring $K[V]^{G}$

the relations here are also known and proven in the second fundamental theorem but we do not go into that.

3. Consider the Euclidean group $G = AO_{n} = \set{f: K^{n} \to K^{n} \midline f(v) = \phi(v) + v_{0} \midline \phi \in O_{n}, v_{0} \in K^{n}}$

The invariants here are not given only by scalar products but specifically by mutual distances. We can consider
$d_{ij}: (p_{1}, \ldots, p_{n}) \mapsto (p_{i} - p_{j})^{T} (p_{i} - p_{j})$
and they generate $K[V]^{G}$
