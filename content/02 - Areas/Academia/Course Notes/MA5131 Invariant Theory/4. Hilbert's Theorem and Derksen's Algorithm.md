## Prelude: Graded Algebras

> [!definition|5.1] Graded Algebras and Graded Modules
> A graded $\K$-algebra is a ring $R$ with a direct sum decomposition
> $R = \bigoplus_{d \in \N} R_{d}$
> as abelian groups such that
>
> - $R_{0} = \K$
>
> - $R_{i} R_{j} \subset R_{i+j}$
>
> - $R_{+} := \bigoplus_{d > 0} R_{d}$ is maximal homogeneous ideal.
>
> An $R$-Module $M$ in this context is called Graded if
> $M = \bigoplus_{d = N}^{\infty} M_{d}$
> with $N \in \Z$ such that for all $i, j$ we have
> $R_{i} M_{j} \subset M_{i+j}$
> Elements of $R_{d}$ or $M_{d}$ are called **homogeneous** or **homogeneous of degree $d$**. A submodule $N \subset M$ is called graded (= homogenous) if it is generated by homogenous elements. Then $N$ is graded with
> $N_{d} = N \cap M_{d}$

> [!example|\*]
>
> 1. $\K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]$ is graded. Homogeneous of degree $d$ means every monomial has degree $d$.
> 2. Let $G$ be a linear algebraic group and $V$ a $G$-Module, then the [[Invariant Ring|invariant ring]] $\K[V]^{G}$ is graded by $\K[V]^{G}_{d} = \K[V]_{d} \cap \K[V]^{G}$
> 3. If $N \subset M$ is a graded submodule of a graded module, then $\faktor{M}{N}$ is again graded by $\left(\faktor{M}{N}\right)_{d} = \set{m + N \midline m \in M_{d}}$

---

> [!proposition|5.2] Ideal Generation is Algebra Generation
> Let $R$ be a graded algebra and $S \subset R_{+}$ consisting of of homogeneous elements of degree $d$. Then the following are equivalent
>
> 1. $R = \K[S]$ (generated as an Algebra)
> 2. $R_{+} = (S)$ (generated as an Ideal)
>
> For the implication $1 \implies 2$ elements of $S$ need not even be homogenous.

`\begin{proof}`
$1 \implies 2$
Let $R = \K[S]$ and consider some $f \in R_{+}$. Then $f \in R$ gives us that
$f = F(s_{1}, \ldots, s_{n})$
with $s_{i} \in S$ and $F$ some polynomial. Since $f \in R_{+}$ and all $s_{i} \in R_{+}$ we know that $F$ must have a constant coefficient $0$.

This means though by the definition of an ideal that $f \in (s_{1}, \ldots, s_{n}) \subset (S)$

$2 \implies 1$
We are now assuming that $R_{+} = (S)$. Note that it suffices to show that $R_{d} \subset \K[S]$ since then the statement is true. We thus use induction on $d$.

The base case is given by $R_{0} = \K \subset \K[S]$ holds true. Now let $d > 0$ and let $f \in R_{d}$ then $f \in R_{+} = (S)$ and therefore
$f = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} g_{i} s_{i}$
where $g_{i} \in R, s_{i} \in S$. Since all of $S$ is homogeneous we can multiply out the expression and considering the $g_{i}$ in their homogeneous parts, thus wlog all $g_{i}$ are homogeneous. Then the summands of degree $\neq d$ will sum to zero and we can delete them.
Then we can see that
$\mathrm{deg}(g_{i} s_{i}) = d \implies \mathrm{deg}(g_{i}) < d$
by induction we know that $g_{i} \in \K[S]$ and thus $g_{i}s_{i} \in \K[S]$ and finally
$f \in \K[S]$
`\end{proof}`

> [!proposition|5.3] Graded Nakayama Lemma
> Let $M$ be a graded $R$-module (and $R$ thus a graded Algebra) then for any $S \subset M$ consisting of homogeneous elements then the following are equivalent
>
> 1. $M = (S)$ (generated as an $R$-Module)
> 2. $\faktor{M}{R_{+}M}$ is generated by $\set{s + R_{+}M \midline s \in S}$ as a **$\K$-Vector Space**
>
> The implication $a \implies b$ does not require “homogeneous” property.

`\begin{proof}`
$1 \implies 2$
Let $m \in M$. Then by $1$ we get that $m = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} f_{i} s_{i} \implies m + R_{+}M = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} f_{i}^{(0)} s_{i} + R_{+}M$
where $f_{i}^{(0)}$ is the degree $0$-part of $f_{i}$, as the higher degrees get eaten by $R_{+}M$. This gives us our statement since $R_{0} = \K$ and thus $f_{i}^{(0)} \in \K$ and thus the expression is a $\K$-linear expression

$2 \implies 1$
We need to show $M_{d} \subset (S)_{R}$ and we will do this via strong induction on $d \in \Z$. Let $m \in M_{d}$ by $2.$ we ca write
$m + R_{+}M = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_{i} s_{i} + R_{+}M$
with $s_{i} \in S$ and $\alpha_{i} \in \K$. We can rewrite this and get
$m- \sum\limits \alpha_{i} s_{i} = \sum\limits_{j=1}^{l} g_{i}m_{i}$ with $g_{i} \in R_{+}, m_{i} \in M$. Since $m$ is fully of degree $d$ we can throw all $s_{i}$ that are not of degree $d$ and thus the sum is now a sum of homogeneous elements $s_{i}$. This means that
$\deg(g_{i}m_{i}) = d \implies \deg(m_{i}) < d$
which by induction is given by $m_{i} \in (S)_{R}$ and via the above equation we can see that thus see that
$m \in (S)_{R}$`\end{proof}`

> [!assumption|\*] From CA we take
>
> - A Ring is called Noetherian if every ideal is finitely generated
>
> - A Module is called Noetherian if every submodule is finitely generated
>   Equivalently: Every ascending chain $I_{1} \subset I_{2} \subset \ldots$ of ideals / submodules is finite
>
> - A module over a Nötherian ring $R$ is itsself Noetherian if and only if it is finitely generated
>
> - If $R$ is Noetherian ring, so it $R[x]$
>
> - Hilberts Basis Theorem: $\K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]$ is Noetherian

> [!corollary|5.4]
> For a graded algebra $R$ we have
>
> 1. $R$ is finitely generated $\iff$ $R$ is Noetherian
> 2. If $R$ is generated by $n$ elements (as algebra) then it is also generated by $n$ homogeneous elements.
> 3. If $R$ is Noetherian, then all homogeneous minimal generating sets have the same size and the same degrees. (where the minimal generating set means that none can be removed)

`\begin{proof}`
$1.$
We can show the first implication $\implies$ by noting that $R$ finitely generated means that $R = \faktor{\K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]}{I}$ which by hilberts basis theorem is noetherian.
The other implication assumes that $R$ is noetherian. Which means that $R_{+}$ is finitely generated. By the previous propositions (5.2) we have that $R$ is finitely generated

$2.$
Assume $R = \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}]$, then we can remove the degree zero part of $f_{i}$ and construct $f_{i}^{+} \in R_{+}$. Then we know that since $R_{0} = \K$ that $R = \K[f_{1}^{+}, \ldots, f_{n}^{+}]$. From the previous propositions we first get that $R_{+} = (f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n})_{R}$ and since this is a module we can us ethe nakayama version and notice that with $M = R_{+}$
$\faktor{M}{R_{+} M} = (f_{1}^{+}, \ldots, f_{n}^{+})_{\K}$
Conside the sub vectorspace: $V := \faktor{R_{+}}{R_{+}^{2}}$ then we get that
$\dim_{\K}(V) \leq n$
Since $V$ graded it has a homogeneous basis. From previous propositions we get our claim

$3.$
The size of a minimal generting set is $\dim_{K}(V)$ where $V$ is as in the previous construction. The degrees are given by $\dim_{\K}(V_{d})$
`\end{proof}`

## Way to Hilbert’s Theorem

> [!theorem|5.5]
> Let $G$ be a linearly reductive group and $V$ a $G$-Module then let $P := \K[V]$ and $R: P \to P^{G}$ the reynolds operator and consider the ideal $P_{+}^{G}$ the invariants with $0$ constant part and generate the ideal $H := (P_{+}^G)_{P}$ called the Hilbert Ideal. Then for $S \subset P_{+}$ homogeneous the following are equivalent
>
> 1. $P^{G} = \K[R(S)]$
> 2. $H = (S)_{P}$

`\begin{proof}`
We will first assume that $S \subset P_{+}^G$ and prove both directions under this

$1 \implies 2$
$P^G = \K[S] \overset{\text{prop 5.2}}{\implies} P_{+}^G = (S)_{P^G} \implies H = (S)_{P}$

$2 \implies 1$
We assume $H = (S)_{P}$ and let $f \in P^G_{+}$, then $f \in H$. This gives us
$f = \sum\limits_{i = 1}^n g_{i} f_{i}$
with $g_{i} \in P$ and $f_{i} \in S$. Applying the reynolds opertor we get
$f = R(f) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^n R(g_{i}) f_{i}$
since $f_{i} \in S \subset P_{+}^G$ by assumption and we have linearity for invariant factors. This sum is now in $(S)_{P^G}$ meaning
$P_{+}^G \subset (S)_{P^G}$
by proposition 5.2 we get $P^G = \K[S]$

Form what we have so far we could already prove Hilbert’s Finiteness Theorem. But we keep going to prove the statement and want to drop the assumption $S \subset P_{+}^G$. For this consider the map
$\bar{R} : \faktor{H}{P_{+} H} \to \faktor{H}{P_{+} H}, \quad \bar{f} \mapsto \overline{R(f)}$
this is well-defined since $H$ is $G$-stable and so is $P_{+}H$, meaning that $\at{R}{{P_{+}H}} = R_{P_{+}H}$, which gives us that
$R(P_{+}H) \subset P_{+}H$
Also note that $\bar{R}$ is linear and a $G$-homomorphism. Also
$\im(\bar{R}) \subset \faktor{H^G}{P_{+}H} \subset \left(\faktor{H}{P_{+}H} \right)^G$
Let $\bar{f} \in \left(\faktor{H}{P_{+}H} \right)^G$ then we have a $G$-epimorphism $H \to \faktor{H}{P_{+}H}$ and by an equivalent statement to linear reductivity we can now assume that $f \in H^G$. This implies that
$\overline{R(f)} = \bar{f}$
so we get that
$\at{\bar{R}}{\left(\faktor{H}{P_{+}H} \right)^G} = id$
meaning $\bar{R}$ is the reynolds operator of $\faktor{H}{P_{+}H}$.
$H$ is generated by invariants (as an ideal in $P$) which by nakayama tells us that $\faktor{H}{P_{+}H}$ is generated by invariants as a $\K$-space. Therefore $\faktor{H}{P_{+}H}$ has trivial $G$-action and thus
$\left(\faktor{H}{P_{+}H}\right)^G = \faktor{H}{P_{+}H}$
and thus the reynolds operator is the identity.

Now we get to the general case:
$2 \iff 1$
The next is all an iff
Assume $H = (S)_{P}$. Then this means that $\faktor{H}{P_{+}H} = (\bar{S})_{K}$ has a trivial reynolds operator and thus $(\overline{R(S)})_{\K} = \faktor{H}{P_{+}H}$. By the homogeneous Nakayama lemma we get
$H = (R(S))_{P}$
From the restricted case we know that for $S \subset P^G$ we get
$P^G = \K[R(S)]$`\end{proof}`

> [!corollary|5.6] Hilbert's Finiteness Theorem
> Let $G$ be linearly reductive and $V$ a $G$-Module then $\K[V]^G$ is finitely generated as an algebra.

`\begin{proof}`
Simply Apply Hilberts Basis theorem telling us that from the previous theorem $H$ is finitely generated as an ideal. By the previous theorem we can simply apply the reynolds operator to the generators of $H$.
`\end{proof}`

Historical context. The whole concept of noetherian rings was invented for the purpose of proving this theorem. He was then criticised by a man named Gordan that this “isnt mathematics but religion”. Since the proof is not constructive as the Hilbert Ideal can only be computed if we have the full invariant ring. Later he then came up with a constructive way.

> [!corollary|5.7]
> Let $G$ be a linearly reductive group and $X$ a $G$-Variety. Then $\K[X]^G$ is finitely generated

`\begin{proof}`
Let $\K[X] = \K[h_{1}, \ldots, h_{n}]$ then by local finiteness there exists a $G$-Module $V \subset \K[X]$ such that $h_{i} \in V$. This $V$ is embedded into our coordinate ring and also $V \cong V^{**} = \K[V^{*}]_{d=1}$ and $V^{*}$ is a $G$-Module.

Thus we have a $G$-homomorphism
$\K[V^{*}]_{d=1} \to \K[X]$
which can be extended by multiplicativity to a $G$-homomorphism
$\phi: \K[V^{*}] \to \K[X]$
$\phi$ by this construction is then a surjective algebra homomorphism since $h_{i} \in \im(\phi)$. Now using the equivalent condition to linear reaductiveness for epimorphisms we get that
$\phi(\K[V^{*}]^{G}) = \K[X]^{G}$
which reduces it to the case of $G$-Modules
`\end{proof}`

### Some additional facts

> [!theorem|\*] Nagata (1963)
> For a reductive group it follows that $\K[G]$ is finitely generated.

> [!example|\*] Nagata (1959)
> Hilbert’s 14. Problem was solved by a counterexample in the negative. Meaning he found a group $G$ such that
> $\K[V]^{G}$
> is not finitely generated ($\dim(V) =32$), these days we have found a simpler example with $\dim V = 5$.

> [!theorem|\*] Popov (1979)
> $G \text{ is reductive} \iff \K[X]^{G} \text{ f.g. for all G-Varieties } X$

## Derksen’s Algorithm

> [!definition|\*] Graph of the Action
> Let $G$ be a linearly reductive group. Consider the set
> $\Delta = \set{(v, \sigma(v)) \in X \times X \midline v \in V, \sigma \in G}$
> called the **graph of the action**. In general this is neither Zariski open nor closed.
> Also define $\mathcal{D} = \mathrm{Id}(\Delta) \subset \K[X \times X]$
> which we often call the **Derksen Ideal**

We will see that $\mathcal{D}$ is nice and can be computed, which makes it valuable.

> [!exercise|\*]
> Show that $\mathcal{D}$ is homogeneous ideal in $\K[X \times X]$

Now we get to the actual algorithm

> [!theorem|\*] Derksens Algorithm
> Given a linearly reductive Group $G$ and its Derksen Ideal $\mathcal{D}$ let $f_{1}(x_{1} \ldots x_{n}, y_{1}, \ldots, y_{n}), \ldots f_{l}(x_{1} \ldots x_{n}, y_{1}, \ldots, y_{n}) \in \K[V \times V]$ be homogeneous generators of $\mathcal{D}$. Then
> $T = \set{f_{i}(x_{1} \ldots x_{n}, 0, \ldots, 0) \midline 1 \leq i \leq l}$
> generate the hilbert ideal $H$. This means by Hilberts theorem that
> $\K[V]^{G} = \K[\mathcal{R}(T)]$

`\begin{proof}`

> [!claim|\*]
> $H \subset (T)_{\K[\underline{x}]}$

Let $f \in \K[V]^{G}_{+}$, then see that for $v \in V, \sigma \in G$ we calculate that
$f(v) - f(\sigma(v)) = 0$
This can be intepreted in the graph of the action to say that
$f(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}) - f(y_{1}, \ldots, y_{n}) = 0$ for any points in $\Delta$. This means that $f(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}) - f(y_{1}, \ldots, y_{n}) \in \mathrm{Id}(\Delta) = \mathcal{D}$ so, by calculating we can explicitly write that
$f(\underline{x}) - f(\underline{y}) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{l} g_{i}(\underline{x}, \underline{y}) f(\underline{x}, \underline{y}) \implies f(\underline{x}) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{l} g_{i}(\underline{x}, \underline{0}) f_{i}(\underline{x}, \underline{0}) \in T$
which gives us our claim.

> [!claim|\*]
> $f_{i}(\underline{x}, \underline{0}) \in H$

More generally, let $f \in \mathcal{D}$
Let $G$ act on $V \times V$ by $\sigma(v, w) = (v, \sigma(w))$. This gives us an action on $\K[\underline{x}, \underline{y}]$. By linear reductivity we have a Reynolds operators $R_{y}$. $G$ fixed $\K[\underline{x}]$ which implies by theorem 5.9 that $R_{y}$ is $\K[\underline{x}]$-linear.

Now let $(v, w) \in \Delta$, then $(v, \sigma(w)) \in \Delta$, meaning that the graph of the action is invariant under our specially defined action. This means that also its vanishing ideal is stable which tells us that $\mathcal{D}$ is $G$-Stable.

Using a previous proposition we get that $R_{y}(\mathcal{D}) \subset \mathcal{D}$

Now finally consider
$f(\underline{x}, 0) - f(\underline{x}, \underline{y}) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{r} g_{i}(\underline{x}) u_{i}(\underline{y})$
When considering the $y$ variables as the main variables we notice that this polynomial has no constant coefficient in $y$, thus $u_{i} \in \K[\underline{y}]_{+}$

$f(\underline{x}, 0) = R_{y}(f(\underline{x}, 0)) = R_{y}(f(\underline{x}, \underline{y})) + \sum\limits_{i=1}^{r} g_{i}(x) R_{y}(u_{i}(\underline{y}))$

Keep this equation in mind and consider a map
$\phi: \K[\underline{x}, \underline{y}] \mapsto \K[\underline{x}],  \quad x_{i} \mapsto x_{i}, y_{i} \mapsto x_{i}$
For some $g \in \mathcal{D}$ we get by this map that
$\phi(g)(v) = g(v, v) = 0$
since $(v, v) \in \Delta$. This means that $\mathcal{D} \subset \ker \phi$.

Applying $\phi$ to the entire equation above we get:
$f(\underline{x}, 0) = \phi(f(\underline{x} ,0)) = \phi(R_{y}(f(\underline{x},\underline{y}))) + \phi\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r} g_{i}(x) R_{y}(u_{i}(\underline{y}))\right)$
since $f \in \mathcal{D}$ and $R_{y}(\mathcal{D}) \subset \mathcal{D}$ we know that
$f(\underline{x}, 0) = \phi\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r} g_{i}(x) R_{y}(u_{i}(\underline{y}))\right) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{r} g_{i}(x) \cdot (\phi \circ R_{y} \circ h_{i})(\underline{y})$
We can now use the fact that $\phi \circ R_{y} \circ h_{i}(\underline{y}) = R_{x} \circ h_{i} (\underline{x}) \in \K[\underline{x}]^{G}_{+}$. Since these are elements that generate the hilbert ideal we know that
$f(\underline{x}, 0) \in (K[x]_{+}^{G})_{\K[x]}$

`\end{proof}`

To consider the practicability of this algorithm lets discuss some pertinent issues. Firstly the computation of $\mathcal{D}$, how is it given. We consider the linear algebraic groups $G \subset \K^{r}$ an affine variety. We consider it as given by its vanishing ideal
$I_{G} = \mathcal{I}(G) \subset \K[t_{1}, \ldots, t_{n}]$
where this ideal is given by generators $I_{G} = (g_{1}, \ldots, g_{m})$.
We also need a $G$ Module $V$ given by a morphism
$G \to \mathrm{GL}_{n}(\K), \sigma \mapsto (a_{ij} \sigma) = A \sigma$
with $a_{ij} \in \K[t_{1}, \ldots, t_{n}]$.

This data is almost always given and is thus a good starting point to go into comutation. We first consider a precursory Derksen Ideal
$\mathcal{D}_{0} = \left(g_{1}, \ldots , g_{m}, y_{1} - \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n} a_{1j} x_{j}, \ldots, y_{n} - \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n} a_{nj} x_{j}\right) \subset \K[\underline{t}, \underline{x}, \underline{y}]$

> [!proposition|5.9] Derksen Ideal is given by it’s Precursor
> $\mathcal{D} = \K[\underline{x}, \underline{y}] \cap \mathcal{D}_{0}$
> Where we sometimes call $\mathcal{D}_{0}$ the “elimination Ideal”

`\begin{proof}`
Consider
$\Delta_{0} = \set{(\sigma, v, \sigma(v)) \midline \sigma \in G, v \in V}$

> [!claim|\*]
> $\mathcal{D}_{0} = \mathcal{I}(\Delta_{0})$

This is an easy computation by simply evaluating each generator of $\mathcal{D}_{0}$ at the points in $\Delta_{0}$. This gives that
$\mathcal{D}_{0} \subset \mathcal{I}(\Delta_{0})$
For the other inclusion consider a $f \in \mathcal{I}(\Delta_{0})$. Obtain $\widehat{f} \in \K[\underline{t}, \underline{x}]$ by setting
$y_{i} = \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} x_{j}$
It is now obvious that $f- \widehat{f} \in \mathcal{D}_{0} \subset \mathcal{I}(\Delta_{0})$. Since $f \in \mathcal{I}(\Delta_{0})$ we get that $\widehat{f} \in \mathcal{I}(\Delta_{0})$
For $v \in V, \sigma \in G$ we get
$\widehat{f}(\sigma, v) = \widehat{f}(\sigma, v, \sigma(v))$
by considering $\widehat{f}$ as inside $\K[\underline{t}, \underline{x}, \underline{y}]$ and since its independent we can chose the third variables as we please. This then gives us that
$\widehat{f}(\sigma, v) = 0$
this gives s that $\widehat{f} \in (g_{1}, \ldots, g_{m})$ and thus $\widehat{f} \in \mathcal{D}_{0}$. Now given $f - \widehat{f} \in \mathcal{D}_{0}$ we get that $f \in \mathcal{D}_{0}$

> [!claim|\*]
> $\mathcal{D} = \K[\underline{x}, \underline{y}] \cap \mathcal{I}(\Delta_{0})$

Let us first prove the first inclusion, so let $f \in \mathcal{D}$. This gives us that obviously $f \in \K[x, y]$ and that for $\sigma \in G, v \in V$ we can evaluate
$f(\sigma, v, \sigma(v)) = f(v, \sigma(v))$
since $f$ doesnt depend on $t$ we can ignore the first entry. Since $(v, \sigma(v)) \in \Delta$ we get that $f(\sigma, v, \sigma(v)) = 0$ meaning $f \in \mathcal{I}(\Delta_{0})$

For the other inclusion consider $f \in \K[\underline{x}, \underline{y}] \cap \mathcal{I}(\Delta_{0})$. Let $(v, \sigma(v)) \in \Delta$ then we know that
$f(v, \sigma(v)) = f(\sigma, v, \sigma(v)) = 0$
where we can again add an entry $\sigma$ by considering $f \in \K[t, x, y]$ with now components in $t$. Thus $f \in \mathcal{I}(\Delta) = \mathcal{D}$
`\end{proof}`

This gives us the entire computational aspect, since $\mathcal{D}_{0}$ is computable via gröbner basis. Specifically choose a suitable monomial ordering on $\K[\underline{t}, \underline{x}, \underline{y}]$. For example one might choose the lexicographic ordering with $x_{1} < x_{2} < \ldots < x_{n} < y_{1} < \ldots < y_{n} < t_{1} < \ldots t_{r}$
Let $g_{0}$ be a gröbnerbasis of $\mathcal{D}_{0}$. Then $\K[\underline{x}, \underline{y}] \cap \delta_{0} =: g$ is a (Gröbner) basis of $\mathcal{D}$

### Variant for Applying Reynolds

For some $f \in g$ let $d = \deg (f(\underline{x}, 0))$ (which assumes that this can be done, but this is given by gröbner basis). Now compute a $\K$-Basis of $\K[V]_{d}^{G}$ (this is $\ker\left(\K[V]_{d} \ni f \mapsto \tilde{\mu}(f) - 1 \otimes f\right)$ )

> [!info] Algorithm
> **Input**: A linear reductive group $G$ and a $G$-Module $V$
> **Output:** Generators of $\K[V]^{G}$
> **Steps**
>
> 1. Form $\mathcal{D}_{0} \subset \K[\underline{t}, \underline{x}, \underline{y}]$
> 2. Compute a Gröbner basis $\delta_{0}$ of $\mathcal{D}_{0}$ with respect to a suitable monomial ordering
> 3. The set $\set{R(f(\underline{x}, 0)) \midline f \in \delta} = \K[\underline{x}, \underline{y}] \cap \delta_{0}$ generate $\K[V]^{G}$. Alternatively take the union of all $B_{d} := \text{basis of } \K[V]^{G}_{d}, d = \deg(f(\underline{x}, 0))$

> [!remark|\*]
> There is also an algorithm for $G$ reductive (not linearly reductive) and $V$ a $G$-Module then there exists an Algorithm by G. Kemper (2003) for $\K[V]^{G}$.
> For $G$ reductive and $X$ a $G$-Variety there also exists an algorithm for $\K[X]^{G}$ given by Derksen and Kemper in 2008.
