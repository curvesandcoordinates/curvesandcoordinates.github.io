# Linearly Reductive Groups

> [!definition|4.1]
> Let $G$ be an affine algebraic group and $V$ a $G$-Module.
>
> 1. A submodule is a subspace $W \subset V$ such that $G(W) \subset W$. Then we know that $W$ and $\faktor{V}{W}$ is a $G$-Module itsself.
> 2. $V$ is called _irreducible_ (or simple) if $\set{0}$ and $V$ are the only submodules.
> 3. $V$ is called _indecomposable_ if $V$ is not the direct sum two non-zero submodules
> 4. $V$ is called _fully reducible_ (or semi-simple) if it is a direct sum of irreducible submodules.
> 5. $G$ is called _linearly reductive_ if for every $G$-Module $V$ and a Submodule $W \subset V$ there exists $U \subset V$ such that $W \bigoplus U = V$
> 6. For two $G$-Modules $V, W$ a $G$-Homomorphism is a map $\phi: V \to W$ which is linear and satisfies $\forall \sigma \in G: \phi \circ \sigma = \sigma \circ \phi$

> [!example|\*]
> Consider $G = \mathbb{G}_{a}$ which acts on $V = \K^{2}$ by $\rho(a) \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}1 & a \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ then $K \cdot e_{1}$ gives us a submodule without a complement, which means that $V$ is indecomposable but not irreducible. From this we know that $\mathbb{G}_{a}$ is not linearly reductive.
> We can also consider $G = SL_{2}(\K)$ acting in the natural way on $\K^{2}$ since any vector is in the same orbit this is irreducible

> [!theorem|4.2]
> For an affine algebraic group $G$ the following are equivalent:
>
> 1. $G$ is linearly reductive
> 2. Every $G$-Module $V$ is fully reducible
> 3. For every $G$-Module $V$ there is a $G$-homomorphism $R: V \to V^{G}$ such that $\at{R}{V^G} = id$
> 4. For a $G$-Module $V$ and a $G$-homomorphism $0 \neq \Pi: V \to \K$ (where $\K$ is given by the trivial action) then there is a $G$-Homomorphism $\phi: K \to V$ with $\Pi \circ \phi = \mathrm{id}_{K}$
> 5. For every surjective $G$-Homomorphism $\phi: V \to W$ we have $\phi(V^{G}) = W^{G}$

Before we go on to the proof consider the following example. Assume that $\mathrm{char}(K)$ does not divide $|G| < \infty$ then we can construct a map:
$R: V \to V^{G}, v \mapsto \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{\sigma \in G} \sigma(v)$
which then shows us that $G$ is linearly reductive by the equivalence.

`\begin{proof}`
$1. \implies 2.$
Induction on $\dim(V)$. Begin with $V = \set{0}$ or irreducible, then we are done. Otherwise we have a nontrivial submodule $U \subset V$ with $U \neq 0$. Which gives us by linear reducibility that
$V = U \oplus W$
where $W$ is the complement.
This means that by the induction step $U, W$ is fully reducible, so  is $V$.

$2. \implies 3.$
By the full reducibility of any $G$-Module we know that any $G$-Module $V$ can be written as
$V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^{r} V_{i}$
with $V_{i}$ irreducible submodules.
For each $i$ we also get that
$V_{i}^{G} \subset V_{i}$
is a submodule meaning that by irreducibility $V_{i}^{G} = \set{0}$ or $V_{i}^{G} = V_{i}$.
Now take $R: V \to V^{G}$ the projection onto the direct sum of those $V_{i}$ with $V_{i}^{G} = V_{i}$

$3. \implies 4.$
We are given a $G$-Module $V$ and an epimorphism $\Pi: V \to \K$. We know that $G$ acts on the dual space $V^{*}$ by
$\sigma(f)(v) = f(\sigma^{-1}(v))$
In fact we can see that $V^{*}$ becomes a $G$-Module. We also get that $\left(V^{*}\right)^{G} = \mathrm{Hom}_{G}(V, K)$ and can see that the given $\Pi \in \left(V^{*}\right)^{G}$. Now using the assumtion from $3.$ on $V^{*}$ we know that we have a map
$R: V^{*} \to \left(V^{*}\right)^{G}, \quad \at{R}{\left(V^{*}\right)^{G}} = \mathrm{id}$
This gives us that we can write
$V^{*} = \left(V^{*}\right)^{G} \oplus \ker(R)$
Decomposing gives us that we can define a map $\psi: V^{*} \to \K$ such that $\psi_{\ker (R)} = 0$ and
$\psi \circ \Pi = 1$
This means that this $\psi$ must be a $G$-Homomorphism. Also note that $\psi$ is in the Bidual $\left(V^{*}\right)^{*} \cong V$ which gives us that there exists a vector $v \in V$ such that
$\forall f \in V^{*}: \psi(f) = f(v)$
We would now like to show that $v$ must be invariant. Consider
$f (\sigma(v)) =\sigma^{-1}(f(v)) = \psi(\sigma^{-1}(f)) \overset{G-Hom}{=} \sigma^{-1} \psi(f) = \psi(f) = f(v)$
We now finally define a map $\phi: K \to V$ with $\phi(\alpha) = \alpha v$. Then we can easily see that $\phi$ is a $G$-Homomorphism
since:
$\Pi(\phi(\alpha)) = \Pi(\alpha v) = \alpha \Pi(v) = \alpha \psi(\Pi) = \alpha$
$4. \implies 5.$
Clearly we have that since $\phi$ is a $G$-Homomorphism that
$\phi(V^{G}) \subset W^{G}$
We only need to prove the reverse inclusion. Let $0 \neq w \in W^{G}$ and set $U := \phi^{-1}(\K \cdot w)$. Then we can see that $U$ is a submodule since it is the preimage of a submodule. We also have that
$\at{\phi}{U}: U \to \K \cdot w \cong \K$
is a surjective $G$-Homomorphism. Using the assumtions given in $4.$ we know that there exists a $\psi: \K \cdot w \to U$ such that $\phi \circ \psi = \mathrm{id}_{K \cdot v}$ which gives us that
$w = \phi(\psi(w))$
Since the image of $\psi$ has trivial $G$-Action we get that $\psi(w) \in V^{G}$ and thus $w$ is in the image of $\phi$.

$5. \implies 1.$
This step is rather large so we need some preperation. Let $V$ be a $G$-Module and $U \subset V$ a submodule. We can then say that $\mathrm{Hom}_{\K}(V, U)$ is a $G$-Module by the action
$\sigma(\phi) = \sigma \circ \phi \circ \sigma^{-1}$
And also all invariants $\mathrm{Hom}_{\K}(V, U)^{G} = \mathrm{Hom}_{G}(V, U)$ since the equation for invariance is
$\sigma \circ \phi = \phi \circ \sigma$
which is exactly the condition needed to be a $G$-Homomorphism.
We can now consider the restriction map
$\mathrm{res}: \mathrm{Hom}_{\K}(V, U) \to \mathrm{Hom}_{\K}(U, U)$
Note that we have $\mathrm{id}_{U} \in \mathrm{Hom}_{\K}(U, U)^{G}$. By basis extension from linear algebra we can see that $\mathrm{res}$ is surjective and also a $G$-homomorphism since restriction commutes with group action.

By $5.$ we get that there exists $\phi \in \mathrm{Hom}_{\K}(V, U)^{G}$ such that $\mathrm{res}(\phi) = \mathrm{id}_{U}$ meaning it is a $G$-Projection. Thus
$\phi \in \mathrm{Hom}_{G}(V, U)$
and by linear algebra we can decompose
$V = U \oplus \ker \phi$
`\end{proof}`

From this large statement we get a lot

> [!proposition|4.3] Reynolds Operator on Varieties
> Let $G$ be linearly reductive and $V$ a $G$-Module then
>
> 1. $R: V \to V^{G}$ be a $G$-Hom with $\at{R}{V^{G}} = \mathrm{id}$ is uniquely determined. Write $R_{V}$ and call it the **“Reynolds Operator”**
> 2. For a submodule $U \subset V$ we have $R_{U} = \at{R_{V}}{U}$

`\begin{proof}`
$1.$
The existence of the Reynolds operator is given by the previous statement. By linear recutiveness we know that
$V = V^{G} \oplus W$
with $W$ submodule and let $R: V \to V^{G}$ be a $G$-Homomorphism with $\at{R}{V^{G}} = \mathrm{id}$. Then
$\at{R}{W} : W \to R(W)$
is by definition surjective and by using the previous theorem statement $5.$ we get that
$R(W) = R(W)^{G} =R(W^{G}) =R(\set{0}) = \set{0}$ since $W^{G} = W \cap V^{G} = \set{0}$. This gives us that $\at{R}{W} = 0$. Thus $R$ is uniquely determined since its action on all of $V$ is defined.

$2.$
Have $V = U \oplus W$ with $W$ some submodule. Then we have
$R_{V} = R_{U} + R_{W}, \quad V^{G} = U^{G} \oplus W^{G}$
Now we see that restricting
$\at{R_{V}}{U} = R_{U}$
`\end{proof}`

It might seem weird that we are currently discussing the invariance of vectors in a vector space instead of the invariance of polynomials or regular functions. To this end consider the following corolaray

> [!corollary|4.4] Reynolds Operator on Coordinate Rings
> Let $G$ be linearly reductive and $X$ a $G$-Variety. Then there is a $G$-Homomorphism
> $R: K[X] \to K[X]^{G}$
> meaning we have that $R \circ \sigma = \sigma \circ R = R$ but $R$ is not an algebra homomorphism (not multiplicative). It also has the property that
> $\at{R}{K[X]^{G}} = \mathrm{id}$
> In this context $R$ is again called the **Reynolds Operator**

`\begin{proof}`
We know that $k[X]$ is made up of small $G$-Modules. Vor any $G$- Module $V \subset K[X]$ we define the corresponding Reynolds Operator:
$R_{V}: V \to V^{G} \subset K[X]^{G}$
For another $G$-module $W \subset K[X]$ we have
$\at{R_{V}}{V \cap W} = R_{V \cap W} = \at{R_{W}}{V \cap W}$
by the previous proposition. This compatability allows us to extend our operators to a map $R: K[X] \to K[X]^{G}$.
`\end{proof}`

The definition of Linearly Reductive Groups is slightly extrinsic since it is a property of possible Representations of the Group. We can still ask outselves what maybe the intrinsic idea can be. We ask: What groups are linearly reductive? And how can we intrinsically define linear redcutvity.

We can note that $G$ can act on itself by left and right translation. For any $\sigma \in G$ let
$L_{\sigma}: G \to G , \tau \mapsto \sigma \tau$
and
$R_{\sigma}: G \to G, \tau \mapsto \tau \sigma^{-1}$

> [!theorem|\*] Intrinsic Characterisation using Invariant Integral
> For an affine algebraic group $G$ the following are equivalent
>
> 1. $G$ is linearly reductive
> 2. There exists a linear map $I: \K[G] \to \K$ such that $I(1) = 1$ and $\forall \sigma \in G, g \in \K[G]$: $I(g \circ L_{\sigma}) = I(g) = I(g \circ R_{\sigma}) \iff \forall \sigma \in G: I \circ L_{\sigma}^{*} = I = I \circ R_{\sigma}^{*}$
>    We call the map $I$ a (translation-)invariant integral.

`\begin{proof}`
$1. \implies 2.$
Let $G$ be a linearly reductive subgroup. Then by an exercise we get that $G \times G$ is also linearly reductive. We know that $G \times G$ acts on $G$ by
$(\sigma, \tau)(\rho) = \sigma \rho \tau^{-1}$
By the linear reductivity we get that there exists a **Reynolds Operator** $I := R: \K[G] \to \K[G]^{G \times G}$. By noting that our action has only one orbit we get that $\K[G]^{G \times G} = \K$ and thus our operator is a good candidate for the integral.

Checking the desired property gives us that
$I(1) = R(1) = 1$
since $1 \in K[G]$ is constant and thus an invariant. Now noting that $R$ is a $G \times G$ homomorphism i.e. for $\sigma, \tau \in G, g \in K[G]$ we get that
$I(g \circ L_{\sigma} \circ R_{\tau}) = I(g)$
since this holds for $\sigma = e$ and $\tau = e$ we get our desired property

$2. \implies 1.$
Assume that there exists such an $I: \K[G] \to \K$ and let $V$ be a $G$-Module. We now need to find a suitable Reynolds operator. We can consider the map induced by our action
$\tilde{\mu}: V \to \K[G] \otimes V$
For $\tau \in G$ and $v \in V$ we write $\tilde{\mu}(v) = \sum\limits_{i=1} g_{i} \otimes v$. Then we have
$\tau(v) = \sum\limits_{i} g_{i}(\tau) v_{i}$
For some $\sigma \in G$

$$$\begin{align*}
\sum\limits_{i} g_{i}(\tau) \sigma(v_{i}) = \sigma \left(\sum\limits_{i} g_{i}(\tau) v_{i}\right) = \sigma(\tau(v)) = (\sigma \tau)(v) \\\end{align*}$$
This gives us the equality
$$\sum\limits_{i} g_{i} \circ R_{\tau^{-1}}(\sigma) v_{i} = \sum\limits_{i} g_{i}(\sigma \tau) v_{i} = \sum\limits_{i} g_{i} \circ L_{\sigma}(\tau) v_{i}$$
This gives that
$$\sum\limits_{i} g_{i} \otimes \sigma (v_{i}) = \sum\limits_{i} (g_{i} \circ L_{\sigma}) \otimes v_{i}$$
and also
$$\tilde{\mu}(\tau(v)) = \sum\limits_{i}(g_{i} \circ R_{\tau^{-1}} \otimes v_{i})$$

Now define a map
$$R: V \to K[G] \otimes V \to V \otimes V \cong V$$
given by $R = (I \otimes \mathrm{id}_{V}) \circ \tilde{\mu}$

> [!claim|*] The Defined Operator Projects to Invariants
> $$R(V) \subset V^{G}$$

Let $v \in V$ and $\sigma \in G$ and consider
$$\sigma(R(v)) = \sigma\left( I \otimes id \left(\sum\limits_{i = 1} g_{i} \otimes v_{i}\right) \right) = \sigma \left( \sum\limits_{i =1} I(g_{i}) v_{i}\right) = \sum\limits_{i = 1} I(g_{i}) \sigma(v_{i}) = (I \otimes \mathrm{id}_{V}) \left( \sum\limits_{i} g_{i} \otimes \sigma(v_{i})\right)$$
By our previous findings we get that this is equal to
$$\sigma(R(v)) = (I \otimes \mathrm{id}_{V}) \left(\sum\limits(g_{i} \circ L_{\sigma}) \otimes v_{i}\right) = \sum\limits_{i} I(g_{i} \circ L_{\sigma}) v_{i} = \sum\limits I(g_{i}) v_{i} = R(v)$$
showing the claim

> [!claim|*]
> $R$ is a $G$-homomorphism

Let $\tau \in G, v \in V$ then consider
$$R(\tau(v)) = (I \otimes \mathrm{id}_{V})( \tilde{\mu} (\tau(v))) = (I \otimes \mathrm{id}_{V})\left(\sum\limits_{i}(g_{i} \circ R_{\tau^{-1}}) \otimes v_{i}\right)$$
using the previous considerations. Now we can apply invariance of $I$ to obtain
$$R(\tau(v)) = R(v)$$
> [!claim|*]
> $$\at{R}{V^{G}} = id$$

Let $v \in V^{G}$, then we know that $\tilde{\mu}(v) = 1 \otimes v$ which gives us
$$R(v) = I(1)v = v$$
`\end{proof}`

> [!example|*] Multiplicative Group and its Families are linearly reductive
> The group $\mathbb{G}_{m}$ is linearly reductive. Also the algebraic Torus defined by
> $$T_{n} = \mathbb{G}_{m} \times \ldots \times \mathbb{G}_{m} \cong \set{ \mathrm{diag}(a_{1}, \ldots, a_{n}) \midline a_{i} in \mathbb{G}_{m}}$$
> is also linearly reductive.


> [!corollary|4.6]
> Let $|G| < \infty$ then
> $$G \text{ is linearly reductive} \iff \mathrm{char}(K) \nmid |G|$$

`\begin{proof}`
The direction from right to left has been shown in a previous example.

The left to right direction is proven here:
Let $G$ be linearly reductive, meaning by our theorem there exists a translation invariant integral function
$$I: \K[G] \to \K$$
For $\sigma \in G$ define the indicator function $\delta_{\sigma}(\tau) = \delta_{\sigma, \tau} =\begin{cases} 1, \quad \sigma = \tau \\ 0 \end{cases}$ .

It is easily calculable that this map behaves nicely with left and right multiplication as
$$\delta_{\sigma} \circ R_{\rho} = \delta_{\sigma \rho}$$
Now note that
$$1 = I(1) = I \left( \sum\limits_{\sigma \in G} \delta_{\sigma} \right) = \sum\limits_{\sigma \in G} I(\delta_{\sigma}) = \sum\limits_{\sigma \in G} I(\delta_{e} \circ R_{\sigma}) = |G| I(\delta_{e})$$
For this to be true we know that
$$\mathrm{char}(K) \nmid |G|$$
`\end{proof}`

We now want to increase the number of examples we have for linearly reductive groups. The following theorem will be used to show that all classical groups are linearly reductive.

> [!theorem|4.7]
> Let $K = \C$ and let $G$ be an affine algebraic group such that $G$ has a subgroup $H \leq G$ which is Zariski dense, meaning $\bar{H} = G$ and compact in the euclidean topology. Then $G$ is linearly reductive.

`\begin{proof}`
The proof requires a so called Haar Measure
> [!definition|*] Haar Measure
> A measure $\mu$ on $H$ such that $\mu(H) = 1$ and for $S \subset H$ measurable and $\sigma \in G$ we want
> $$\mu(\sigma S) = \mu(S) = \mu(S \sigma)$$
> Such an object exists under the condition of $H$ being compact in eucleadean topology, which we will not show here

From the Haar measure we obtain a map
$$I_{H}: C(H, \C) \to \C, \quad f \mapsto \int_{H} f d\mu$$
This is a good candidate for an integral in our sense since
- $I_{H}(1) = \mu(H) = 1$
- For all $\sigma \in H, f \in C(H, \C)$ we have $$I_{H}(f \circ L_{\sigma}) = I_{H}(f) = I_{H}(f \circ R_{\sigma})$$
- $I_{H}$ is always $\C$-linear

which we wont go into too much detail on.

Now for any $f \in \K[G]$ define 
$$I_{G}(f) = I_{H}(\at{f}{H})$$
This now shares all the properties since
- $I_{G}(1) = I_{H}(1) =1$
- For $\sigma \in H$ we have $I_{G}(f \circ L_{\sigma}) = I_{H}(\at{f \circ L_{\sigma}}{H}) = I_{H}(\at{f}{H} \circ \at{L_{\sigma}}{H})$

Now consider the action of $G$ acting on $\C[G]$ by $f \mapsto f \circ L_{\sigma^{-1}}$. Since $\C[G]$ is a $G$-Variety we know by previous corollary that it is locally finite and there exists $V \subset \C[G]$ which is a $G$-Module with $f \in V$.

Now we can restrict our integral by considering
$$\at{I_{G}}{V}: V \to \C$$
and since this is linear we know that $\at{I_{G}}{V} \in V^{*}$. We also know that $V^{*}$ is a $G$-module. Now we can act on this map by considering it simply a vector in $V^{*}$. Define
$$\phi: G \to V^{*}, \sigma \mapsto \sigma \left(\at{I_{G}}{V}\right)$$
Now consider 
$$Z = \set{ \sigma \in G \midline \phi(\sigma) = \at{I_{G}}{V}} \subset G$$
which is a Zariski closed subset, since it is the preimage under $\phi$ of the single point $\set{\at{I_{G}}{V}}$.

Now we have that for all $\sigma \in H$ we have
$$\sigma(\at{I_{G}}{V}) = \at{I_{G}}{V} \implies H \subset Z$$
Due to translation invariance???
Using that $H$ is dense in $G$ we find that $Z = G$. This implies trivially that
$$I_{G}(f \circ L_{\sigma}) = I_{G}(f)$$

by a similar argument we get
$$I_{G}(f \circ R_{\sigma}) = I_{G}(f)$$

`\end{proof}`
> [!example|*]
> Let $G = \mathbb{G}_{m} = \C^{\times}$ and let $H = S^{1} \subset \C$ is a prime example for this theorem. We can extend this to $n$-dimensional torus $\prod_{i=1}^{n} H$ inside $G = \prod_{i = 1}^{n} \mathbb{G}_{m}$


> [!theorem|*]
> $$GL_{n}(\C)$$
> is linearly reductive

`\begin{proof}`
Consider the [[Rotation Matrix Groups|unitary matrix]] group for $H$. Then this group is Euclidean closed, since its defined by a set of equations but it cant be written as a locus of polynomials over $\C$ which means that it isnt Zariski closed. We also know that each column has unit norm, which means that $H$ is bounded which means its a compact group.

> [!claim|*]
> $$\overline{H} = G$$

Consider the torus $T = \set{(\mathrm{diag}(a_{1}, \ldots, a_{n}) \midline a_{i} \in \C^{\times}}$ then we can see that 
$$H \cap T = \set{\mathrm{diag}(a_{1}, \ldots, a_{n}) \midline |a_{i}| = 1}$$
We have seen that $\overline{H \cap T} = T$ which implies that $T \subset \overline{H}$. To tie up this argument consider the singualr value decomposition for any $A \in G$ we have
$$A = U \Sigma V$$
with $U, V \in H$ and $\Sigma \in T$. This allows us to write our group
$$G = H T H \subset H \overline{H} \overline{H} \subset \overline{H} \overline{H} \overline{H} = \overline{H} \subset G$$
which proves that
$$\overline{H} = G$$
`\end{proof}`

We now go back to the reynolds operator and state a property of it

> [!theorem]
> Let $G$ be linearly reductive and $X$ a $G$-variety, then the Reynolds operator
> $$R: \K[X] \to \K[X]^{G}$$
> Then for $f \in \K[X]^{G}$ and $g \in \K[X]$ we have
> $$R(f g) = f R(g)$$
> So $R$ is a homomorphism of $\K[X]^{G}$-Modules

`\begin{proof}`
Let $f \in \K[X]^{G}$ and $g \in \K[X]$. By the previous corollary stating that we have local finiteness we know that there exists a $G$-module $V \in \K[X]$ such that $g \in V$. Now we know that $W = f \cdot V \subset \K[X]$ is a $G$-stable subspace and ths a $G$-Module. Define a map
$$\phi: V \to W, h \mapsto f h$$
which is a $G$-homomorphism because $f$ is an invaraint. Let $U := \ker(\phi)$ then because $G$ is linearly reductive we must have a complement and have
$$V = U \oplus W'$$
Now we note that $\at{\phi}{W'}: W' \to W$ is now an isomorphism.
$$ \begin{CD} W' @>\at{\phi}{W'}>> W\\ @VR_{W'}VV @VVR_{W}V \\  W'^{G} @>>> W^{G}\end{CD}$$
then the map $\phi \circ R_{W'} \circ \at{\phi}{W'}^{-1}$ has all the properties of a reynolds operator and thus can be defined as $R_{W}$

This gives us that since $fg \in W$ we get
$$R(fg) = R_{W}(fg) = f \cdot R_{W'}(\at{\phi}{W'}^{-1}(fg)) = f R_{W'}(h)$$
using $h = \at{\phi}{W'}^{-1}(fg)$ we can find that $\phi(h) = fg = \phi(g) \implies g - h \in U$ so now consider the other way
$$f R(g) = \phi(R_{V}(g)) = \phi(R_{V}(g-h) + R_{V}(h)) = \phi(R_{U}(g - h) + R_{W'}(h))$$
Since $R_{U}(g - h) \subset U$ we get that
$$f R(g) = \phi(R_{W'}(h)) = f R_{W^{'}}(h)$$
`\end{proof}`

## Other Notions of Reductivity
An affine algebraic group $G$ is called **reductive** if every connected unipotent, normal subgroup $U \subset G$ is trivial equivalently every connected solvable normal subgroup is a Torus.

> [!theorem|*] Haboush's Theorem
> Every reductive group is geometrically reductive. This means for every $G$-Module $V$ and non-zero $0 \neq v \in V^{G}$ there exists $d \in \N$ and $f \in \K[V]^{G}_{d}$ such that
> $$f(v) \neq 0$$
> (If $G$ is linearly reductive then $d = 1$)

> [!example|*]
> Let $G$ be finite then we can show that this is always geometrically inductive. Let $0 \neq v \in V^{G}$, then we know that there exists $g \in V^{*}$ such that $g (v) \neq 0$. Define
> $$f = \prod_{\sigma \in G} \sigma(g) \in \K[V]^{G}_{|G|}$$
> testing
> $$f(v) = \prod_{\sigma \in G} g(\sigma^{-1}(v)) = g(v)^{|G|} \neq 0$$

So reductive is the same as geometrically reductive. Moreover if $\mathrm{char}(\K) = 0$ then al three notions of reductivity fall together.
$$$
