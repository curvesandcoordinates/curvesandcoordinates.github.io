### Recap

We first require some setup taken from commutative algebra, similar to [[CIT4130018 Linear Algebraic Groups| Linear Algebric Groups from Liedtke]].

> [!definition|\*] Coordinate Rings
> Let $X \subset \K^{n}$ be an affine variety and a function $f: X \to K$ is called regular if it is given by a polynomial in $K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]$.
> We denote $K[X]$ as the ring of all regular functions on $X$ and call it the coordinate ring.
> The ring has a surjective map $K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}] \to K[X], \quad f \mapsto \at{f}{X}$
> with kernel $\mathcal{I}(X)$. If $\mathcal{V}(I) = X$

> [!theorem|\*] Hilbert's Nullstellensatz
> For some affine variety $X$ the genrating ideal is a radical ideal $\mathcal{I}(X) = \sqrt{I}$ and we have an isomorphism
> $K[X] \cong \faktor{K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]}{\sqrt{I}}$

> [!definition|\*] Morphisms
> A map $\phi: X \to Y$ between affine varieties for $X \subset K^{n}$ $Y \subset K^{m}$ is called a morphism if it s given by polynomials. So $\phi(\xi_{1}, \ldots, \xi_{n}) = (f_{1}(\xi_{1}, \ldots, \xi_{n}), \ldots, f_{m}(\xi_{1}, \ldots, \xi_{n}))$ with $f_{i} \in K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]$
> From a given morphism we obtain a map
> $\phi^{*}: \K[Y] \to \K[X], \quad f \mapsto f \circ \phi$
> Explicitly if $J = \mathcal{I}(Y) \subset \K[y_{1}, \ldots, y_{m}]$  and $I = \mathcal{I}(X) \subset \K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]$ then
> $\phi^{*}(y_{i} + J) = f_{i} + I$
> we sometimes call $\phi^{*}$ the induced algebra homomorphism.
> Giving us a 1-to-1 correspondence between variety morphisms and algebra homomorphisms.

> [!definition|\*] Product varieties
> Let $X, Y$ be affine varieties $X \subset \K^{n}, Y \subset \K^{m}$ then
> $X \times Y \subset \K^{n + m}$
> If $I = \mathcal{I}(X)$ and $J = \mathcal{I}(Y)$ then
> $\mathcal{I}(X \times Y) = (I, J) \subset \K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}, y_{1}, \ldots, y_{m}]$
> where $(I, J) = (I \cap J)$ the generation of the union of the ideals.
> The coordinate Ring is then given by
> $\K[X \times Y] = \faktor{\K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}, y_{1}, \ldots, y_{m}]}{(I, J)} = \K[X] \otimes_{K} \K[Y] = \K[X] \otimes \K[Y]$

A note about tensor products that we should keep in mind is that in this situation we have a bilinear map
$\K[X] \times \K[Y] \to \K[X \times Y], \quad (f, g) \mapsto f \otimes g$
with $(f \otimes g)(x, y) = f(x) g(y)$
Elements from $\K[X] \otimes \K[Y]$ are finite sums of these pure tensors $f \otimes g$.

### End Recap

> [!definition|3.1] Linear Algebraic Group
> A linear (affine) algebraic group is an affine variety $G$ together with morphisms
>
> $$$\begin{align*}
> G \times G \to G, \quad &(\sigma, \tau) \mapsto \sigma \tau \\
> G \to G, \quad &\sigma \mapsto \sigma^{-1}
> \end{align*}$$
> $$$

> [!example|\*]
>
> 1. Let $\mathbb{G}_{a} = (\K, +)$ the additive group with mutliplication given by $(\eta, \xi) \mapsto \eta + \chi$ is a linear algebraic group.
> 2. $GL_{n}(\K)$ is a canonical linear algebraic group, we need a little trick though to prove that this is an affine variety as it is defined by an innequality. Namely we define $\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\K) = \set{(d, \xi)\in \K \times \K^{n \times n} \midline d \cdot \det(\xi) - 1 = 0}$
> 3. $SL_{n}, O_{n}, SO_{n}, \mathbb{G}_{m} = GL_{1}, \mathrm{diag}_{n} \subset GL_{n}$
> 4. Finite groups can be written as algebraic groups.

Note that the Unitary group falls out of this pattern as it requires complex conjugation to be defined.

We now wanto consider how these algebraic groups act on affine varieties.

> [!definition|3.2] Algebraic Group Actions
> Let $G$ be an affine algebraic group. A **$G$-variety** is an affine variety $X$ togehter with a morphism
> $G \times X \to X$
> defining a $G$-action.
> A **$G$-module** $V$ is a $G$-Variety that is a finite dimensional vector space with $G$ acting linearly on $V$ (i.e $\sigma(v + w) = \sigma(v) + \sigma(w)$ and $\sigma(av) = a \sigma(v)$)

Defining that a $G$-variety needs to be affine is a choice for brevity. One could consider our objects as affine $G$-varieties but as we will never consider non-affine varieties there is no use for this in our case.

> [!example|\*]
> $G$ acts on itself by $\sigma(\tau) = \sigma \tau$. Meaning $G$ is a $G$-Variety but not into a $G$-Module.

> [!remark|\*]
> Given a $G$-modulle $V$ we can choose a basis. Obtain from this a map
> $\rho: G \to GL_{n}(\K), \quad \sigma \mapsto T_{\sigma}$
> where $T_{\sigma}$ is the representation matrix of the map $v \mapsto \sigma(v)$. Then $\rho$ is a homomorphism of algebraic groups which is sometimes called a “rational representation of $G$”.

When we consider the general case of the invariant ring in our setting we can use the affine variety properties of $G$ and $X$ to express the induced map on coordinate rings explicitly.

Let us first express the action of $G$ by the map
$\mu: G \times X \to X$
we then know by the theory of [[Varieties]] that this induces a map on algebras
$\mu^{*}: \K[X] \to \K[G] \otimes \K[X], \quad \tilde{\mu}(f)(g \otimes x) = f(\mu(g, x)) = f(g \cdot x)$
this leads us to the following definition

> [!definition|\*] Coaction on G-Varieties
> Given a $G$-Variety $X$ with the action $\mu: G \times X \to X$ we define the coaction onto the Variety by the map
> $\mu^{*}: \K[X] \to \K[G] \otimes \K[X], \quad \mu^{*}(f)(g \otimes x) = f(\mu(g, x)) = f(g \cdot x)$
> The action on a regular function can be expressed by this coaction in the following way
> $\sigma^{-1} f (x) = f(\sigma(x)) = \mu^{*}(f)(\sigma \otimes x)$
> for $f \in \K[X], x \in X, \sigma \in G$

Sometimes for the sake of computation we assume that we can express an element
$\mu^{*}(f) = \sum\limits_{i=0}^{n} g_{i} \otimes f_{i}$
for $g_{i} \in K[G]$ and $f_{i} \in \K[X]$.

This allows us to express invariance of $f$ in terms of the coaction

> [!proposition|\*] Characterisation of Invariance by Coaction
> In the above situation we have that
> $K[X]^{G} = \set{f \in \K[X] \midline \mu^{*}(f) = 1 \otimes f}$

`\begin{proof}`
Let $f \in \set{f \in \K[X] \midline \mu^{*}(f) = 1 \otimes f}$  meaning $\mu^{*}(f) = 1 \otimes f$ then for some $\sigma \in G, x \in X$ we have
$\sigma^{-1} f(x) =\mu^{*}(f)(\sigma, x) = (1 \otimes f) (\sigma, x) = 1 (\sigma) f(x) = f(x)$
proving the reverse inclu
Now for the inclusion consider that we choose a basis $B$ of $K[G]$ with $1 \in B$. Now let $f \in K[X]^{G}$ then we can write
$\mu^{*}(f) = \sum\limits_{g \in B} g \otimes f_{g}$
with $f_{g} \neq 0$ only finitely often. Then for $\sigma \in G$ and $x \in X$ we get
$f(x) = f(\sigma(x)) = \sum\limits_{y \in B} g(\sigma)f_{g}(x)$
meaning for all $x \in X$
$f (x) = \sum\limits_{g \in B} g \cdot f_{g}(x)$
since these are two representations of the same function we can use the fact that $B$ is a basis to see that
$f_{1}(x) = f(x), \quad f_{i}(x) = 0, i \neq 0$
`\end{proof}`

Now we consider the composition
$G \times X \overset{(\sigma, x) \mapsto (\sigma^{-1}, x)}{\longrightarrow} G \times X \overset{\mu}{\longrightarrow} X$
with
$(\sigma, x) \mapsto \sigma^{-1}(x)$
then the induced morphism
$\tilde{\mu}(f) = \sum\limits_{i} g_{i} f_{i}$
so for all $\sigma \in G, x \in X$ we can express
$\sigma(f)(x) = f(\sigma^{-1}(x)) = \sum\limits_{i} g_{i}(\sigma) f_{i}(x) \implies \sigma(f) = \sum\limits_{i} g_{i}(\sigma) f_{i}$

> [!definition|3.4] Locally Finite Vector Spaces
> Let $G$ be a group acting on a vector space $V$. We call $V$ **locally finite** if
> $\forall v \in V: \dim_{V}\left<G(v)\right>_{K} < \infty$
> meaning the $K$-span of our orbit is finite dimensional as a vector space.

We now turn the observations from before into a generalised hypothesis

> [!theorem|3.5] Defined Coactions give Local Finiteness
> Let $G$ be an affine algebraic group acting linearly on a vector space (not necessarily finite dimensional) $V$ such that
> $\exists \tilde{\mu}: V \to K[G] \otimes V$
> such that $\tilde{\mu}(v) = \sum\limits_{i} g_{i} \otimes v_{i}$ then
> $\sigma(v) = \sum\limits_{i} g_{i}(\sigma) v_{i}$
> Given this we get that
>
> 1. $V$ is locally finite
> 2. Every finite dimenisonal $G$-Stable subspace $W \in V$ is a $G$-Module

`\begin{proof}`
To prove the first property consider $v \in V$ and write $\tilde{\mu}(v) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} g_{i} \otimes v_{i} \implies G(v) \subset \left<v_{1}, \ldots, v_{n}\right>_{K}$ which is finite dimensional meaning $\left<G(v)\right>_{K}$ is finite dimensional too.

Now consider a finite dimesional $G$-Stable subspace $W \subset V$ and consider a basis $\set{w_{1}, \ldots, w_{n}}$ of $W$ and extend to a basis
$\set{w_{1}, \ldots, w_{n}} \cup B$
of $V$. Now we write
$\tilde{\mu} (w_{i}) = \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n} g_{ji} \otimes w_{j} + \sum\limits_{v \in B} g_{v, i} \otimes v$
this gives us that applying a group element
$\sigma(w_{i}) = \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n} g_{ji}(\sigma) w_{j} + \sum\limits_{v \in B} g_{v j}(\sigma)$
Since this is simply just all in $W$ we know that $g_{v,i}(\sigma) = 0$ for all $v$, meaning that for all $i$ we get
$g_{v, i} = 0$
This gives us
$\tilde{\mu}(w_{j}) = \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n} g_{ji} \otimes w_{j}$ and
$\sigma(w_{i}) = \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n} g_{ji}(\sigma) w_{j}$
which means that $(g_{ij}(\sigma))_{ij = 1}^{n}$ is the representation matrix of the action $w \mapsto \sigma(w)$. We thus obtain
$\rho: G \to \mathrm{GL}_{n}(\K), \quad \sigma \mapsto (g_{ij}(\sigma))_{ij = 1}^{n}$ a homomorphism of algebraic groups and thus $W$ is actually a $G$-Module with
$(\sigma, w_{i}) \mapsto \sum\limits_{j} g_{ji}(\sigma) w_{j}$
defining the action
`\end{proof}`

> [!corollary|3.6] Coordinate Ring of $G$-Varieties are locally finite
> For $G$ an affine algebraic group, $X$ a $G$-Variety. Then $K[X]$ is locall finite and a union of $G$-Modules

Let us now consider $V$ a $G$-Module. Choose a basis of $V$, called $B = \set{v_{1}, \ldots, v_{n}}$ and obtain a rational representation
$\rho: G \to \mathrm{GL}_{n}(\K), \sigma \mapsto (g_{ij}(\sigma))_{ij =1}^{n}$
Then the $G$-Action is given by
$\tilde{\phi}: V \to K[G] \otimes V, \quad v_{i} \mapsto \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n} g_{ji}(\sigma) v_{j}$

> [!corollary|\*]
> Let $V$ be a $G$-Module, $W \subset V$ a subspace such that $G(W) \subset W$. Then $W$ is a $G$-Module

`\begin{proof}`
This is given by Theorem 3.5 (b).
`\end{proof}`
