We now go to the question of orbit seperation. For this let us set up the setting:

- Let $\K = \overline{\K}$
- $G$ affine algebric group
- $X$ group variety
- Assume $\K[X]^{G}$ is finitely generated, so $\K[X]^{G} = \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}]$.

In general let $I$ be the kernel of
$\K[T_{1}, \ldots, T_{n}] \mapsto \K[X]^{G}, \quad T_{i} \mapsto f_{i}$
which represents a relation between the generating invariants $f_{i}$. This gives us a so-called presentation of the [[Invariant Ring|invariant ring]]
$\K[X]^{G} \cong \faktor{\K[T_{1}, \ldots, T_{n}]}{I}$
where $I$ is the relation ideal in $\K[T_{1}, \ldots, T_{n}]$.  Set $Y := \mathcal{V}(I)$ an [[Affine Varieties|affine variety]] in $\K^{n}$, and we will obtain a morphism
$\pi: X \to Y, \quad x \mapsto (f_{1}(x), \ldots, f_{n}(x))$
which is constant on orbits. Sometimes this setup is called a “quotient”

> [!remark|\*] Scheme Theoretic Version of this Setup
> The inclusion of $\K[X]^{G} \subset \K[X]$ induces a morphism
> $\tilde{\pi}: \tilde{X} \to \tilde{Y}, \quad \mathfrak{y} \mapsto \K[X]^{G} \cap \mathfrak{y}$
> where $\tilde{X} = \spec(\K[X]), \tilde{Y} = \spec(\K[X]^{G})$
>
> This is simpler in its definition but we will remain with the affine variety version of this setup

> [!theorem|6.1] Linearly Reductive Groups have surjective Quotients
> If $G$ is linearly reductive then $\pi$ is a surjective morphism.

`\begin{proof}`
Let $(\eta_{1}, \ldots, \eta_{n}) \in Y$. Consider the ideal
$\mathfrak{J} = (f_{1} - \eta_{1}, \ldots, f_{n} - \eta_{n}) \subset \K[X]$

> [!claim|\*]
> $\mathfrak{J} \neq \K[X]$
> meaning this is a proper ideal

Assume for a contradiction that $\mathfrak{J} = \K[X]$. Then we know that $1 \in \mathfrak{J}$ and that we can write
$1 = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} g_{i} (f_{i} - \eta_{i})$
Using the reynolds operator and the linearity for invariants we get that
$1 = R(1) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} R(g_{i}) (f_{i} - \eta_{i})$
where $R(g_{i}) \in \K[X]^{G}$.  We may write $R(g_{i}) = G_{i}(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n})$. This means that our above equation turns into
$1 = \sum\limits_{i} G_{i}(T_{i} - \eta_{i})$ is a relation between the invariants and thus $\sum\limits_{i} G_{i}(T_{i} - \eta_{i}) - 1 \in I$ where $I$ is the relation ideal. Evaluating at the point $(\eta_{1}, \ldots, \eta_{n}) \in Y = \mathcal{V} (I)$ gives
$0 = \sum\limits_{i} G_{i}(\eta_{i} - \eta_{i}) - 1  = 0 - 1 = -1$
which is a contradiction

Given this claim we can apply hilberts nullstellensatz to the idela corresponding to $\mathfrak{J}$ insider $\K[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{m}]$. The nullstellensatz tells us that the ideal corresponds to a point $\mathcal{V}_{X}(\mathfrak{J})$ as a variety in $X$ given by $\mathfrak{J}$. This then tells us that
$f_{i}(x) = \eta_{i}$
which would mean that $(\eta_{1}, \ldots, \eta_{n}) \in \im(\pi)$

`\end{proof}`

> [!remark|\*]
> A ring inclusion $A \subset B$ induces a morphism $\spec(B) \to \spec(A)$ which is always dominant, i.e, its image is dense. This  tells us that $\pi$ is always dominant, meaning always close to surjectivity. In the linear reductive case we get full surjectivity.

## Orbit Seperation

To get at orbit speration we first need a technical nice Lemma:

> [!lemma|6.2] Technical Lemma
> Let $G$ be linearly reductive and let $X_{1}, X_{2} \subset X$ be closed and $G$-Stable, then
> $\overline{\pi(X_{1} \cap X_{2})} = \overline{\pi(X_{1})} \cap \overline{\pi(X_{2})}$

`\begin{proof}`
First we show the left inclusion.  The following inclusions simply follow
$\pi(X_{1} \cap X_{2}) \subset \pi(X_{1}) \cap \pi(X_{2}) \subset \overline{\pi(X_{1})} \cap \overline{\pi(X_{2})}$
Now taking the closure gives us the inlcusion

For the right inlcusion consider the following claim

> [!claim|\*]
> $\mathcal{I}(\pi(X_{1} \cap X_{2})) \subset \mathcal{I}(\overline{\pi(X_{1})} \cap \overline{\pi(X_{2})})$

Let $F \in \mathcal{I}(\pi(X_{1} \cap X_{2}))$ then we know that $\forall x \in X_{1} \cap X_{2}$ that
$0 = F(\pi(x)) = F(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n})(x)$
Thus $F(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}) \in \mathcal{I}(X_{1} \cap X_{2})$. By algebraic geometry we know that
$\mathcal{I}(X_{1} \cap X_{2}) = \sqrt{\mathcal{I}(X_{1}) + \mathcal{I}(X_{2})}$
which means that there exists a $k$ such that
$F(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n})^{k} = g_{1} + g_{2} \quad g_{i} \in \mathcal{I}(X_{i})$
Seeing that the left is invariant and the right isnt aproi, we apply the reynolds operator. Note that due to $G$-Stability of $G_{i}$ that $R(\mathcal{I}(X_{i})) \subset \mathcal{I}(X_{i})$ giving us
$F(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n})^{k} = R(g_{1}) + R(g_{2})$
with $R(g_{i}) = G_{i}(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n})$ with $G_{i} \in \K[T_{1}, \ldots, T_{n}]$.
Now consider that
$F(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n})^{k} - G_{1}(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}) - G_{2}(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}) = 0$
giving us that $H := F^{k} - G_{1} - G_{2} \in I$ the relation ideal.

Now let $y \in \overline{\pi(X_{1})} \cap \overline{\pi(X_{2})}$. We know that $G_{i}(f_1, \ldots, f_{n}) \in \mathcal{I}(X_{i})$ thus
$\forall x \in X_{i}: 0 = G_{i}(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n})(x) = G_{i}(\pi(x))$
meaning that $G_{i} \in \mathcal{I}(\pi(X_{i})) = \mathcal{I}(\overline{\pi(X_{i})})$. For
$G_{1}(y) = G_{2}(y) = 0$
Plugging thi into $H$ we get that
$F(y)^{k} - G_{1}(y) - G_{2}(y) - H(y) = 0$
since $y \in Y = \mathcal{V}(I)$ thus $F(y) = 0$ and thus $F \in \mathcal{I}(\overline{\pi(X_{1})} \cap \overline{\pi(X_{2})})$.

Given this claim then $\overline{\pi(X_{1})} \cap \overline{\pi(X_{2})} = \mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}(\overline{\pi(X_{1})} \cap \overline{\pi(X_{2})}))$ since it is closed and with the claim we pass to a smaller ideal meaning we enlarge the variety and thus
$\overline{\pi(X_{1})} \cap \overline{\pi(X_{2})} \subset \mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}(\pi(X_{1} \cap X_{2}))) = \overline{\pi(X_{1} \cap X_{2})}$
`\end{proof}`

> [!theorem|6.3] Characterisation of Orbit Closures in Linearly Reductive Groups
> Let $G$ be linearly reductive and $X$ a $G$-Variety and let $x, y \in X$. Then
> $\pi(x) = \pi(y) \iff \overline{G(x)} \cap \overline{G(y)} \neq \emptyset$

Note that $\pi(x) = \pi(y)$ means that all invariants agree on these two points

`\begin{proof}`
$\Leftarrow$
Note that $\pi$ is constant on orbits and continous. This means that $\pi$ is also constant on the orbit closures. Thus if the orbit closures meet it means that $\pi$ is constant on the union. and thus its equal.

$\Rightarrow$
Let $X_{1} = \overline{G(x)}, X_{2} = \overline{G(y)}$ be two $G$-Stable spaces. Now using the previous lemma we know that.
$\pi(x) = \pi(y) \implies \pi(X_{1}) \cap \pi(X_{2}) \neq \emptyset \implies \overline{\pi(X_{1}) \cap \pi(X_{2})} \neq \emptyset$
by the lemma this implies
$\overline{\pi(X_{1})} \cap \overline{\pi(X_{2})} \neq \emptyset$
`\end{proof}`

> [!lemma|6.4]
> Situation as in the previous theorem. Let $X_{1} \subset X$ be closed $G$-Stable, then
> $\pi(X_{1}) \subset Y$
> is closed

`\begin{proof}`
Let $y \in \overline{\pi(X_{1})}$. Set $X_{2} = \pi^{-1}(\set{y})$, which is certainly closed and $G$-Stable. By 6.1 the surjectivity of $\pi$ is given by linear reductivity and thus there exists $x$ such that $\pi(x) = y \implies x \in X_{2}$.

We then get that $y \in \pi(X_{2}) \subset \overline{\pi(X_{2})} \implies y \in \overline{\pi(X_{2})} \cap \overline{\pi(X_{1})}$. By our lemma we get that
there exsits some $x' \in X_{1} \cap X_{2} \implies \pi(x') = y$. Giving us that $y \in \overline{\pi(X_{1})}$.
`\end{proof}`

> [!corollary|6.5]
> The topology on $Y$ is the quotient topology given by $\pi$. This means that
> $Z \subset Y \text{ is closed} \iff \pi^{-1}(Z) \subset X \text{ closed}$

`\begin{proof}`
The direction from left to right is given by the continuity of $\pi$. Now let us assume that $\pi^{-1}(Z) \subset X$ closed. We can then see that $\pi^{-1}(Z)$ is $G$-Stable by definition. Now by Lemma 6.4 we get that $\pi(\pi^{-1}(Z)) \subset Y$ is closed. Now finally note that since $\pi$ is surjective we know that
$\pi(\pi^{-1}(Z)) = Z$
`\end{proof}`

### Generalisation to Reductive case and more nice properties

> [!remark|\*] Generalisation to Reductivity
> All these results extend to (geometrically) reductive groups. In fact the criterion for orbit seperation characterises reductive groups. In fact let $G$ be linear algebraic group with the property from Theorem 6.3 and let $V$ be a $G$-Module and let $v \neq 0$ some vector with $v \in V^{G}$. Then we get that $\overline{G(v)} = \set{v}, \overline{G(0)} = \set{0}$ and thus $\pi(v) = \pi(0)$ which means there exists $f_{i} \in \K[V]^{G}$ such that $f_{i}(v) = f_{i}(0)$. Set $\tilde{f_{i}} = f_{i} - f_{i}(0) \in \K[V]^{G}_{+}$ and $\tilde{f_{i}}(v) \neq 0$ so $G$ is reductive.

Let $G$ be reductive. We now ask the question when can all orbits be separated by invariants? By theorem 6.3 this happens exactly if all orbits are closed, which via algebraic geometry corresponds to the fact that all orbits have the same dimension.

> [!example|\*]
> Let $V$ be a $G$ module, then all orbits can be seperated if and only if all orbits are $0$ dimensional meaning finite, since we always have the closed orbit $\set{0}$. This again is equivalent to the connected component of $G$ acting trivially.
> In this sense $G$ acts as a finite group and this logic tells us that only finite groups have perfect orbit seperation.

Another consideration is given in the following:
Let $G$ be a reductive group. We then consider the fibres $\pi^{-1}(\set{y})$ and note that all these contain exactly one closed orbit (this can be proven). This is due to the fact that the fibre is always closed and $G$-Stable and thus must be a union of orbits but we can in fact show that this is also only one . Also the fibre is in the closure of every other orbit in the fibre.

# Categorical Quotients

From algebraic geometry we take the definition of a [[Varieties|variety]] as a topological space locally covered by [[Affine Varieties|affine varieties]].

We now consider some important types of varieties:

- Affine Varieties
- Quasi-Affine Varieties: Open in an affine variety (think of a punctured plane for example)
- Projective Varieties: Subsets of of $\mathbb{P}^{n}$ given by homogeneous polynomials
- Quasi-Projective Varieties: Open in a projective variety

These concepts are nested in each other in an intuitive way. Here we can also consider algebraic groups $G$ which in this context are simply varieties that are also groups.

> [!definition|6.6] Categorical Quotient
> Let $G$ be an algebraic group acting on a variety by a morphism $G \times X \to X$. A **categorical quotient (c.q.)** is a variety $Y$ togehter with a morphism $\pi: X \to Y$
> such that
>
> 1. $\pi$ is constant on orbits, meaning you have commutation of the action composed with $\pi$ and the projection of the product composed with $\pi$
> 2. For a variety $Z$ with a morphism $\phi: X \to Z$ that is consant on orbits  there exists exists a unique morphism $\psi: Y \to Z$ such that $\psi \circ \pi = \phi$

If a categorical quotient exists it is unique up to isomorphism. Indeed, if $\pi':X \to Y$ is another categorical quotient we obtain
$ \begin{CD} Y @<\pi<< X @>\pi>> Y\\ @V\psi VV @V\pi 'VV @VV\psi'V \\  Y' @>id>> Y' @<id<<Y'\end{CD}$
which by the uniqueness of the categorical quotient gives us that $\psi$ is an isomorphism.

The notation for the categorical quotient is $Y = \dfaktor{X}{Y}$

# Actions on $\mathbb{P}^{n}$

Let $G$ be a reductive group and $V$ a $G$-Module. We can consider the action on $\P(V) = \P^{n}$ the projective space if $\dim(V) = n + 1$.

**What can we say about this?**
Due to the reductivity of $G$ we know that $\K[V]^G = \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{m}]$ , with $f_{i}$ homogeneous. For $d_{i} = \deg(f_{i})$ and $I$ the relation ideal weighted homogenous (meaning the grading on $I$ is weighted which is different to the usual grading) we define
$\P(d_{1}, \ldots, d_{m})$
called the **weighted projective space**. We should think of this space as a space of points where points are identified if
$a \sim b \iff a_{i}^{d_{i}} = b_{i}$
This space is embedded in the usual projektive $k$ by the _vernonese embedding_.

Now form $Y := \mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{P}(d_{1}, \ldots, d_{m})}(I)$ a projective variety. In scheme theoretic language we call this sussinctly
$Y := \mathrm{Proj}(\K[V]^{G})$

We now aim to construct a map $\P(V) \to Y$ via
$x \mapsto (f_{1} (x): \ldots : f_{m}(x))$
For this map we need $f_{i}(x) \neq 0$ for some $i$. This needs to be caputred in a definition.

> [!definition|\*] Semi-Stable Points
> A point $x \in V$ is called semi-stable if $\exists f \in \K[V]^{G}_{+}$ such that
> $f(v) \neq 0$

From this we obtain a well-defined map
$\pi: \tilde{X} \to Y, \quad x \mapsto (f_{1} (x): \ldots : f_{m}(x))$
where $\tilde{X}$ is the set of semi-stable points.

It turns out that when $G$ is reductive the map $\pi$ is a categorical quotient due to Mumford.

### Orbit Stabilization in the full contex of categorical quotients

> [!definition|6.8] Geometric Quotient
> Let $G$ be an algebraic group acting morphically on a variety $X$. A varitey $Y$ togehter with a morphism $\pi: X \to Y$ is called a geometric quotient if
>
> 1. The fibers of $\pi$ are $G$-Orbits (this is equivalent to stating that fibres Orbits and that it is constant on orbits)
> 2. $\pi$ is surjective and an open map
> 3. For every open subset $U \subset Y$ the map $\pi_{U}^{*}: \K[U] \to \K[\pi^{-1}(U)]$ has the image $\K[\pi^{-1}(U)]^{G}$

Condition 3. seems to require an additional explanation. First note that $\K[U]$ is the [[Coordinate Ring|ring of regular functions]] on $U$ meaining locally (on parts of an affine covering) any function $f \in \K[U]$ are given by polynomials  and the map $\pi_{U}^{*}$ acts as the induced morphism restricted to these. From this we can see that $\im \pi_{U}^{*} \subset \K[\pi^{-1}(U)]^{G}$ since $\pi$ is constant on orbits and $\pi_{U}^{*}$ precomposes with it. If $\pi$ is surjective we know that the induced morphism $\pi_{U}^{*}$ injective. More on this considition will be considered later.

Condition 2. also has the additional property of being open which we also need to bring into context. Assume that 1. is given and $\pi$ is surjective. Then we can actually show that $\pi$ automatically must then be an open map.

`\begin{proof}`
Let $U \subset X$ be open and assume the assertion of Lemma 6.4, meaning we assume that for cloesed $G$-Stable subsets the image is again closed. Then consider $\pi^{-1}(\pi(U))$ and by $1.$ this must be a union of $G$-Orbits since
$\pi^{-1}(\pi(U)) = \bigcup_{x \in \pi(U)}\pi^{-1}(\set{x}) = \bigcup_{x \in \pi(U)} G(x)$
this is a union of open sets??.
We then get that $X \setminus \pi^{-1}(\pi(U)) = \pi^{-1}(Y \setminus \pi(U))$ is closed and $G$-Stable. By our second assumtion we know that
$\pi\left(\pi^{-1}(Y \setminus \pi(U)))\right)$
is also closed. By surjectivity this means that $Y \setminus \pi(U)$ is closed.
`\end{proof}`

This means that in the context of reductive groups the extra assertion of openness is automatically given since for these we have lemma 6.4

This definition is very subtle and not super transparent. We will try to “defang” it slightly though by using the following proposition.

> [!proposition|6.9] Simplicity of Third Condition of Geometric Quotients
> Let $G$ be an algebraic group and $X$ be irreducible (affine) $G$-Variety and $\K[X]^{G} = \K[f_{1}, \ldots, f_{m}]$. Set $Y = \mathcal{V}(I) \subset \K^{m}$ with $I$ the relation ideal. Then
> $\pi: X \to Y$
> defined in the begginning of the chapter satisfies the third condition of the previous definition

`\begin{proof}`
Let $U \subset Y$ be open. In this context this gives us that $U = Y \setminus \mathcal{V}_{Y}(G_{1}, \ldots, G_{r})$ with $G_{i} \in \K[Y]$. Now set $g_{i} = G_{i}(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{m}) = G_{i} \circ \pi$. This gives us that
$\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{V}_{Y}(G_{1}, \ldots, G_{r})) = \mathcal{V}_{X}(g_{1}, \ldots, g_{r})$
Now consider carefully $\pi^{-1}(U) = X \setminus \mathcal{V}_{X}(g_{1}, \ldots, g_{m}) = \bigcup_{i=1}^{r} (X \setminus \mathcal{V}_{X}(g_{i}))$which is an open affine covering.
By definition we know that $g_{i} \in \K[X]^{G}$. Now let $h \in \K[\pi^{-1}(U)]^{G}$, we can see that by definition this means we can decompose into
$h_{i} = \at{h}{X \setminus \mathcal{V}_{X}(g_{i})} \text{ regular}$
since $X$ is irreducible we have that $h_{i} \in \K[X][g^{-1}_{i}] \subset \mathrm{Quot}(\K[X])$ and that $h_{i}$ is $G$-Invariant meaning in particular (take this on faith)
$h_{i} \in \K[X]^{G}[g^{-1}_{i}] \subset \mathrm{Quot}(\K[X])$
this gives us that there exists $H_{i} \subset \K[Y][G_{i}]$ such that $h_{i} = H_{i}(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{m}) = H_{i} \circ \pi$
We now want to glue these together. Thus consider $U_{i} := Y \setminus \mathcal{V}_{Y}(G_{i})$ and that $H_{i}$ is a map on $U_{i} \subset U$.

Let $i, j \in \set{1, \ldots, r}$, and consider the overlaps
$\at{H_{i}}{U_{i} \cap U_{j}} \circ \pi = \at{h_{i}}{\pi^{-1}(U_{i} \cap U_{j})} = \at{h}{\pi^{-1}(U_{i} \cap U_{j})} = \at{h_{j}}{\pi^{-1}(U_{i} \cap U_{j})} = \at{H_{j}}{U_{i} \cap U_{j}} \circ \pi$
since $\pi$ is dominant we actually get that the functions $H_{i}$ agree on intersections
$\at{H_{i}}{U_{i} \cap U_{j}} = \at{H_{j}}{U_{i} \cap U_{j}}$
By gluing there thus exists a $H \in \K[U]$ such that $\at{H}{U_{i}} = H_{i}$. For this $H$ we get that
$\pi_{U}^{*} (H) = h$
by construction and restriction to each affine component.
`\end{proof}`

> [!corollary|6.10]
> Let $G$ be a reductive group and $X$ an irreducible $G$-Variety. Then $\pi: X \to \dfaktor{X}{G}$ is a geometric quotient if and only if all orbits are closed.

`\begin{proof}`
The first property of our geometric quotient is given as an if andonly if by closed orbits and theorem 6.3. If $1$ is tr then $2$ follows and $3$ follows from the previous proposition.
`\end{proof}`

The previous corollary is also true for $X$ not irreducible, but we wont show this here.

The seeming obsession with the third conition will be warranted in the following example, since we will see that the third property is fundamentally separate from the others.

> [!example|\*]
> Consider the trivial group $G = \set{id}$ and $X = \A^{1}$ and $Y = \A^{1}$. We can then define
> $\pi: \A^{1} \to \A^{1}, \quad \xi \mapsto \xi^{p}$
> where $p = \mathrm{char}(\K) \neq 0$ then we can see that $\pi$ is a open and closed bijective map whose fibers are orbits. This gives us that the first and second condition are satisfied.
> If we want to understand the third condition in this case consider $U = X$ which is open, then
> $\pi^{*}: \K[x] \to \K[x], \quad x \mapsto x^{p}$
> as $\K[U] = \K[X] = \K[Y] \cong \K[x]$.
> We can see though that $\im(\pi^{*}) = \K[x^{p}] \neq \K[x] = \K[X]^{G}$ and thus $3.$ does not hold.
> The finite characteristic assumtion is not necessary here as we can construct a very similar example in infinite characteristic.

> [!theorem|6.12] Mumford
> A geometric quotient is a categorical quotient.

`\begin{proof}`
No proof.
`\end{proof}`

This means that a geometric quotient inherits the uniqueness of the categorical one. For a given situation there can thus be at most one geometric quotient. If $\dfaktor{X}{G}$ is not geometric, there exists no geometric quotient. For this unique quotient we now simply denote the geometric quotient as $\faktor{X}{G}$

> [!proposition|6.13]
> Let $\pi: X \to Y$ be a geometric quotient and let $U \subset X$ be a $G$-Stable open subset. Then $\at{\pi}{U} : U \to \pi(U)$ is a geometric quotient.

`\begin{proof}`
Properties $1$ and $2$ pass down to $\at{\pi}{U}$. For the this property consider $V \subset \pi(U)$ open, then we know
$\pi^{-1}(V) \subset \pi^{-1}(\pi(U)) = \bigcup_{\sigma \in G} \sigma(U) = U$
Using this we consider
$\at{\pi^{-1}}{U}(V) = \pi^{-1}(V)$
using this we now can consider the induces maps
$\at{\pi^{*}}{U} = \pi^{*}(V)$
giving us that property $3$ also passes down from $\pi$.
`\end{proof}`

> [!theorem|6.14] Rosenlicht
> Let $G$ be an affine algebraic group acting morphically on an irreducible variety $X$. Then $X$ has an open $G$-Stable subset $\emptyset \neq U \subset X$ such that
> $\faktor{U}{G}$
> exists

`\begin{proof}`
no proof
`\end{proof}`

> [!example|6.15]
> To see this theorem in action let us now consider $\mathbb{G}_{a}$ acting on $\K^{2}$ with weight $(1, 1)$. Then we know that $\K[V] = \K[x, y]$ and $\K[V]^{G} = \K$.
> Consider now $U = \set{\begin{pmatrix}\xi \\ \eta\end{pmatrix} \in V \midline \eta \neq 0}$ and from this obtain

## Rational Invariants

In the statemnt of rosenlicht the assumtion of irreducibility is obvious. This comes from the theory of rational invariants which we will briefly dive into here.

Let $X$ be an irreducible variety. A rational function on $X$ is given by a pair $(U, f)$ where $\emptyset \neq U \subset X$ open and $f \in \K[U]$. We call two functions equivalent if
$(U, f) \sim (V, g) \iff \at{f}{U \cap V} = \at{g}{U \cap V}$
We now define the rational function field $\K(X)$ as the set of equivalence classes under this equivalence relation. The reason its called a field is that every non-zero function can be inverted on some open subset

If $G$ acts on $X$ is also acts on $\K(X)$ by
$\sigma(U, f) := (\sigma(U), \at{f \circ \sigma^{-1}}{\sigma(U)})$

> [!theorem|6.16]
> Let $Y = \faktor{X}{G}$ be a geometric quotient and $X$ irreducible. Then $\K(X)^{G} = \K(Y)$ with the natural isomorphism of composing with $\pi: X \to Y$
