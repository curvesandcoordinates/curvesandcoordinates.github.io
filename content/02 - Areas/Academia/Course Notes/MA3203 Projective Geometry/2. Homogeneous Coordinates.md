# Homogeneous Coordinates

Now we want to work on making fields into projective spaces via a constructions. So let $\mathbb{K}$ be a field.

> [!definition] 2 Dimensional Projective Space over $\mathbb{K}$
> We define the set
> $\mathcal{P}_\mathbb{K} = \faktor{\mathbb{K^{3}}\setminus\set{0}}{\mathbb{K^{}}\setminus\set{0}}$
> and similarly
> $\mathcal{L}_\mathbb{K} = \faktor{\mathbb{K^{3}}\setminus\set{0}}{\mathbb{K^{}}\setminus\set{0}}$
> We also define the incidence relation as
> $[p] \mathcal{I}_\mathbb{K} [l] \iff \innprod{p}{l} = 0$
> Then the tuple $(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{K}}, \mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{K}}, \mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{K}})$ is called the Projective Plane over $\mathbb{K}$

> [!theorem|3.1]
> The tuple $(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{K}}, \mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{K}}, \mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{K}})$ is infact a projective plane by our definition

`\begin{proof}`
We simply have to show the axioms:

For A1 consider $[p], [q] \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{K}}$ and $[p] \neq [q]$ then we know that this means $p \neq \lambda q$ for any $\lambda \in \mathbb{K}$. This in turn means that the matrix equation
$\begin{pmatrix}p_{1} & p_{2} & p_{3} \\ q_{1} & q_{2} & q_{3}\end{pmatrix} \cdot l = 0$
has a $1$ dimensional solution space which means in equivalence classes it has one unique solution $[l]$

For A2 we simply apply a symmetric argument.

For A3 consider simply the vectors
$\begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}$
`\end{proof}`

## The Fano Plane Revisited

To construct the Fano plane using the above construction we consider $\mathbb{K} = \set{0, 1}$ all calculations $\mod 2$. Then all possible vectors are given by
$\begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}$

## The Real Projective Plane

We take our definition and simply set $\mathbb{K} = \R$.

Why should we even consider the real numbers?

1. It aids visualization and imagination
2. They - to some extent - model things in the “real world”
3. $\R$ also has very good properties compared to other fields

Explicitly in the real projective plane our structure then looks as follows:

- We denote a point by its equivalence class $p = \left[ \begin{pmatrix}x  \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix} \right]$
- A line is denoted by the equivalence class $l = \left[ \begin{pmatrix}a  \\ b \\ c\end{pmatrix} \right]$
- We say that $[p] \mathcal{I}_{\R} [l] : \iff \innprod{p}{l} = 0 \iff ax +yb + zc = 0$

To get to the image of an actual plane we first note that the equivalence classes gives us a degree of freedom to choose one of the coordinates and the other two will by scaling still define the same point, meaning if we choose say, $z' = \alpha z$ that

$\left[ \begin{pmatrix}x  \\ y \\ z'\end{pmatrix} \right] = \left[ \begin{pmatrix}x  \\ y \\ \alpha z\end{pmatrix} \right]= \left[ \begin{pmatrix}\frac{x}{\alpha}  \\ \frac{y}{\alpha} \\ z\end{pmatrix} \right]$
Thus simply choosing $\alpha = \frac{1}{z}$ gives us the standard embedding mapping any equivalence class to a point with only $x$ and $y$ coordinates.

$\begin{pmatrix}x  \\  y\end{pmatrix} \mapsto \left[ \begin{pmatrix}x  \\ y \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \right]$
This simplification gives us that we can describe all the “finite” points as the ones whose last entry is $1$. These points can be normally drawn in the plane and be described by an $x$ and a $y$ coordinate. In this construction the only points we miss out are those whose $z$-coordinate is $0$ which we will mentally ad to the picture as some sort of horizon-points and call them the “points at infinity”.

### Join and Meet Operations

We now want to explicitly describe how we can do our two operations given by our projective axioms. We want to be able to join two points and meet two lines.

For points, consider the fact that given $p, p' \in \mathbb{R}P^{2}$ we know that any line $l$ between them must be incident to both, thus we want the conditions
$\innprod{p}{l} = 0 = \innprod{p'}{l}$
If we consider this before moving into equivalence classes we are essentially looking for a vector $l \in \R^{3}$ such that it is orthogonal to two given vectors $p$ and $p'$. This sounds like the cross-product. So we will define an operation
$join(p, p') = p \vee p' := p \times p'$

Similarly looking at the definition of the meet of two given lines $l, l' \in \mathbb{R}P^{2}$ gets us to the same condition meaning we can also define
$meet(l, l') = l \wedge l' := l \times l'$

### Other Operations

We can in a similar way describe that determining if three points $p, q, r \in \mathbb{R}P^{2}$ are collinear amounts to finding one vector $l$ that is orthogonal to these three as vectors in $\R^{3}$. For this consider the matrix
$M = \begin{pmatrix}\vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ p & q & r  \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\end{pmatrix}$
We notice that finding $l$ can only be possible if the vectors $p, q, r$ do not span the entire space. If they dont we can simply consider their span, which will be a plane on which we can easily find an orthogonal vector. Thus the linear dependence of the vectors is crucial. This is encoded in the matrix via its determinant being $0$. We can thus define
$collinear(p, q, r) \iff \det(M) = \det \begin{pmatrix}\vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ p & q & r  \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\end{pmatrix} = 0$

The dual statement here is not often used but also possible. We know that dually given three lines $l, m, n$ we know that they intersect in a single point if and only if $\det(l, m, n) = 0$

### A Typical calculation and Trick

Given four points $A, B, C, D$ and two lines $l = join(A, B)$ and $m = join(C, D)$ we would like to compute the intersection of $l$ and $m$ called $P$. If we write this out algebraically we get that
$P = (A \times B) \times (C \times D) = [A, B, C] D - [A, B, D] C$
where $[X, Y, Z] = \det(X, Y, Z)$.
The last equality is simply a trick that one can use when calculating cross products. To see its validity just check the colinearity of the pair $(A, B, P)$ and note that since $P = \lambda_{1} D + \lambda_{2} C$ that it must lay on the line connecting $C$ and $D$.

### Parallel Lines meet at Infinite Points

Computing the above construction with only two points $A, B$ and a given direction vector $R$ where
$A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{x} \\ a_{y} \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} b_{x} \\ b_{y} \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \quad R = \begin{pmatrix} r_{x} \\ r_{y} \\ 0\end{pmatrix}$
We can pick as $C = A + R$ and $D = B + R$. Then intuitively we would call the lines $l, m$ parallel. These must meet in projective space though and their meet is exactly the point
$P = [RAB] R - [RAR] B = [RAB] R = \lambda R \implies [P] = [R]$
Thus since multiples are identified we know that parallel lines meet exactly at the point decribed by the coordinates of $R$.

### Calculatin a Parallel

A further freedom that we can do in projective space is that given a line $l$ and a point $P$ we can easily calculate a parallel to $l$ going through $P$. This is done by remembering that we have a canonical “line at infitnity” consisting of all the points of the form
$P = \begin{pmatrix}a \\ b \\ 0\end{pmatrix}$
They all intersect with the line defined by
$l_{\infty} = \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}$
We know that two parallel lines intersect in $l_{\infty}$ so let us consider the fact that that any parallel line $m$ to $l$ must be the meet of the point $l \times l_{\infty}$ and $P$. Thus
$m = (l \times l_{\infty}) \times P$

## Collineations

> [!definition] Collineations
> A bijection $\tau: \mathcal{P} \cup \mathcal{L} \to \mathcal{P} \cup \mathcal{L}$ such that
>
> - $\tau(\mathcal{P}) = \mathcal{P}$
> - $\tau(\mathcal{L}) = \mathcal{L}$
> - $p \mathcal{I} l \iff \tau(p) \mathcal{I} \tau(l)$

This is simply a map between two projective planes, that preserves the structure we care about in the projective setting. Its not strong enough to keep all properties we care about though. For that we use

> [!definition] Projective Transformations
> Given a field $\mathbb{K}$ and a matrix $M \in \mathbb{K}^{3 \times 3}$ be non-degenerate. Then a collineation $\tau$ with
> $\tau(p) = M \cdot p \quad \tau(l) = \left( M^{T} \right)^{-1} \cdot l$
> is called a projective transformation.

The non-degeneracy preserves that geometric structure which a collineation does not preserve. Additionally the matrix structure makes the transformation linearly consistent. Note that we can immeadiately see that

> [!theorem|3.2]
> Every projective transformation is a collineation

`\begin{proof}`
We want to check if $\tau([p]) \mathcal{I} \tau([l])  \iff [p] \mathcal{I} [l]$ which we can simply do by expanding

$$
\tau([p]) \mathcal{I} \tau([l]) \iff \innprod{\tau(p)}{\tau(l)} = 0 \iff \tau(p)^{T} \tau(l) = 0 \iff (Mp)^{T}(M^{T})^{-1} l = 0 \iff p^{T}M^{T} (M^{T})^{-1}l = 0 \iff p^{T}l = 0 \iff [p] \mathcal{I} [l]
$$

`\end{proof}`

A very deep theorem that we can prove after this that

> [!theorem|3.3]
> Over $\R$ every collineation is a projective transformation

`\begin{proof}`
The proof will not be shown, but it uses some pretty deep field theoretic theory. Mainly we follow these steps

1. Show that over $\R$ every collineation is determined by the image of four points
2. We then using some density argument that over $\R$ any collineation can be composed into a projective transformation and a field automorphism
3. Then we obtain our result by noting that $\R$ has only one field automorphism.

`\end{proof}`

The first statement can be decomposed into a way to simply determine any projective transformation Matrix. Mainly we obtain these basic matrix structures:

- **Translation Matrices are given by**: $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & t_{x} \\ 0 & 1 & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$
- **Rotation Matrices are given by**: $\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha) & \sin(\alpha) & 0 \\ -\sin(\alpha) & \cos(\alpha) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$

So a general transofmration has the shape of
$\begin{pmatrix}a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$

### On Other Fields

Over an arbitrary field we can use the second step of the previous theirem to basically determine interesting collineations as they might be based on a different field automorphism.

For example in $\C$ we have two field automorphism which are $id$ and also $\overline \cdot$ which is complet conjugation.

If we look for finite fields that have non trivial automorphisms we can take as an example $\mathbb{K} = G \mathbb{F}_{2^{2}}$
