We want to axiomatize what a projective plane is from the ground up. Keep in mind that the reason we stay in 2D and speak a lot about planes is because most interesting projective properties can already be seen in 2 Dimensions. If higher dimensions are required we will talk about them.

> [!definition] Projective Plane
> A Projective Plane is a tripple $(\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{L}, \mathcal{I})$ where we call $\mathcal{P}$ “points” and $\mathcal{L}$ “lines” and the incidence relations $\mathcal{I} \subset \mathcal{P} \times \mathcal{L}$ giving us which points lie on which lines. Keep in mind that all names are arbitrary. Calling things points and lines is just useful.

We now define three axioms for this triple.

> [!axiom|A1] Joining Two Points
> For any two distinct points $p, q \in \mathcal{P}$ there exists exactly one line $l \in \mathcal{L}$ with $p \mathcal{I} l$ and $q \mathcal{I} l$

> [!axiom|A2] Meeting Two Lines
> For any two distinct lines $l, m \in \mathcal{L}$ there exists exactly one point $p \in \mathcal{P}$ with $p \mathcal{I} l$ and $p \mathcal{I} m$

> [!axiom|A3] Richness Axiom
> There are four points such that no three of them are incident to the same line

An interesting fact here is that while the first two axioms seem symmetric in every way, the third somehow might break this symmetry. You can though if wanted construct a consequence from these axioms that basically is the symmetric statement to the Richness Axiom we have here, stating: _There exists 4 lines such that no three of them are incident to the same point_

Keep in mind that we will start drawing these projective planes and use points and lines to denote them. This is nothing but a symbolic representation of these abstract sets. The projectiveness of this axiom system is inherent in this abstract structure and we dont have to write everything in the usual points and lines view.

## Models for Projective Planes

### Extending the euclidean plane

Given some portion of the euclidean plane we can see that the first axiom is automatically given. The problems start appearing when we consider parallel lines since these dont intersect.
To solve this we simply add a point and define the “meet” of two parallel lines $l, l'$ to be this point $P_{[l]}$, where $[l]$ denotes the equivalence class of all lines parallel to $l$. Doing this for every bundle of parallel lines gives us a lot of points we are adding but it allows us to satisfy A2.
We have now begun violating A1 though since no lines join these extra points. We now simply define a new line $l_\infty$ and make this line go through all new points. This “line at infinity” now finally solves our problems

![[04 - Resources/Assets/1. Axioms_ Extended Plane.png|center| 500]]

The explicit contstruction is given by

- $\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{E}} \cup \set{p_{[l]} \midline l \in \mathcal{L}_\mathbb{E}}$
- $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_\mathbb{E} \cup \set{l_\infty}$
- $\mathcal{I} = \mathcal{I}_\mathbb{E} \cup \set{(p_{[l]}, l) \midline l \in \mathcal{L}_\mathbb{E}} \cup \set{(p_{[l]}, l_{\infty)}\midline l \in \mathcal{L}_\mathbb{E}}$

### Smallest Projective Plane

If we can to be economic in constructing a projective plane. Since this must have atleast 4 points to satisfy A3 we can lay 4 Points. Using A1 we construct lines. Now these must also intersect, giving us 3 new points. We then check all axioms and have to add 1 extra line for A1 to be satisfied.
This construction is called the fano Plane

This object, as can especcially seen in the second ordering is highly symmetric in the sense that the group of automorphisms is rather complex. This can especially see if we reorder the points and lines to this

## First Simple Consequences

Given the Axioms we want to find some interesting conclusions

> [!lemma|2.1]
> The Incidence graph is square Free

`\begin{proof}`
The proof is given by looking at what squareness in the incidence graph means and noticing that a square would violate A1 and A2
`\end{proof}`

> [!lemma|2.2]
> Every Line is incident to at least 3 Points

`\begin{proof}`
Given our axiom A3 we can for a given line find 4 points $a, b, c, d$ such that non of them lie on the given line. Now picking out $a$ and meeting $a$ and $c$, $a$ and $d$, $a$ and $b$ gives us three lines such that these must intersect with our line, giving us 3 intersection points.

![[04 - Resources/Assets/1. Axioms_2 1 Proof.png|center| 400]]

`\end{proof}`

> [!lemma|2.3]
> For every point $p$ there is one line $l$ not incident to $p$

`\begin{proof}`
Given a point $p$ and some to $p$ incident lines $l$ and $m$ we can use the previous lemma to show that there must be some other $q$ on $l$ and $b$ on $m$. Joining these two points must give you a line that is not incident to $p$
`\end{proof}`

> [!theorem|2.1]
> Given a finite projective plane the number of points on each line is a constant

`\begin{proof}`
To prove this we assume we have two lines $l, m$ such that we know that on $l$ there are $n$ sets of points. One of these points by A2 is the meet of $l$ and $m$ say $p = l \land m$. Then by the previous lemma there exists a line $k$ such that it is not incident to $p$. Now we know that $q = k \land m$ and $q' = k \land l$ exist where $q'$ is the second of our $n$ points on $l$. Joining $q$ with all other points on $l$ gives us $n$ points on $m$.
Thus if $l$ has $n$ points $m$ has atleast $n$ points. Same if you swap $l$ and $m$ meaning that they have an equal number of points.
`\end{proof}`

Using the previous theorem we call one less than the constant number of points per line in a finite projective plane $n-1$ the order of the plane. So that fano plane has order $3-1 = 2$ .

> [!theorem|2.1']
> Given a finite projective plane of order $n$ then through every point there are $n+1$ lines

`\begin{proof}`
This is just an application of the symmetry of our system, since given a line $l$ we know that by the previous theorem there are $n+1$ points on this line. Joining these points with an arbitrary other point means that there are also $n+1$ lines going through this point.
`\end{proof}`

> [!theorem|2.2]
> A projective plane of order $n$ has $n^{2} + n + 1$ points and $n^{2} + n + 1$ lines.

`\begin{proof}`
Given a point we have $n+1$ lines going through them. On each of these lines we have $n+1$ points. Thus in total we have $(n+1)^{2} = n^{2} + n + 1$ points. By symmetry the same holds for lines.
`\end{proof}`

> [!remark] Higher Dimension
> In higher dimensions the number of points is given by
> $\sum\limits_{k=0}^{d} n^{d}$
