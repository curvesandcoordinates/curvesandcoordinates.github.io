# Projective Calculations

By projective calculations we mean the close interplay between projective planes and the numbers in their fields over which they are built

## From Projective Planes to Numbers

Say we want to measure an object in $\R^{2}$. Intuitively we want our measurement (as it is in the real world) to be invariant with respect to  transformations like rotation and translation. Here we can see that any direct measurement is invariant under these.

Felix Klein noted to this effect that defining a Geometry consists of either defining the respective and relevant transformation Groups and the invariants we want to preserve.

If we now extend our possible transformations to rotation, translations and scaling a simple measurement will not stay invariant. We can though look at ratios of measurements and thus obtain another valid geometry, by which I mean in the Kleinian sense a set of transformations and a measurement that is invariant under them.
![[04 - Resources/Assets/3. Projective Calculations_ Smiley Ratio.png|center|600]]

In the projective space though we now already know how a transformation should look. What are the invariants of these transformations? Direct measurements and ratios of measurements will just not work here, so what can we do?

If we note how our entire structure looks we can figure our that we want a projective measurement to be invariant under:

- Matrix Multiplication (By which we mean applying a projective transformation)
- Rescaling of representatives, since they are all identified in projective space

We in this spirit we define a projectively invariant function as

> [!definition|\*] Projectively Invariant Functions
> Given a Domain $D$ we call a function _projectively invariant_ if it is a map
> $f: \mathcal{P_\mathbb{K} }^{n} \setminus \set{\text{deg. situations}} \mapsto D$ such that
>
> 1. For all $M \in \mathbb{K}^{3 \times 3}, \det M \neq 0$ we get that $f(P_{1}, \ldots, P_{n}) = f (M \cdot P_{1}, \ldots, M \cdot P_{n})$
> 2. For all $\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n} \in \mathbb{K}$ with $\lambda_{i} \neq 0$ we have that $f(P_{1}, \ldots, P_{n}) = f( \lambda_{1} \cdot P_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n} \cdot P_{n})$

From this idea of invariance we can now define what invariant we will start measuring with in projective space. We fine for this the Cross-Ratio

> [!definition|\*] Cross-Ratio
> Given four points in projective space $A, B, C, D$ and some fifth point $X$ we call the _cross ratio seen from $X$_ the number
> $(A, B; C, D)_{X} := \frac{[X, A, C] [X, B, D]}{[X, A, D][X, B, C]}$

> [!proposition|\*] Invariance of the Cross-Ratio
> The Cross-Ratio is projectively invariant.

`\begin{proof}`
For this we need mainly the formula
$[MA, MB, MC] = \det(M) [A, B, C]$
Then we can see that trivially
$(MA, MB; MC, MD)_{MX} = \frac{[MX, MA, MC] [MX, MB, MD]}{[MX, MA, MD][MX, MB, MC]} = \frac{\det(M)^{2}}{\det(M)^{2}}\frac{[X, A, C] [X, B, D]}{[X, A, D][X, B, C]} = (A, B; C, D)_{X}$
and the invariance under scalar transformations is trivially given by the linearity of the determinant.

`\end{proof}`

## Cross Ratios and Their Properties

We have seen that the Cross Ratio somehow formally is exactly a measurement that is conserved under projective transformations. What we now might want to look at is how it behaves when given a transformation that isn’t projective.

The image we should always have in mind when considering Cross Ratios is this

![[04 - Resources/Assets/3. Projective Calculations_Cross Ratio.png|center|400]]

We can now ask, since the naming implies that is the only the Cross-Ratio seen from $X$ what happens if we move one Point, say $A$ along the connecting line between $A$ and $X$.

This amounts to considering a point $A' = \lambda A + \mu X$ such that $\lambda \neq 0$. Substituting this into the cross ratio gives us
$(A', B; C, D)_{X} = (A, B; C, D)_{X}$
This property extends to any possible point on the line. This means that inherently only the view “from $X$” really matters. We might aswell define a cross ratio for lines in this way, meaning for given lines $l, m, n, o$ all intersecting in a point $X$ we can define
$(l, m; n, o)_{X}= (A, B; C, D)_X$
for any points $A \mathcal{I} l, B \mathcal{I} m, C \mathcal{I} n, D \mathcal{I} o$.

### Cross Ratios as invariant coordinates

We can use the projective invariance of Cross-Ratios and given four points determine some unique numbers that determine any point spanned by these. The situation looks as follows
![[04 - Resources/Assets/3. Projective Calculations_Coordinates.png| center | 500]]

Given are the points $T, U, V, W$ and as long as these form a projective basis, meaning that no three of them are collinear we can calculate for any given point $P$ the values

$$$\begin{align*}
(T,U; W, P)_{V} &= \alpha\\
(T,U; V, P)_{W} &= \beta
\end{align*}$$
These define the point $P$ uniquely and are projectively invariant, meaning we can use these as sort of coefficients or coorinates describing $P$.

### Removing the view
If we now use the fact that we can move all points around on the respective connecting line with $X$ we can always imagine them being collienar.
![[04 - Resources/Assets/3. Projective Calculations_Collinear Cross Ratio.png|center |400]]
Then we can note that the cross-ratio can be interpreted by considering first the oriented area of a Triangle with given points $A, B, C$
$$area(A, B, C) = \frac{1}{2} \det \begin{pmatrix}a_{x} & b_{x} & c_{x} \\ a_{y} & b_{y} & c_{y} \\ 1 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$$
Noting that we can assume $A, B, C, D$ in our Cross-Ratio case are all in standard embedding since the cross-ratio is invariant underprojective transformations. Now applying this and the standard area formula $\frac{1}{2}h b$ with height $h$ and base $b$ we get
$$\begin{align*}
(A, B;C, D)_{X} :&= \frac{[X, A, C] [X, B, D]}{[X, A, D][X, B, C]}\\
&= \frac{area(X, A, C) \cdot area(X, B, D)}{area(X, A, D) \cdot area(X, B, C)} \\
&= \frac{\frac{h}{2} |AC| \cdot \frac{h}{2} |BD|}{\frac{h}{2} |AD| \cdot \frac{h}{2} |BC|}\\
&= \frac{|AC| \cdot |BD|}{|AD| \cdot |BC|}
\end{align*}$$
importantly we have found out that in the case of $A, B, C, D$ being collinear that the Cross-Ratio has no dependence on $X$ anymore. Using this case as a definition we can consider the cross-ratio of points on a line $l$ such that $A \mathcal{I} l, B \mathcal{I} l, C \mathcal{I} l, D \mathcal{I} l$ and define it by
$$(A,B; C, D) = \frac{|AC| \cdot |BD|}{|AD| \cdot |BC|}$$
> [!remark] A Note on Infinity
> We will sometimes divide by infinity but in the projective geometry case we will simply define
> $$\frac{1}{0} = \infty \quad \frac{1}{\infty} = 0$$
> Also
> $$\infty + 1 = \infty \quad \infty + \infty = \infty$$
> 

## The Projective Line
We will now talk about the Projective line and simply construct it in the same way as we did the projective plane, meaning
> [!definition] Projective Line
> We define the projective line over a field $\mathbb{K}$ as
> $$\mathbb{KP}^{1} = \frac{\mathbb{K}^{2} \setminus \set{0}}{\mathbb{K}\setminus \set{0}}$$
> We can consider the standard embedding in this case as a mapping
> $$\tau: \mathbb{K} \to \mathbb{KP}^{1}, \quad x \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}x  \\ 1\end{pmatrix}$$
> A projective transformation on the line is given by non degenerative matrix
> $$\begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}$$

A good thing to keep in mind is that any line in $\mathbb{KP}^{2}$ can be considered a projective line in this sense. So we should always think about those lines when we consider properties of projective lines.

With this definition we can define the crossratio of four points $A, B, C, D \in \R P^{1}$ can be simplified nicely by defining
$$(A, B; C, D) = \frac{[A, C][B, D]}{[A, D] [B, C]}$$
and noting that
$$[A, B] = \det\begin{pmatrix}a_{x} & b_{x} \\ a_{y} & b_{y}\end{pmatrix} = \det \begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix} = a-b$$
since this cross ratio is also invariant under projective transformations, thus we can assume our points are in standard embedding. With this in hand we can determine that the cross-ratio on a projective line is given by
$$(A, B; C, D) = \frac{(a-b)(b-d)}{(a-d)(b-c)} \quad A = \begin{pmatrix}a \\ 1\end{pmatrix}, B = \begin{pmatrix}b \\ 1\end{pmatrix}, C = \begin{pmatrix}c \\ 1\end{pmatrix}, D = \begin{pmatrix}d \\ 1\end{pmatrix}$$


Having now defined the cross-ratio we can actually see very nicely how this defines in a sense a sort of projective invariant scale that we can use. Specifically consider the points
$$ \bf{0} = \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \quad \infty = \begin{pmatrix}1  \\  0\end{pmatrix} \quad x = \begin{pmatrix}x \\ 1\end{pmatrix} \quad 1 = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}$$
then calculating the cross-ratio explicitly gives us
$$(0, \infty; x, 1) = \frac{(-x) \cdot \det\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}}{(-1) \cdot \det\begin{pmatrix}1 & x \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}} = \frac{x}{1} = x$$
so we have in a sense created a ruler, which we know is projectively invariant, which in our standard embedding gives every value the correct value but can be projectively transformed without problems. This is a good motivation for the different slots in the cross-ratio since we can think of the first slot as giving us where our ruler is $0$ and $\infty$ as the point showing where we are pointing the ruler. The ratio then tells us how many distances from $0$ to $1$ we have between $0$ and $x$ in a projectively invariant way.dar
## Harmonic Points and Quadrilateral Sets

Lets now work on some interplay between the projective plane and our projective line and the cross-ratio. Given some configuration of points and lines it might be interesting to characterize this configuration by projecting each point down to some projective line and looking at these projected points on the line.

Given say 4 lines and all their intersections (meaning 6 points). If these are chosen non-degenerativly we can consider exactly this projection

![[04 - Resources/Assets/3. Projective Calculations_Quadset.png|center | 400]]

We would like to ask a realisation question about this situation more specifically: *If we are given a projective line with points $a$ through $f$ when can we recreate the construction in the projective plane given we have the collinearities $(A, B, C), (B, D, F), (C, D, E)$?*

For this question let us first ask how free the projectiction or shadow of our construction is in general. We can first have to pick the line containing $A, B, C$ freely and also an additional point $E$

![[04 - Resources/Assets/3. Projective Calculations_Constructing Shadow Free.png|center|400]]
but from here on out all the other points are determined by joins and meets.

This means that our construction should be independent of our first choice of line and the first point thus we can simplify all the situations to a case where the line $(A, B, C)$ is parallel to our projective line and even translating them to coincie. We now parametrize the problem by considering the heights of the points from our projective line. Since we have already said that the line $(A, B, C)$ is set to be at the same position as our projective line we know $h_{a} = h_{b} = h_{c} = 0$ and we now have to only find $h_{d}, h_{e}, h_{f}$.

Here is where we bring our collinearities back in. Let us translate them to formulas
$$\begin{align*}
(A, B, C) \text{ collinear } &\iff [A, B, C] = 0\\
(B, D, F) \text{ collinear } &\iff [B, D, F] = 0\\
(C, D, E) \text{ collinear } &\iff [C, D, E] = 0
\end{align*}$$

These determinants can help us if we explicitly embedd all our points. Let us consider the embedding 

$$\begin{pmatrix}x \\ y\end{pmatrix} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}x \\ 1 \\ y\end{pmatrix}$$
and our line has the embedding
$$x \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}x \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}$$

Then using this each of the points in the plane can be represented like this
$$A = \begin{pmatrix}a \\ 1 \\ h_{a}\end{pmatrix}$$
and thus our collinearities give us
$$\begin{align*}
0 &= [A, B, C] = \det \begin{pmatrix}a  & e & f \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & h_e & h_f\end{pmatrix} = h_{e} [af] - h_{f} [ae] \\
0 &= [B, D, F] = \det \begin{pmatrix}b  & d & f \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & h_d & h_f\end{pmatrix} = h_{d} [bf] - h_{f}[bd]\\
0 &= [C, D, E] = \det \begin{pmatrix}c  & d & e \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & h_d & h_e\end{pmatrix} = h_{e}[cd] - h_{d}[ce]
\end{align*}$$
where we switch perspective slightly since $[af]$ describes the determinand of the $2 \times 2$ matrix given by the embeddign of the plane in the usual way. Now this is a list of three equations that is linear, thus we can describe it as a matrix problem

$$\begin{pmatrix}0 & [af] & -[ae] \\ -[bf]  & 0 & [bd] \\ [ce] & -[cd] & 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}h_{d} \\ h_e \\ h_{f}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

This means that finally we have described that we can find a realisation of some set of points on a projective line into the projective plane if and only if this matrix equation is solvable. We know that this amounts to have non-zero solutions thus have zero determinant. This means that for any det of points satisfying
$$[af][bd][ce] - [ae][bf][cd] = 0$$
we can find a realisation and solve our problem. Note that this equation is equivalent to
$$\frac{[af][bd][ce] }{[ae][bf][cd]} = 1$$
which is a projectively invariant equation, thus telling us that our choice to simplify were all justified and also that we dont have to project onto a line via some linear projection (which is simply a central projection to the point at infinity) but rather centrally project to any point and still allow for realisations
![[04 - Resources/Assets/3. Projective Calculations_Quadset Central Projection.png|center|400]]

We now define sets of points by exactly this property
> [!definition] Quadrilateral Sets
> Any set of six points on a projective line $\mathbb{KP}^{1}$ that can be realised in the above sense is called a quadrilateral set, denoted by the tuple
> $$(ad \mid be \mid cf)$$
> equivalently this amounts to 
> $$[af][bd][ce] - [ae][bf][cd] = 0$$

> [!remark] Why Quadrilatera
> The question about why these sets might be called quadrilateral is natural. We can use the duality of points and lines to show why. Specifically we can create a construction that is dual to the one before which has $4$ points and $6$ lines and project that down. This set also satifies the same arguments as before and is also called a quadrilateral set
> ![[04 - Resources/Assets/3. Projective Calculations Dual Quad Set.png| center |400]]


> [!definition] Harmonic Sets
> We call a quadrilateral set in $(a, d \mid bb \mid cc)$ a harmonic set. It satisfies the equation
> $$-1 = \frac{[ac] [db]}{[ab][dc]} = (a, d; b, c)$$ 

A useful way to use these harmonic sets is to consider the fact that if we let $c$ go to infinity in $\mathbb{KP}^{1}$ we can easily prove that this construction makes $b$ the exact center between $a$ and $d$

![[04 - Resources/Assets/3. Projective Calculations Harmonic Points Use.png]]

## From Planes to Fields
We would like to now take the step to define for any projective line some addition and multiplication, prove that the set of points with these operations is a field and find a bijection between our projective lines $\mathbb{KP}^{1}$ and $\mathbb{K}$. To do this and specifically to prove the properties of a field we will use some useful incidence theorems which will be connected to the theorem of pappos and desargue.

### Five Useful Incidence Theorems
Let us first define the five theorems we will be useing 

$$$
