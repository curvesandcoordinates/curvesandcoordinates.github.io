# Tensors in a nutshell

**Previous**: [[2.4 Sub-bundles, Submersions, Foliations and Frobenius Theorem MA3205]]
**Next**: [[4.1 (semi-) Riemannian Metrics MA3205]]

> [!definition] Tensor
> Let $V$ be an $R$-module, $r, s \geq 0$ and $r$ or $s$ non-zero. A multilinear form $A: (V^{\ast})^{r} \times V^{s} \to R$ is called a $(r, s)$-tensor on $V$. Here $A$ is called $r$-times contravariant and $s$-times covariant. The set of all $(r,s)$-tensors forms an $R$-module on its own.
>
> A $(r, s)$-tensor field on a manifold $M$ is a smooth section in the vector bundle $T_{s}^{r}(M):= \Gamma \left(\bigcup_{p \in M} \set{L: (T_{p}M^{\ast})^{r} \times T_{p} M^{s} \to \R \midline L \text{ multilinear}}\right)$
> Alternatively one can define the elemtns of $T^{r}_{s}(M)$ to be tensors on the $\mathcal{F}(M)$-module $\Gamma(TM)$

> [!remark] Simple Examples of Tensor Fields
>
> - $(0, 1)$- Tensor fields are often called $1$-Forms.
>   - A good example of a $1$-Form we already know is given for any map $f \in \mathcal{F}(M)$ we have that $df: p \mapsto d_{p} f \in T_{p}M^{*}$ is a $1$-Form
> - One has of course $T^{0}_{1}(M) = \Gamma(TM^{*})$ but one also can identify $T^{1}_{0}(M)$ and $\Gamma(TM)$ by considering a vector field $V$ applies to a $1$-Form $\omega \in \Gamma(TM^{*})$ by $V(\omega) := \omega(V)$ with a pointwise identification.
>   - Using this application on $df$ results in thinking of ${} df: \Gamma(TM) \to \mathcal{F}(M), d_{p} f (X) = d_{p} f (X_{p}) = X_{p}(f) {}$
> - For completeness we set $\mathcal{F}(M) = T^{0}_{0}(M)$
> - Tensor fields are $\mathcal{F}(M)$-Linear

> [!note]  Differential Forms are Tensors
> A specific type of Tensor is also given by a [[MOC - CIT4130022 Differential Forms|Differential Form]] on a Manifold. These have the extra property that they are not just multilinear but alternating $(0, s)$-Forms.

> [!definition] Tensor Product and Basis of Tensors
> The tensor product of an $(r, s)$-tensor $A$ and a $(\tilde r, \tilde s)$-tensor $B$ is the $r+ \tilde r, s + \tilde s)$-tensor $A \otimes B$ given by$(A \otimes B)(\omega_{1}, \ldots, \omega_{r+ \tilde r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s + \tilde s}) = A(\omega_{1}, \ldots, \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s}) \cdot B(\omega_{r+1}, \ldots \omega_{r+ \tilde r}, v_{s+1}, \ldots, v_{s + \tilde s})$
> Obviously ${} \otimes$ is $\mathcal{F}(M)$ linear and associative.
>
> Let $\phi= (x^{1}, \cdots, x^{n}): U \to \R^{n}$ be a coordinate system on $M$. In each $p \in U$ the $\del_{1}, \ldots, \del_{n}$ form a Basis of $T_{p} M$. Let $dx^{1}, \ldots, dx^{n}$ denote the corresponding dual basis. As a multilinear map a $(r, s)$-tensor $A$ is completely determined by its values on these basis elements. $A^{i_{1}, \ldots, i_{r}}_{j_{1}, \ldots, j_{r}}= A(dx^{i_{1}}, \ldots, dx^{i_{r}}, \del_{j_{1}}, \ldots, \del_{j_{1}})$
> These numbers a re called the tensor components of $A$ with respect to $\phi$

> [!definition] Contraction
> The contraction $C$ is the unique $\mathcal{F}(M)$-linear map $C: T^{1}_{1} \to \mathcal{F}(M)$ for which $C(X \otimes \omega) = \omega(X)$holds for $X \in \Gamma(TM), \omega \in \Gamma(TM^{*})$
> In general the $(i,j)$-contraction $C_{j}^{i}$ of an $(r, s)$-tensor $A$ with $1 \leq i \leq r$ and $1 \leq j \leq s$ is the $(r-1, s-1)$-tensor $C_{j}^{i}(A)$ given by$C_{j}^{i}(A)(\omega_{1} , \ldots, \omega_{r-1}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s-1}) = C(A(\omega_{1} , \ldots,\omega_{i-1}, \cdot, \omega_{i} ,\ldots, \omega_{r-1}, v_{1},  , \ldots,v_{j-1}, \cdot, v_{j} ,\ldots, v_{s-1}))$

`\begin{proof}`
We need to justify the uniqueness claims of this definiition.
Given a coordinate system $\phi: U \to \R^{n}$ one can write a $(1, 1)$-tensor $A$ as $A = \sum\limits_{i,j} A^{i}_{j} \left( \del_{i}\otimes dx^{j} \right)$ since $\left( \sum\limits_{i,j} A^{i}_{j} \del_{i}\otimes dx^{j} \right)(dx^{l}, \del_{k}) = \sum\limits_{i,j} A_{j}^{i} \left( \del_{i}(dx^{l}) \cdot dx^{j}(\del_{k}) \right) = \sum\limits_{i,j}A_{j}^{i} \left( \delta_{il} \delta_{jk} \right) =A^{l}_{k} = A(dx^{l}, \del_{k})$ Since $C$ is $\mathcal{F}(M)$ linear we find that $C(A) = \sum\limits_{i,j} A^{i}_{j} C(\del_{i}\otimes dx^{j}) = \sum\limits_{i} A_{i}^{i}$
If we wrtie $X$ and $\omega$ as $X = \sum\limits X^{i} \del_{i}$ and $\omega = \sum\limits \omega_{j} dx^{j}$. The tensor components of $B = X \otimes \omega$ are just $B^{i}_{j} = X^{i} \cdot \omega_{j}$
Now the contraction $C(B) = C( X \otimes \omega) = \sum\limits_{i} X^{i} \cdot \omega_i$
`\end{proof}`

> [!prompt]- What connection is there between the Contraction and the Trace of Matrices
> When thinking of Matrices $M \in \R^{n\times n}$ as a $(1, 1)$-Tensor by noting that $T_{M}(v, w) = vMw$ then we can see that $T_M(e_{i}, e^{j}) = M_{ij}$
> Meaning the contraction by the above logic is given by $C(T_{M)}= \sum\limits_{i} M_{ii} = tr M \in \R$ turning it into a real number so our $(1, 1)$-Tensor becomes a $(0, 0)$-Tensor aka a real number.

> [!prompt]- What does $X \otimes \omega$ correspond to in Matrix-World and how does the contractions definiition on this object make sense
> If we think of matrices, then we would have $v \otimes w$ where $v \in V$ and $w \in V^{*}$ then we can think of the tensor as the matrix multiplication
> $M =vw$
> which corresponds to the dyadic product of two vectors. This means the contraction simply corresponds to $w(v)$ since $C(M) = \sum\limits_{i} v_{i} w^{i} = w v = w(v)$

> [!remark] Invariance of Contractions under Basis Change
> This representation is invariant under the choice of coordinates:
> If $\eta = (y^{1}, \ldots, y^{n}): V \to \R^{n}$ is another coordinate system then on $U \cap V$ we have $\sum\limits_{l} A \left( dy^{l}, \frac{\del}{\del y_{l}} \right) = \sum\limits_{l} A \left( \sum\limits_{i} \frac{\del y^{l}}{\del x^{i}} dx^{i}, \sum\limits_{j} \frac{\del x^{j}}{\del y^{l}} \frac{\del}{\del x^{j}}\right) = \sum\limits_{l,i,j} \frac{\del y^{l}}{\del x^{i}} \frac{\del x^{j}}{\del y^{l}} A \left( dx^{i}, \frac{\del}{\del x^{j}} \right) = \sum\limits_{i,j} \delta_{ij} A \left( dx^{i}, \frac{\del}{\del x^{j}} \right) = \sum\limits_{i} A\left(dx^{i}, \frac{\del}{\del x^{i}}\right)$
> where the second equality comes from the fact that $dy^{l}$ is the total differential of the coordinate function $y^{l}$ and an element of the co-[[1.1 Tangent Space MA3205|tangent space]]. So $dy^{l} = \sum\limits_{i} \lambda_{i} dx^{i}$. Appying this to $\frac{\del}{\del x^{j}}$ we get that $\lambda_{j} = \sum\limits_{i} \lambda_{i} dx^{i} \left( \frac{\del}{\del x^{j}} \right) = dy^{l} \left(\frac{\del}{\del x^{j}} \right) = \frac{\del y^{l}}{\del x^{j}}$

> [!definition] Tensor Derivations
> A tensor derivation $D$ is a family of $\R$-linear maps $D = D^{r}_{s}: T^{r}_{s}(M) \to T^{r}_{s}(M)$ such that for all tensors $A, B$ we have
>
> - $D(A \otimes B) = D(A) \otimes B + A \otimes D(B)$
> - $D(C(A)) = C(D(A))$ for all contractions $C$

> [!remark] Connection between Tensor Derivations and Usual Derivations
> We have that $T^{0}_{0}(M) = \mathcal{F}(M)$. So $D^{0}_{0}$ is a derivation. Thus there must be a vector field $V$ such that $D^{0}_{0}(f) = V(f)$ for all $f \in \mathcal{F}(M)$.

With this remark in mind we will now show that any tensor derivation is determined completely by how it operates on $\mathcal{F}(M)$ and $\Gamma(TM)$

Take $A \in T^{0}_{1}(M)$ and $B \in T^{1}_{0}(M)$. Then given some coordinates we can write explicitly for some $X = \sum\limits_{j} X^{j} \del_{j}$ and some ${} \omega = \sum\limits_{j} \omega_{j} dx_{j} {}$

$$$$$\begin{align*}
A(X) = \sum\limits_{i}A_{i} dx^{i} \left( \sum\limits_{j} X^{j} \del_{j} \right) = \sum\limits_{i} A_{i}X^{i} && B(\omega) = \sum\limits_{j} B^{j} \omega_{j}
\end{align*}$$
$$$$So $A(X) = C(A \otimes X)$ and $B(\omega) = C(B \otimes \omega)$. 
With this we can use the properties of derivations to see that:
$$D(A(X)) = D(C(A \otimes X)) = C(D(A \otimes X)) = C(D(A) \otimes X + A \otimes D(X)) = D(A)(X) + A(D(X))$$
Meaning we know what $D(A)$ does if we know what $D$ does on functions and vector fields: 
$$D(A)(X) = D(A(X)) - A(D(X))$$
Thus the derivation acting on $1$-Forms only depends on the behaviour of $D$ on function $A(X)$ and its behaviour on vector fields like $X$.
Likewise for a general $(r, s)$-tensor
$$\begin{align*}
D(A(\omega_{1}, \ldots, \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s})) &=  D \left( \sum\limits A^{i_{1}, \ldots i_{r}}_{j_{1}, \ldots j_{r}} \del_{i_{1}} \otimes \cdots \otimes \del_{i_{r}} \otimes dx_{j_{1}} \otimes \cdots \otimes dx_{j_{s}} (\omega_{1}, \ldots, \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s}) \right)\\
&= \sum\limits D \left(A^{i_{1}, \ldots i_{r}}_{j_{1}, \ldots j_{r}} \del_{i_{1}}(\omega_{1}) \otimes \cdots \otimes \del_{i_{r}}(\omega_{r}) \otimes dx_{j_{1}}(v_{1}) \otimes \cdots \otimes dx_{j_{s}} (v_{s}) \right)\\
&= \sum\limits A^{i_{1}, \ldots i_{r}}_{j_{1}, \ldots j_{r}}D \left( \del_{i_{1}}(\omega_{1}) \otimes \cdots \otimes \del_{i_{r}}(\omega_{r}) \otimes dx_{j_{1}}(v_{1}) \otimes \cdots \otimes dx_{j_{s}} (v_{s}) \right)
\end{align*}$$
Now we simply have the derivation of a big product. The general product rule basically applys $D$ to each faktor once and adds all those tensors together, giving us
$$\begin{align*}
D(A(\omega_{1}, \ldots, \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s})) &= D(A)(\omega_{1}, \ldots, \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s}) + \sum\limits_{i} A(\omega_{1}, \ldots, D(\omega_{i}) , \ldots \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s}) + \sum\limits_{j} A(\omega_{1}, \ldots, \omega_{r}, v_{1},\ldots, D(v_{j}), \ldots, v_{s})
\end{align*}$$
Using the simplification from before we can alway write $D(\omega_{i})(X) = D(\omega_{i}(X)) - \omega_{i}(D(X))$ meaning in total we can calculate the derivation of a tensor by applying the derivation on the evaluated tensor (which is just a function) and then evaluating the tensor on the inputs where one input is derivated.


> [!info] Calculating Derivations of Tensors
> Given a Tensor $A$ and a derivation $D$ we can explicitly calculate using the above formula
> $$\begin{align*}
D(A)(\omega_{1}, \ldots, \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s}) &= D(A(\omega_{1}, \ldots, \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s})) - \sum\limits_{i} A(\omega_{1}, \ldots, D(\omega_{i}) , \ldots \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s}) - \sum\limits_{j} A(\omega_{1}, \ldots, \omega_{r}, v_{1},\ldots, D(v_{j}), \ldots, v_{s})
\end{align*}$$


> [!lemma] Tensor Derivations are determined by its behaviour on functions and vector fields
> - If two tensor derivations coincice on functions and vector fields then they are identical. 
> - Given a vector field $V$ and an $\R$-linear map $\delta: \Gamma(TM) \to \Gamma(TM)$ with $\delta(fX) = V(f) X + f(\delta(X))$ then there is a unique tensor derivation $D$ with D(f) = V(f) and $D(X) = \delta(X)$.

`\begin{proof}`
Part 1 was already proven above.

Part 2: Take the above formula as a definition and check product rule by expanding and the contraction property.
`\end{proof}`


> [!idea] Everything given coincides with the Leibniz Property
> If we consider the statement of the previous Lemma it might look like a random restriction. But one should think of a Derivation as having two parts: The Function acting part and the vector field acting part. We define a derivation $D$ in this way. Given some vector field $V$ and a function $\delta: \Gamma(TM) \to \Gamma(TM)$ we define $$D(f) = V(f) \quad D(X) = \delta(X)$$
>But since this derivation has tu support the leibniz property we get for $f \in \mathcal{F}(M)$ that 
>$$\delta(fX) = D(fX) = D(f)X + f D(X) =V(f)X + f \delta(X)$$
