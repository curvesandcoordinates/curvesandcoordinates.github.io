# Group actions, flows and integrale curves

**Previous**: [[2.1 Vector Bundles and the Tangential Bundle MA3205]]
**Next Chapter**: [[2.3 Baby Lie Groups MA3205]]

> [!definition] Group Actions in General
> Let $X$ be a set and $G$ a group. A (left-) group action of $G$ on $X$ is a map $\Phi: G \times X \to X, \quad (g, x) \mapsto g\cdot x$with the following properties:
>
> 1. For the neutral element $e$ we should have that $e \cdot x = x$ for all $x \in X$
> 2. For all $g, h \in G$ and $x \in X$ we want $g \cdot (h \cdot x) = gh \cdot x$

> [!example] Examples of Group Actions
>
> - A group acts on itself by left multiplication.
> - Let $V$ be a vector space. Then the group $Aut(V)$ of automorphisms acts on $V$, by applying $A \cdot v = A(v)$
> - $SL(2, \C)$ acts on the one-point compactification of $\C$, denoted $\hat{\C} \cong \C P^{1}$ by mean sof fractional linear maps (Möbius Transformations). So $\begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix} \cdot z = \frac{az+b}{cz + d}$

> [!definition] Flows
> Let $X$ be a set. A left group action of $(\R, +)$ on $X$ is called a [[Flow CIT413033]].

> [!example] Rotation in the Complex Plane as an Action
> Consider $F: \R \times \C \to \C$ with $(t, z) \mapsto e^{it}z$ is a [[Flow CIT413033]] on $\C$ which rotates each point in $\C$ by the angle $t$

> [!definition] Integral Curves
> Let $M$ be a manifold, $V \in \Gamma(TM). A curve \gamma: I \to M$ on $M$ is called an integral curve of $V$ through $p \in M$ if $\gamma(0) = p$ and $\at{\frac{d}{dt}}{t=0}\gamma = V_{\gamma(0)}$An integral curve is called maximal if it has maximal domain. A vector field is called complete, if all its maximal integral curves are defined on all of $\R$.

With these definitions we can for any Manifold $M$ and vector field $V \in \Gamma(TM)$ define a maximal integral curve at the point $p \in M$ by $\gamma_{p}$. We can then define $\Phi_{t}(p) = \gamma_{p}(t)$ and if $V$ is complete this is a [[Flow CIT413033]] on $M$. If $V$ is not complete then we can call $\Phi$ a local [[Flow CIT413033]].[^6]

> [!definition] Lie Bracket
> Let $M$ be a manifold and $V, W \in \Gamma(TM)$. The Lie bracket of $V$ and $W$ is the vectorfield $[V, W]$ given by $[V, W](f) = V(W(f)) - W(V(f))$

It is easy to check $\R$-Linearity of the Lie-Bracket. For the Leibniz rule we explicitly compute.

$$$\begin{align*}
[V, W](fg) &= V(W(fg)) - W(V(fg))\\
&= V(W(f)g + fW(g))- W(V(f)g + fV(g))\\
&= V(W(f))g + W(f)V(g) + V(f)W(g) + fV(W(g)) \\
& \quad- W(V(f))g-V(f)W(g)-W(f)V(g)-fW(V(g))\\
&= \left(V(W(f))-W(V(f))\right)g+ f(V(W(g))-W(V(g)))\\
&= [V, W](f) \cdot g + [V, W](g) \cdot f
\end{align*}$$

We can also get to the Lie bracket by not explicitly defining this operation but rather finding a product of vectors in a vector space with the following properties and call the resulting algebra a Lie algebra. In that way the following Lemma contains the defining properties of our Lie bracket.

> [!lemma] Chracterisation of a Lie Bracket
> For the Lie bracket the following holds for all $X, Y, Z \in \Gamma(TM)$
> 1. $[X, Y]$ is $\R$-Linear in both entries
> 2. $[X, X] = 0$ and therefore $[X, Y] = - [Y, X]$
> 3. Jacobi-Identity: $[X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]] = 0$
> 4. If $\Phi$ is the local [[Flow CIT413033]] of $X$ in a nbhd of $p$. then $[X, Y]_{p} = \lim_{p \to 0} \frac{d_{\Phi_{t}(p)}\Phi_{-t}(Y_{\Phi_{t}(p)}) - Y_{p}}{t}$
>
>The last property can intuitively be thought of as the derivative of $X$ wrt to $Y$. Since we are in essence comparing $Y_{\Phi_{t}(p)}$ with $Y_{p}$ and taking the limit over their “distance” given by $t$.

^a1e6df

`\begin{proof}`
1 to 3 are trivial in since they can be easily shown. So let us only prove 4.

Assume at first that $X_{p}\neq 0$. We constrcut a coordinate system adapted to $X$. For this consider the curve $t \mapsto \Phi_{t}(p)$ is a curve (1- Dim Submanifold in $M$). Let $\phi: U \to \R^{n}$ be a coordinate system in $p$ with $\phi(p) = 0$. Then $t \mapsto \phi \circ \Phi_{t}(p)$ is a curve in $\R^{n}$ and we can assume that its derivative at $0$ is transversal to $e_{2}, \cdots, e_{n}$, meaning it points into the direction of $e_{1}$.
Then we define $N = \phi^{-1}\left( \set{0} \times \R^{n - 1} \cap \phi(U)\right)$ is a $n-1$ dimensional submanifold of $M$. Now let $\eta: V \to \R^{n-1}$ be a coordinate system in $N$ around $p$. For a small $t_{0}$ and a small nbhd $W$ of $p$ the map $$q \mapsto \Phi_{t_{0}}(q)$$is a diffeomorphism. Thus $${} \nu^{-1}: (-\epsilon, \epsilon) \times \nu(W \cap N) \to M \quad  (t, x) \mapsto \Phi_{t}(\eta^{-1}(x))$$is a diffeomorphism onto its image aswell meaning $\nu$ is a coordinate system in $p$.
Moreover, we have $$\del_{1} = X \quad \nu_{i}(\Phi_{t}(q)) = \nu_{i}(q) + t \delta_{i 1} \quad d \Phi_{t}(\del_{i}) = \del_i$$
These three facts are obtained by simple calculation
1. For every $p \in M$ we have that for some $f: M \to \R$ that $$\at{\del_{i}}{p}(f) = \at{\frac{\del (f \circ \nu^{-1})}{\del x_{i}}}{\nu(p)} = \at{\frac{\del (f \circ \Phi_{\alpha} \circ\eta^{-1}(\beta))}{\del x_{i}}}{(\alpha, \beta)}=\at{\frac{\del (f \circ \gamma_{p}(t) \circ\eta^{-1}(\beta))}{\del x_{i}}}{(\alpha, \beta)} = d_{\alpha}\gamma_{p}(\at{\del_{i, \eta}}{p})(f) = X_{\Phi_{\alpha}(p)}(f)$$
2. It is clear that due to the definition of $\nu^{-1}(t, x) = \Phi_{t}(\eta^{-1}(x))$ we get that $$\nu_{i}(\Phi_{t}(q)) = t \delta_{1, i} + \eta^{-1}_{i}(x)$$
3. Given a point $p \in M$ with the property that $p = \nu^{-1}(\alpha, \beta)$ and a map $f: M \to \R$ we can calculate that $$\begin{align*}
d_{p}\Phi_{t}(\at{\del_{i}}{p}) (f) &=  \at{\del_{i}}{p} (f \circ \Phi_{t}) =  \at{\frac{\del (f \circ \Phi_{t} \circ \nu^{-1})}{\del x_{i}}}{(\alpha, \beta)} \\
&=  \at{\frac{\del (f \circ \Phi_{t} \circ \Phi_{\alpha} \circ \eta^{-1}(\beta)))}{\del x_{i}}}{(\alpha, \beta)} =  \at{\frac{\del (f \circ \Phi_{t + \alpha} \circ \eta^{-1})}{\del x_{i}}}{(\alpha, \beta)} \\
&=  \at{\frac{\del (f \circ \Phi_{t + \alpha} \circ \eta^{-1})}{\del x_{i}}}{(\alpha, \beta)} = \at{\frac{\del (f \circ \nu^{-1})}{\del x_{i}}}{(t + \alpha, \beta)}\\
&= \at{\del_{i}}{\nu^{-1}(t + \alpha, \beta)}
\end{align*}$$
Now, define $$\begin{align*}
g(t) &:=  d_{\Phi_{t}(p)}\Phi_{-t}(Y_{\Phi_{t}(p)}) \\
&=   d_{\Phi_{t}(p)}\Phi_{-t} \left(\sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(\Phi_{t}(p)) \at{\del_{i}}{\Phi_{t}(p)} \right) \\
&=  \sum\limits_{i} d_{\Phi_{t}(p)}\Phi_{-t} \left( Y_{i}(\Phi_{t}(p)) \at{\del_{i}}{\Phi_{t}(p)} \right) \\
&=  \sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(\Phi_{t}(p)) d_{-t}\Phi(\at{\del_{i}}{p}) \\
&=  \sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(\Phi_{t}(p)) \at{\del_{i}}{p}\\
&=  \sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(\gamma_{p}(t)) \at{\del_{i}}{p}
\end{align*}$$
Now we would like to compute explicitly $$\lim_{t \to 0} \frac{d_{\Phi_{t}(p)}\Phi_{-t}\left( Y_{\Phi_{t}(p)}\right) - Y_{p}}{t} = g'(0)$$
To do this we first notice $$g'(0) = \sum\limits_{i} \at{\frac{d}{dt}}{t = 0} Y_{i}(\gamma_{p}(t)) \at{\del_{i}}{p}$$
Now we can simplify a lot by seeing that
$$\at{\frac{d}{dt}}{t = 0} Y_{i} \circ \gamma_{p} = (Y_{i} \circ \gamma_{p})'(0) = d_{p}Y_{i}(\gamma_{p}'(0)) = d_{p}Y_{i}(X_{p})$$
since $\gamma$ is integral curve of out vector field $X$. Now we can also notice using the fact that $\phi: T_{Y_{i}(p)} \ni v \mapsto v(id) \in \R$ is an isomorphism of vector spaces that $$d_{p}Y_{i}(X_{p}) (id) = X_{p}(Y_{i}) = X(Y_{i})(p)$$
thus $$\at{\frac{d}{dt}}{t = 0} Y_{i} \circ \gamma_{p} = X_{p}(Y_{i}) = \at{\del_{1}}{p}(Y_{i})$$
finally this means that $g'(0) = \sum\limits_{i} \at{\del_{1}}{p}(Y_{i}) \at{\del_{i}}{p}$

We now calculate on the other hand the Lie bracket of our vector spaces
$$\begin{align*}
[X, Y]_{p}(f) &= X_{p}(Y(f)) + Y_{p}(X(f))\\
&= X_{p}\left(\sum\limits_{i} Y_{i} \del_{i}(f)\right) - \sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(p) \at{\del_{i}}{p}\left( X(f) \right)\\
&= \sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(p) X_p(\del_{i}(f)) + \sum\limits_{i} X_{p}(Y_{i}) \at{\del_{i}}{p}(f) - \sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(p) \at{\del_{i}}{p}\left( X(f) \right)
\end{align*}$$
Now we simply note that in our setup and coordinates we have seen that ${} X_{p} = \at{\del_{1}}{p} {}$ thus this simplifies to
$$\begin{align*}
[X, Y]_{p}(f) &= \sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(p) \at{\del_{1}}{p}(\del_{i}(f)) + \sum\limits_{i} \at{\del_{1}}{p}(Y_{i}) \at{\del_{i}}{p}(f) - \sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(p) \at{\del_{i}}{p}\left( \del_{1}(f) \right)\\
&= \sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(p) \at{\del_{i}}{p}\left( \del_{1}(f) \right) + \sum\limits_{i} \at{\del_{1}}{p}(Y_{i}) \at{\del_{i}}{p}(f) - \sum\limits_{i} Y_{i}(p) \at{\del_{i}}{p}\left( \del_{1}(f) \right)\\
&= \sum\limits_{i} \at{\del_{1}}{p}(Y_{i}) \at{\del_{i}}{p}(f) = g'(0) 
\end{align*}$$
Where the middle equality is given by the fact that $\at{\del_{i}}{p}(\del_{j}(f))= \at{\del_{j}}{p}(\del_{i}(f))$ which is due to Schwartz. This proves what we wanted under the assumption that $X_{p} \neq 0$.

If $X_{p} = 0$ we can simply choose a sequence of points $q_{n}$ with $X_{q_{n}} \neq 0$ and $q_{n}\to p$ and via continuity of $g$ the argument still holds.
`\end{proof}`


> [!remark] Lie Bracket it not Linear in Functions
> - The Lie bracket is not $\mathcal{F}(M)$-Linear. Meaning that for $f \in \mathcal{F}(M)$ in general ${} [fV, W] = f[V, W] - W(f) W \neq f[V, W] {}$.
> 	- This is a simple calculation of applying
>$$ [fV, W](f) = f V(W(f)) - W(f \cdot V (f)) = f V(W(f)) - W(f)V (f) - f W(V(f)) = f[V, W] - W(f)V(f) $$
> - For Gauß vector fields $\del_{i}, \del_{j}$ we have that $[\del_{i}, \del_{j}] = 0$

We can define this Lie bracket thing in a more general setting which will also be useful to us.

> [!definition] Lie Algebra
> Let $V$ be an $\R$-vector space. A map $[ \cdot , \cdot ]: V \times V \to V$ with the properties from above is called a (real) Lie Bracket and the pair ${} (V, [ \cdot, \cdot]) {}$ is called a Lie algebra.

> [!example] Examples of Lie Algebras
> - $\R^{n}$ together with ${} [v, w] = 0 {}$ is a Lie algebra
> - $\Gamma(TM)$ on a manifold $M$ is an infinite dimensional Lie algebra
> - $\R^{3}$ togehter with the cross-product $[v, w] := v \times w$ is a Lie algebra
> - $\R^{n \times n}$ together with the commutator $[A, B] = AB - BA$ is a Lie algebra.
> - In general in any associative algebra we can construct a Lie algebra from it by using the commutator as the Lie bracket.
