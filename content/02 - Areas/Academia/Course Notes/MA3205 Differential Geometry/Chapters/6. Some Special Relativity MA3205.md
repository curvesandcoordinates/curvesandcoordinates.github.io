## Newtownian Mechanics

A partical of mass $m$ is represented in newtonian mechanics as a curve $\alpha: I \to E \cong \R^{3}$. Using this physical properties are given by the curve, like:

- Velocity: $v = \alpha'$
- Momentum: ${} p = vm = \alpha' \cdot m {}$
- Accelaration: $a = v' = \alpha''$
- Force acting on $\alpha$: $F = \frac{d}{dt}(m \alpha') = m \alpha'' + v\frac{d}{dt}m$ which in the constant mass case simplifies to $m \alpha'' = F$

In this context we can construct a Space-Time $\R \times E \cong \R^{4}$. In this context the curve actually becomes a _world line_ which is a 1-dim submanifold (which can be thought of as the graph of $\alpha$) with the property the the projection onto the first component (time) is a diffeomorphism (so that we cant be in 2 spaces at the same time).

Problems with this Setup:

- There is no Limit to how quickly I can go
- There is an absolute notion of being at rest, which physically is wrong since there is no absolute center of the universe.
  This means that this whole theory breaks down at high speeds.

Eventhough it has these problems it can quite nicely describe some complex phenomena:

### The Doppler Effect

Assume we hae a listener $B$ at a fixed poition and a source of sound $A$ moving at speed $v$ emitting sound with frequency $f$ (in a stationary medium with speed of sound $c$)

> [!claim]
> Then we can claim that $B$ percieves the sound with frequency $\tilde f = f \frac{c}{c-v}$

`\begin{proof}`
$A$ emits a maximum at distance $d$. It arrives at $B$ exactly $\frac{d}{c}$ seconds later. The next maximum gets emitted $\frac{1}{f}$ seconds later at $A$ at a distance $d - \frac{v}{f}$ so the next maximum arrives at $B$ after
$\frac{1}{f} + \frac{\frac{d-v}{f}}{c} = \frac{d}{c} + \frac{c-f \frac{v}{f}}{cf}$The time difference therfore is $\frac{c-v}{cf}$ meaning the frequency arriving at $B$ is given by the reciprocal
`\end{proof}`

The interesting thing is that only slightly tweaking this setup changes many things completely.

> [!claim]
> Assuming $B$ is emitting and $A$ is listening.  then the frequency $A$ is getting is $\tilde f = f \frac{c+v}{c}$

`\begin{proof}`
If $A$ detects a maximum at distance $d$ is must have originated there $\frac{d}{c}$ seconds before. The next one gets detected $\frac{1}{\tilde f}$ seconds later at some distance $\tilde d = d - \frac{v}{\tilde f}$. Thus it got emitted $\frac{1}{f}$ seconds after the first and $\frac{ \tilde d}{c}$ seconds before the second detection.
$\frac{1}{\tilde f} + \frac{d}{c} = \frac{ \tilde d}{c} + \frac{1}{f}$
Plugging in $\tilde d$ and solving for $\tilde f$ we get
$\tilde f = f \frac{c+v}{c}$
`\end{proof}`

> [!idea] What is not symmetrical in these situations
> This is unusual since the experiment is mainly symmetric in its setup, but apparently it matters who moves towards who. The only difference results from who moves in the surrounding medium. Essentially we are changing the speed of sound, the second scenraio turns into the first if we introduce a second speed of sound $\tilde c = c + v$ then in our second situation is $\tilde f = f \frac{ \tilde c}{\tilde c-v}$

When working with light this effect doesnt even show up. This would mean that accounting for the medium doesnt really work. In this situation Lorentz proposed time dilation in the case of light where we can not measure this discrepancy): Slow down emission by $\lambda$ for a moving emitter and speed up perception by $\lambda$ for a moving reciever.

This way we would get in the first case $\frac{1}{\lambda} \frac{c}{c-v}$ and $\lambda \frac{c+v}{c}$ in the second case.
Wanting the same lambda to account for both situations we get the equation

$\lambda^{2} = \frac{c^{2}}{(c-v)(c+v)}$
assuming that the time dilation factor is positive we get
$\lambda = \frac{1}{1-\sqrt{\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}}}$
Then in both cases we have $\tilde f = f \sqrt{\frac{c+v}{c-v}}$

## Minkowski Space Time

We will now look at a Lorentz manifold isometric to $\R^{4}_{1}$ (meaning $\R^{4}$ with inner product $\innprod{x}{y}=-x_{0}y_{0} + \sum\limits_{i=1}^{3} x_{i}y_{i}$). I f we look at time-like vectors in the same connected component of the time-cone the unit-vectors form one half of a two-sheeted hyperboloid.
This means that $v$ then satisfies the equation $-v_{0}^{2} + v_{1}^{2} + v_{2}^{2} + v_{3}^{2} = - 1$
Thus we can set $cosh (\phi) = v_{0}$ and $sinh(\phi) = \sqrt{v_{1}^{2} + v_{2}^{2} + v_{3}^{2}}$ for a unique hyperbolic angle.

> [!definition]
> Given a time-like vector field, it naturally singles out one of the two halfs of the light cone in every tangential space and we call a Loretnz manifold with such a vector field **time oriented**

> [!idea] Orientation
> We have a light-cone in every tangential space. If we want a nowhere-vanishing vector field of only light vectors on our manifold we know that this vector field must always point into one of the connected components of the light cone, since for the vector field to flip orientation we need to cross $0$ which goes against being non-vanishing.

> [!definition]
>
> - A Minkowski space time $M$ is a $4$-dimensional time oriented Lorentz manifold that is isometric to $\R^{4}_{1}$
> - An intertial coordinate system is a time orientation preserving isometry $\phi: M \to \R^{4}_{1}$
> - A Material particle in $M$ is a time-like future pointing curve $\alpha:I \to M$ with $\norm{\alpha'} = 1$
> - A light-like particle is a future pointing null geodesic $\gamma:I \to M$ (meaning a geodesic going through $0$)
>
> A particle that is moving geodesically is said to be freely falling (meaning moving under the influence of gravity only).

> [!remark] Useful Terminology and Concepts
> When given an intertial coordinate system $\xi$ we know that this is simply an isometry. Given a particle in $M$ say
> $\alpha: I \to M$
> we can describe the $\xi$-time of $\alpha$ as
> $t = t(s) = \xi_{0}(\alpha(s))$
> and the vector
> $\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1} \circ \alpha(s)  \\ \xi_{2} \circ \alpha(s) \\ \xi_{3} \circ \alpha(s)\end{pmatrix}$
> as the $\xi$-position of $\alpha$.
>
> The time function $t(s)$ is invertible since $\alpha$ is time-casual. We will denote this inverse by $s = u(t)$ and then the curve
> $\tilde \alpha(t) = \begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1} \circ \alpha \circ u(t) \\ \xi_{2} \circ \alpha \circ u(t) \\ \xi_{3} \circ \alpha \circ u(t)\end{pmatrix}$
> is called the $\xi$-associated Newtonian particle of $\alpha$, which is the particle observerd from the perspective of $\xi$ including the local $\xi$-time.

> [!lemma] Light Like Particles
> If $\zeta$ is an inertial coordinate system and ${} \gamma$ a light-like partical, then its associated Newtonian particle $\tilde\gamma$ is a straight line in $\R^{3}$ with speed $1$

`\begin{proof}`
In the inertial coordinate system $\zeta$ our curve $\gamma$ mus be given by an affine map since it must be a null-geodesic on $M$ and due to the isometry property $\zeta \circ \gamma$ must correspond to a null geodesic on $\R^{4}_{1}$, so an affine map. This gives us the components
$\zeta_{i}(\gamma(s)) = a_{i}s + b_{i}$
and in total
$\zeta\circ \gamma(s) = \begin{pmatrix}a_{0} \\ a_{1} \\ a_{2} \\a_{3}\end{pmatrix}s + \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ b_{1} \\ b_{2} \\ b_{3}\end{pmatrix}$
meaning the projection to the last three components is a stright line.
We can find the local expression of time by looking at the $0$th coordinate and noticing that
$t(s) = \zeta_{0}(\gamma(s)) = a_{0} s$
Now taking the derivative we get that
$\frac{d \gamma}{ds} = \sum\limits_{i} a_{i} \del_{i} = \frac{dt}{ds} \del_{0} + \sum\limits_{i=1}^{3} \frac{d \left( \zeta \circ \gamma(s) \right)}{ds} \del_{i}$
Since $\frac{dt}{ds}$ is positive we can calulate that if we reparametrize with $u(t)$ that we get that
$\left| \frac{d\tilde\gamma}{dt} \right| = \frac{\left| \frac{d\tilde\gamma}{ds} \right|}{\frac{dt}{ds}} = 1$
`\end{proof}`

> [!lemma] Speed of Material Particle
> If $\xi$ is an intertial coordinate system and $\alpha$ a material particle then its associated Newtonian particle $\tilde \alpha$ has speed
> $v = \left| \frac{d\tilde\alpha}{dt} \right| = \tanh \phi$
> with $\phi$ being the (Lorentz) angle between $\alpha'$ and the time-like coordinate axis. The proper time $\tau$ of $\alpha$ and its $\xi$-time are related by the non-negative factor
> $\frac{dt}{d\tau} = \cosh \phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}$

`\begin{proof}`
Firstly we have a unique angle $\phi$ given by
$- \innprod{\del_{0}}{\alpha'} = \cosh \phi$
Given this result we can explicitly express $\alpha' = \sum\limits \frac{d(\xi_{i} \circ \alpha)}{d \tau} \del_{i}$ giving us
$\cosh \phi = - \innprod{\alpha'}{\del_{0}} = - \innprod{\del_{0}}{\del_{0}} \frac{d \left( \xi_{0} \circ \alpha \right)}{d \tau} = \frac{dt}{d\tau}$
`\end{proof}`

## Relativistic Phenomena

### Addition of Velocity in the Relativistic Sense

Given an inertial coordinate system $\xi$ assume that there is a freely falling space ship $s$ with relative speed $\tanh \phi_{p}$ relative to $s$ in the plane spanned by $s$ and $\xi_{0}$. We can now ask the question at which speed an observer in $\xi$ sees the projectile?

Since our space-time is isomorphic to $\R^{4}_{1}$ we can in fact identify different tangent spaces (similiar to $\R^{n}$) by parallel transport along geodesics. The direction of the projectile relative to the observer is just at an angle $\phi = \phi_{s} + \phi_{2}$

Using hyperbolic identities we get
$\tanh \phi = \frac{\tanh \phi_{s} + \tanh \phi_{2}}{1 + \tanh \phi_{s} \cdot \tanh \phi_{2}}$
giving us
$v = \frac{v_{1} + v_{2}}{1 + v_{1} v_{2}}$
This effect only appears when the velocities arent too big, which is why newtonian thinking still works in most situations.

We will now consider some “paradoxes” of this system. They arent really paradoxes but rather unintuitive consequences.

### Moving Clocks run Slower

> [!example] Twin Paradox
> The twins Alice and Bob separate at their 21st birthday. While bob stays at free fall Alice departs at constant speed $v = \frac{24}{25}$ for seven years to then return symmetrically for another seven years. upon arrival she is thus $35$ years old. But Bob is already $71$.
> The reason for this is that in Bob’s inertial system the turning point gives a right triangle with hypotenuse of $7$, meaning for him the event takes place after $\frac{7}{\sqrt{1 - (\frac{24}{25})^{2}}}= 25$ years. Symmetry then gives that he is $21 + 25 + 25 = 71$ years when the twins reunite.

Free fall is the slowest way to get from one point to another: A material particle moving from $p$ to $q$ has propert time less or equal to $pq$ with equality if and only if it is freely falling.

Another phenomenon we need to consider is Lorentz contraction. Assume we have two freely falling material particles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ parallel to each other (meaning contextually they see each other at rest). One can think of them as a rod the length of which is observed from both of them as their mutual distance in any rest-space for them. This length $L$ of this rod is called the restlength.

For an observer $\omega$ with associated interial coordinate system $\xi$ who moves in direction of the rods axis at speed $v$ the length of the rod $L_\omega$ is different since we can assume that at $t = \xi_{0} = 0$ that $\omega(0) = \alpha(0) = O$. Let $p$ be the point on $\beta$ that $\alpha$ considers simultaneous. Then the length $\overline{OP} = L$. If $Q$ is the point on $\beta$ that $\omega$ considers simultaneous then the triangle $OPQ$ forms a right triangle so
$L_{\omega} = \overline{OQ} = \frac{\overline{OP}}{\cosh \phi} = \sqrt{1- v^{2}} L$
where the last equality is given by $\sqrt{1 - \tanh^{2} x} = \sqrt{\frac{\cosh^{2} x - \sinh^{2} x}{\cosh^{2} x}} = \frac{1}{\cosh x}$

meaning that the length observed by $\omega$ is shorter that $L$ by the factor $\sqrt{1 - v^{2}}$

> [!example] Einstein Train
> Think of a $200$m train in a $100$m station. If the train passes the station at $v = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}$ the trains conductor sees the station with length $100 \cdot \sqrt{1- \frac{3}{4}} = 50$m while the station master will see the train with a length of $100$m exactly fitting his station. This phenomenon is usually refered to as Einstein train.

Here is a puzzle:
Assume the station is a dead-end station (with a very solid stopper). The conductor sees the station as being 50m long so hitting the stopper would leave 3/4 of his train outside the station. The station master sees the train as being 100m long so he believes the last carriage will enter the station. What will happen?

Of course if the train was able to “stop instantaneously” it would come to halt with half the train outside, but that is not very physical. In fact the question sheds light on an interesting aspect of relativity: information transport. Information can travel with light speed at most. So, the fact that the front of the train has hit the stopper will reach the rear of the train only after it has passed the station entrance, thus the station master is sort of right. The train wreck will be completely inside the station (this of course ignores all possible deformations done to the stopper).
