# General Relativity

**Previous**: [[6. Some Special Relativity MA3205]]
**Next Chapter**: [[8. Jacobi Fields MA3205]]

The model given to us in special relativity explains many interesting phenomena that can be observed via experimentation. The only issue with it is that we intertial reference frames to work in special relativity. This means no accelerated situations can occur, which limits us a lot. Given that fundamental forces like gravity apply acceleration we need to somehow incorperate this into our theory why maintaining the interesting and experimentally proven aspects of special relativity. This is achieved in general relativity.

The theory is built up from a set of axioms that will be taken for granted. This might seem wrong to do, as of now all the predictions made by this model have withstood testing on a macroscopic level, meaning that the axioms need not be questioned yet. This is a problem on the microscopic quantum level, where we seem to have different results and different axioms that make sense.

> [!info] Axioms of General Relativity
>
> - Special Relativity has to be a local approximation. Meaning every tangent space to a manifold in this context needs to be a Minkowsky space-time, which leads us to considering Lorentz manifolds.
> - A free falling particle is one moving along a geodesic. This results from the socalled equivalence principal which in its simplest terms states that someone in a sealed capsule in space is unable to qualitatively tell whether he is being accelerated by gravity or not .
> - Energy, [[Impuls| impulse]] are always conserved. This information is contained int eh divergence free stress energy tensor $T$ (which can be thought of as a tensorial distribution of the mass and energy contained in a closed system).

The second postulate gives us some intuition on the cenral point of general relativity. If free falling is the same as geodesic movement, then the only way gravity can be explained is that a mass curves space-time in such a ways that geodesic movement isnt straight but the straight path under the influence of gravity. In other words: the force of gravity is expressed through curvature of space-time.
This means the curvature tensor $R$ has to be related to the stress-energy-tensor $T$ in some way, since curvature modifies the distribution of mass through geodesic movement (usually called gravity). Since in a Vacuum (meaning $T = 0$) we can still have gravity we know that $R$ and $T$ cant be exactly equal to one another.
A good candidate to consider to describe $T$ with is the Ricci curvature. The only problem being that Ricci tensor isnt divergence free, but that can be fixed, giving us:

> [!definition] Gravitational Tensor (Einstein Tensor)
> The gravitational or Einstein tensor is defined as
> $G := Ricc - \frac{1}{2} S g$

> [!lemma]
> The gravitational tensor is a symmetric, divergence free $(0,2)$-tensor.

`\begin{proof}`
Both $Ricc$ and $g$ are symmetric $(0,2)$-tensors, so $G$ is too. Moreover we have calculated before that $div(Ricc) = \frac{1}{2}dS = \frac{1}{2} div(S g)$ meaning that $G$ is divergence free.
`\end{proof}`

The model is now completed by stating connecting all these tensors

> [!definition] Einstein Field Equations
> The Einstein Field Equations are
> $G = 8 \pi T = 8 \pi \left( Ricc - \frac{1}{2} S g \right)$

- We can also motivate the field equations by considering a sort of “newtonian limit”
- $G$ is in no way unique. We can always add a $\lambda g$ to it. In physics the experimentally supportet $\lambda$ is called the cosmological constant.
- $G$ and $Ricc$ carry the same information. Their relation is symmetric when writing $S = C(Ricc)$ we get $G = Ricc - \frac{1}{2} C(Ricc)g \quad Ricc = G - \frac{1}{2} C(G)g$
- For a physically meaningful model one usually requires at least that there are no closed time-like curves.

## Schwarzschild Geometry

We want to now derive the explicit Einstein field equations for a situation. We will model a centel star in a vacuum. We will confine the model with the following assumptions:

1. Static (Not Changing over time)
2. Rotationally Symmetric and the central mass is assumed spherical
3. In Vacuum (The central mass is not part of the model and no more relevant sources of gravity exist)
4. asymptotically flat

Given the assumptions we can write our metric as a quadrativ form which means this quadratic form acts as a norm:
$s(v) = g(v, v)$
The static nature of our setup gives us the existance of a time-like vector field whoose flows gives isometries. Using integral curves as coordinate lines we get a representation of our space-time as
$N = \R \times \tilde N$
which has a metric
$s(dt, dx) = A(x) dt^{2} + \tilde s(x)$
for simplicity we will choose $\tilde N$ to be isomorphic to $\R^{3}$.
Splitting off the time component and using the assumed radial symmetry we can rewrite this as
$s(dt, dr, v) = A(r) dt^{2} + B(r) dr^{2} + C(r) d \sigma^{2}$
where $\sigma$ is the metric on $S^{2}$. Via reparametrization we can find that $C(r) = r^{2}$ meaning in total our metric is
$s(t, r, v) = A(r) dt^{2} + B(r) dr^{2} + r^{2} d \sigma^{2}$

Our final assumtion of being in a vacuum gives us a vanishing stress-energy-tensor and thus a vanishing Ricci curvature. This together with the asymptotic flatness determine the metric almost uniquely.

In the end our metric is simply given by $A(r) = \frac{2M}{r} - 1$ and $B(r) = - \frac{1}{A(r)}$. Giving us a metric
$g = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{2M}{r} - 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & - \frac{r}{2M - r}  & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & r^{2}\end{pmatrix}$
written in the local basis of $\set{dt, dr, d \sigma}$.
An interesting consequence of this is that at the distance of $r = 2M$ our model breaks down. Normally this point will never be reached as it is in the star, but for say a supermassive black hole this distance is reachable and is called the Schwarzschild Radius.

## Orbits

Using the information about the metric we can now in general calculate orbits in this model. We know that freely falling bodies are geodesic, meaning any orbiting object needs to have a geodesic trajectory. This amounts to solving the differential equation that is the geodesic equation.

> [!example]+ Orbits in the Schwarzschild Model
> The geodesic equation was
> $0 = \nabla_{\gamma'} \gamma' = \sum\limits_{k} \left(\frac{d^{2}x^{k} \circ \gamma}{dt^{2}} + \sum\limits_{ij} \Gamma_{ij}^{k} \frac{d(x^{i} \circ \gamma)}{dt} \frac{d(x^{j} \circ \gamma)}{dt}\right) \del_{k}$
> or with $X = \gamma'$
> $0 = \nabla_{X}X = \sum\limits_{i} X^{i} \sum\limits_{k} \left( \del_{i} X^{k} + \sum\limits_{j} \Gamma_{ij}^{k} X^{j} \right) \del_{k} = \sum\limits_{k} \left( X(X^{k}) + \sum\limits_{ij} \Gamma_{ij}^{k} X^{j}X^{i}\right) \del_{k}$
> Given the gauss fields are orthogonal we can simplify the christoffel symbols.
>
> $$$\begin{align*}
> \Gamma_{kk}^{k} &= g^{kk} \del_{k} g_{kk} \\
> \Gamma^{k}_{ii} &= -g^{kk} \frac{1}{2} \del_{k} g_{ii} \quad  k \neq i \\
> \Gamma^{k}_{ki} &= g^{kk} \frac{1}{2} \del_{i} g_{kk}
> \end{align*}$$
> pluggin this in we get
> $$0 
> = \sum\limits_{k}  X(X^{k}) + \left(\sum\limits_{i} \Gamma_{ii}^{k} X^{i}X^{i} + 2\sum\limits_{i, j \neq k} \Gamma_{ik}^{k} X^{i}X^{k}\right) \del_{k} 
> =\sum\limits_{k}  X(X^{k}) + g^{kk} \left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i} \del_{k} g_{ii} X^{i}X^{i} + \del_{k} g_{kk} X^{k}X^{k} + \sum\limits_{i, j \neq k} \del_{i} g_{kk} X^{i}X^{k}\right) \del_{k} $$
> or
>
> $$\sum\limits_{k} \frac{1}{2} \sum\limits_{i} \del_{k}g_{ii} X^{i} X^{i} \del_{k} = \sum\limits_{k} g_{kk} X(X^{k}) + \sum\limits_{i} X^{i} \del_{i} g_{kk} X^{k}(\del_{k}) = \sum\limits_{k} X(g_{kk} X^{k}) \del_{k}$$
> giving us 4 equations
> $$\begin{align*}
