- A non-degenerate, symmetric, bilinear form $\innprod{\cdot}{\cdot}$ is linear in both slots, $\innprod{x}{y} = \innprod{y}{x}$ holds for all $v, w$ and $\innprod{v}{w} = 0$ for all $w$ implies $v = 0$. One can understand non-degeneracy as the representation matrix of the bilinear form having no 0 Eigenvalues.
- The number of negative eigenvalues (or the dimension of the maximal negative definite subspace) is called the index.
- Non-degenerate bilinear forms still furnish a pairing between a vector space and its dual: $v \mapsto \innprod{v}{\cdot} \in V^{*}$ is a canonical isomorphism.
- We will adopt the following names from special relativity:
  - A vector $X$ is called space-like iff $\innprod{X}{X} > 0$ or $X = 0$
  - A vector $X$ is called light-like iff $\innprod{X}{X} = 0$
  - A vector $X$ is called time-like iff $\innprod{X}{X} < 0$
  - The set $L = \set{X \in V \midline \innprod{X}{X} = 0}$ is often called the light cone
  - A real vector space $V$ with $\dim V \geq 2$ together with a scalar product of index $1$ is called a Minkowsky Space
    - Consider $\R^{3}$ with $\innprod{x}{y} = -x_{0}y_{0} + \sum\limits_{i=1}^{2}x_{i} y_{i} = x^{T} \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 & 0  \\ 0 & 1 & 0  \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} y$

The notion of a symmetric bilinear form transfers directly to tensor fields on manifolds

> [!definition] metric tensor
> A metric tensor on a manifolds $M$ is a non-degenerate, symmetric $(0, 2)$-tensor field of constant index. A semi-Riemannian manifold $M$ is a manifold with a metric tensor $g$. It its index is $0$ the manifold is called Riemannian. If the Index is $1$ and $\dim M > 1$, $M$ is called a Lorentz manifold.

> [!remark] Notation for components of the metric tensor
> The metric tensor is usually denoted by $g$ and its tensor components are written as $g_{ij} = \innprod{\del_{i}}{\del_{j}}$. In every point $p \in M$ then $g_{p}: T_{p} M \times T_{p} M \to \R$ is an inner product in our sense
> But thinking of the inne product of two given Vector fields we can also see our Metric / Inner Product as a map on Functions defined by
> $\innprod{X}{Y} =g(X, Y): p \mapsto g_p(X_{p}, Y_{p})$
> Thus we will use this as a function often.

> [!example] Semi-Riemannina Manifolds
>
> - $\R^{n}$ with the canonical inner product on every tangential space is a Riemannian manifold
> - Any Submanifold of a Riemannian Manifold is a Riemannina Manifold via restricting the scaler product at every point via the inclusion $T_{p}N\subset T_{p}M$ for $N \subset M$ submanifold. Formally we can describe this as the pullback on the metric via the inclusion map $j: N \to M$.
> - Given $\R^{4}$ with inner product $\innprod{x}{y} =  -x_{0}y_{0} + \sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}x_{i} y_{i}$. This is a Minkowski space and a Lorentz Manifold. The hyperboloid $H = \set{v \in \R^{4} \midline \innprod{v}{v} = -1, v_{0} > 0}$
>   is a Submanifold of $\R^{4}$ and (via a picture we can see that) it turns out the be Riemannian.

> [!definition] Isometries and Lengths of Curves on Riemannian Manifolds
> Two Semi-Riemannian manifolds $M$ and $N$ are called isometric if there exists a diffeomorphism ${} \phi: M \to N {}$ such that$\phi^{*}(g_{N}) = g_{M}$
>
> If $\gamma: I \to \R^{n}$ is a regular curve, its length on $[a, b] \subset I$ is given by $L_{a, b}(\gamma) = \int_{a}^{b} \sqrt{\innprod{\gamma'(t)}{\gamma'(t)}}dt$
> pulling this up to curves on manifolds we can define for a given curve $\delta: I \to M$ on a Riemanning Manifold to be $L_{a, b}(\gamma) = \int_{a}^{b} \sqrt{g_{\delta(t)}\left( \gamma'(t), \gamma'(t) \right)}dt$

We are working to define curvatures and in $\R^{n}$ curvature is calculated by means of derivatives. Given a Curve $\gamma: I \to \R^{n}$ is a smooth curve with $\norm{\gamma'} = 1$ then the curvature is given by $\kappa = \norm{\gamma''}$. These curves are thus the objects we will want to use to calculate curvature for.

> [!note]
> In the manifold setting taking the second derivative of a curve amounts to taking the derivative of a tangent vector field in the direction of itsself. This motivated the idea of taking the derivative in direction of a vector field.

**More General:** Take $V, W \in \Gamma(T \R^{n})$ we can calculate the directional derivative $D_{V}(W) = \sum\limits V^{i}\del_{i}(W) \in \Gamma(T \R^{n})$. If we now consider a non-open submanifold $M$ of $\R^{n}$ then generically $D_{V}(W)$ for $V, W \in \Gamma(TM)$ is no longer tangential.However we can project back onto $M$ and get a tangent vector field in that way: $\nabla_{V} W := \pi(D_{V}(W))$

> [!idea] Great Circle
> Consider the Great Circle on $S^{2}$ we can think of a tangent vector field on it. The directional derivative results in normal directions at every point, which projected down onto the circle gives us the $0$-Vector everywhere, meaning it has $0$ curvature at every point. This gives us the idea that on the sphere the great circle is the “straightest way to move” in terms of a circle. This is all very vague and will be complicated when moving to general manifolds because of the lacking ambient space but the idea is this.

If we want to axiomatize this idea we need to identify its properties:

1. This operation is $\R$-Bilinear and even $\mathcal{F}(M)$-linear in the lower slot, meaning $\nabla_{fV}W = f \nabla_{V}W$
2. It has a Leibnitz-Rule in the second argument.
   There are two more properties that are way less obvious but important
3. We have that $[V,W](f) = (D_{V}(W) - D_{W}(V))(f)$, since $\begin{align*} [V, W](f) &=  V\circ W(f) - W \circ V(f) \\
   &=  V(D_{w}f) - W(D_{V} f) \\
   &=  \sum\limits_{i,j} V^{j}(\del_{j}(W_{i}) \del_{i}(f) + W^{i} \del_{j} \del_{i}(f)) - \sum\limits_{i,j} W^{i}(\del_{i}(V_{j}) \del_{j}(f) + V^{j} \del_{i} \del_{j}(f)) \\
   &= \sum\limits_{i,j} V^{j} \del_{j} W^{i} \del_{i} f - \sum\limits_{i,j} W^{i} \del_{i} V^{j} \del_{j} f \\
   &= (D_{V}(W) - D_{W}(V))(f)
   \end{align*}$
4. $U(\innprod{V}{W}) = \innprod{D_{U} V}{W} + \innprod{V}{D_{U} W}$

Now pulling this up to the level of manifolds we use these properties as a definition:

> [!definition] Connection and Levi-Cevita Connection
> A Connection on a Manifold $M$ is a map $D: \Gamma(TM) \times \Gamma(TM) \to \Gamma(TM)$ written as $D(V, W) = D_{V}W$ with the following properties:
>
> - $D$ is $\mathcal{F}(M)$-linear in the first argument
> - $D$ is $\R$-linear in the second argument
> - $D_{V}(f W) = V(f) W  + f D_{V}(W)$ for all $V, W \in \Gamma(TM)$ and $f \in \mathcal{F}(M)$
>
> If moreover $M$ is semi-Riemannian and if the connection satisfies for all $U, V, W \in \Gamma(TM)$
>
> - $[V, W] = D_{V}W - D_{W} V$
> - $U \left( \innprod{V}{W} \right) = \innprod{D_{U} V}{W} + \innprod{V}{D_{U} W}$
>
> then it is called Levi-Cevita-Connection and one writes $\nabla_{V} W = D_{V}W$

> [!lemma] Uniqueness of Levi-Cevita and Formula
> The Levi-Civita-connection $\nabla$ on a semi-Riemannian manifold $M$ is unique and given by the Kozul-Formula: $2 \innprod{\nabla_{V}W}{U} = V \innprod{W}{U} + W \innprod{U}{V} - U \innprod{V}{W} - \innprod{V}{[W, U]} + \innprod{W}{[U, V]} + \innprod{U}{[V, W]}$

`\begin{proof}`
Assume $D$ is a Levi-Civita-Connection, then the Kozul Formula holds by applying 4) and 5) to the RHS of the claim and everything but two terms cancel. This is easiliy calculable. So every Levi-Civita-connection satisfies this formula and since $\innprod{X}{\cdot} \mapsto X$ is an isomorphism the Levi-Civita connection needs to be unique if it exists.

> [!example] Levi-Cevita Connection on Lie Groups
> A Metric on a Lie group $G$ is called left invariant (or right-invariant) if left (right) multiplication is an isometry. If it is both left and right invariant we call it bi-invariant.
> On a connected Lie group for a left invariant metric $g = \innprod{\cdot}{\cdot}$ the following statements are equivalent
>
> 1. $g$ is right invariant (thus bi-invariant)
> 2. $g$ is $Ad(G)$ invariant
> 3. $\innprod{X}{[Y, Z]} = \innprod{[X, Y]}{Z}$ for all $X, Y, Z \in \mathcal{g}$
> 4. $\nabla_{X} Y = \frac{1}{2} [X, Y]$ for all $X, Y \in \mathcal{g}$
>
> `\begin{proof}`
> Of course a left invariant metric is also right invariant and thus bi-invariant if and only if it is $Ad(G)$ invariant, thus $1 \iff 2$. For $2 \iff 3$ we first note that $3$ is equivalent to $\innprod{[X, Z]}{X} = 0$due to polarization. Now replace $X$ with $X-Y$ and get $0 = \innprod{[X-Y, Z]}{X-Y} = \innprod{[X, Z]}{X} - \innprod{[Y, Z]}{X} - \innprod{[X, Z]}{Y} + \innprod{[Y, Z]}{Y}$ whcih can be simplified to $\innprod{X}{[Y, Z]} = \innprod{[X, Y]}{Z}$
> Now also remember that the flow of left invariant vector fields is just the one-parameter subgroup and we thus can write $[X, Y] = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{Ad_{\exp(tX)}Y - Y}{t}$
> if our metric is $Ad(G)$ invariant, the function $f(t) = \innprod{Ad_{exp(tX)}(Y)}{Ad_{exp(tX)}(Y)}$ is constant. $0 = \lim_{t \to 0}\frac{f(t) - f(0)}{t} = \lim_{t \to 0}\frac{\innprod{Ad_{exp(tX)}(Y)}{Ad_{exp(tX)}(Y)} - \innprod{Y}{Y}}{t} = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\innprod{Ad_{exp(tX)}(Y) - Y}{Ad_{exp(tX)}(Y) + Y}}{t} = 2 \innprod{[X, Y]}{Y}$
> This already give us the converse in a small neighbourgood of the identity: The Above function must be constant in that neighbourhood and thus the metric must be $Ad(G)$ invariant for elements that can be expressed as image under the exponential map.
> For this to hold globally (on a connected Lie Group) we need the fact that every element can be written as a finite product of elementsfrom that neighbourhood.
> For $3 \iff 4$ we use the Kozul formula. Since the inner products of left invariant vectorfields are constant it reduces to:
> $2 \innprod{\nabla_{X} Y}{Z} = - \innprod{X}{[Y, Z]} + \innprod{Y}{[Z, X]} + \innprod{Z}{[X, Y]}$by $3$ we know that two terms cancel out so we get $4$.
> Assuming $4$ we have $\innprod{X}{[Y, Z]} = 2 \innprod{X}{\nabla_{Y}Z} = -2 \innprod{\nabla_{Y} X}{Z}$ since $\innprod{X}{Z} = 0 \implies 0 = Y \innprod{X}{Z} = \innprod{\nabla_{Y} X}{Z} + \innprod{X}{\nabla_{Y} Z}$
> so finally we get that $\innprod{X}{[Y, Z]} = -2 \innprod{\nabla_{Y} X}{Z} = - \innprod{[Y, X]}{Z} = \innprod{[X, Y]}{Z}$
> `\end{proof}`

> [!definition] Christoffel Symbols
> Given a coordinate system, the coefficients $\Gamma_{ij}^{k}$ of the derivatives of the Gauss fields $\del_{i}$ are called Christoffel symbols:
> $\nabla_{\del_{i}} \del_{j} = \sum\limits_{k} \Gamma_{ij}^{k} \del_{k}$
> **These are not tensor components eventhough they might look like it**

Note that in our situation of the Levi-Cevita Connection we have some symmetries, namely $\Gamma_{ij}^{k} = \Gamma_{ji}^{k}$ since $\nabla_{\del_{i}}\del_{j} - \nabla_{\del_{j}} \del_{i} = [\del_{i}, \del_{j}] = 0$
Using the Christoffel symbols to calculate the covariant derivative of any vectorfield simplifies to
$\nabla_{\del_{i}}(W) = \sum\limits_{k} \left( \del_{i}(W^{k}) + \sum\limits_{j} \Gamma_{ij}^{k} W^{j} \right) \del_{k}$

> [!definition]
> Let $V$ be a vector field. For a tensor field $A$ the covariant derivative $D_{V}(A)$ is a unique tensor derivation such that
> $D_{V}(A)(f) = V(f) \quad D_{V}(A)(X) = \nabla_{V}X$

> [!definition]
> The covariant differential of an $(r, s)$-Tensor field $A$ is the $(r, s+1)$-tensor field $DA$ such that
> $DA(\omega_{1}, \ldots , \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s+1}) = D_{v_{s+1}} A(\omega_{1}, \ldots , \omega_{r}, v_{1}, \ldots, v_{s})$
