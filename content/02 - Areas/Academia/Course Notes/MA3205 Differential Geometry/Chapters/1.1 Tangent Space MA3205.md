# Tangent Space

**Next Chapter**: [[1.2 Partition of Unity MA3205]]

> I have left out the general contruction of a Manifold but have given my own account in [[Formal Construction of Real Manifolds]] which is based on this course and

In the process of defining familiar objects in a manifold-context we come to looking at the differentials of maps. We have defined what it means for a map to be differentiable, but what is this differential that we are allowed to calculate.

In more familiar settings we consider maps $g: \R^{m} \to \R^{n}$ and know that the total differential at a point $q \in \R^{m}$ is given by the linear map

$$
D_{q}g: \R^{m}\to \R^{n}, \quad v \mapsto \sum\limits_{i} v_{i} \at{\frac{\del g}{\del x_{i}}}{q} = J_{q}(g)v
$$

This construction has some weird properties:

- $D_{q}g$ has as a domain always $\R^{n}$ even if our original function was limited by say $g: U \to V$
- The same is true for its image

This gives us cause to think of the domain of $D_{q}g$ as a somehow separate space. Namely we consider it the tangent space at the point $q$. We will now try and replicate these tangent vectors on a manifold.

For this note first that for $n = 1$ the above situation becomes the directional derivate by defining

$$
\at{\del_{v}g}{p} = D_{q}g(v)
$$

We can view this object in two different ways:

- $\R^{n} \to \R \quad v \mapsto \at{\del_{v} g}{p}$ ← This changes the direction we are looking at but keeps the function the same
- $\mathcal{F}(\R^{n}) \to \R \quad g \mapsto \at{\del_{v} g}{p}$ ← This changes the functions and keeps a fixed direction to check

Using this let us try and define this directional derivative on our manifold. Given a point $p \in M$ with its coordinates defined on a nbhd of $p$ $\phi: U \to \R^{n}$ we define
$g = f \circ \phi^{-1}: \phi(U) \to \R$
then we can define

$$
\at{\del_{i} f}{p} := \at{\frac{\del f}{\del \phi_{i}}}{p} := \at{\frac{\del (f \circ \phi^{-1})}{\del x_{i}}}{p} = \at{\frac{\del g}{\del x_{i}}}{\phi(p)}
$$

This concept of a partial derivative of $f$ is useful but restricted in the fact that it is dependent on the choordinate system chosen. In general we find a directional dervative in any direction as a linear combination of these $\del_{i} f$.

To make the idea of a tangent vector seperate from the coordinates chose we will try to characterise them by a property that they have. Namely we will use the fact that a given vector describes a directional derivative in a direction of any function. So considering this operator property that a tangent vector at a point should have we come to the following definition

> [!definition] Tangent Vectors on a Manifolds
> Let $M$ be a manifold and $p \in M$. A tangential vector on $M$ at $p$ is a map
> $v: \mathcal{F}(M) \to \R$
> with the properties that
>
> 1. $v$ is $\R$-Linear: $v(\lambda f + \mu g) = \lambda v(f) + \mu v(g)$
> 2. $v$ satisfies the leibnitz rule: $v(f\cdot g) = v(f)g(p) + f(p) v(g)$

Connecting this to our concept from before we get that

> [!lemma] Gauss Vectors are Tangent Vectors
> We will name the partial derivatives from before “Gauss Vectors” and they are tangential vectors

`\begin{proof}`
To rove that they are tangential vectors we simply use the linearity of the partial derivative. Given $\lambda, \mu \in \R$ and $f, g \in \mathcal{F}(M)$ then
$\at{\del_i}{p}(\lambda f + \mu g) = \frac{\del \left( \lambda (f \circ \phi^{-1}) + \mu (g \circ \phi^{-1}) \right)}{\del x_{i}} = \lambda \cdot \at{\del_{i}}{p}(f) + \mu \cdot \at{\del_{i}}{p}(g)$
also by the usual leibniz property of partial derivatives we can show that they are tangential vectors.
`\end{proof}`

> [!theorem] Gauss Vectors are a Basis of $T_p M$
> If $M$ is an $n$-dim Manifold and $p \in M$, then $T_{p} M$ is an $n$-dim $\R$ vector space.
> If $\phi$ is a coordinate system in $p$, then the Gaus Vectors form a basis - the so called Gaus Basis - of $T_{p} M$

`\begin{proof}`
First note that we will treat the maps $\phi_{i}: U \to \R$ as maps from $M$ to $\R$ since (as we will see later) they can naturally be extended to such maps without changing the argument here.

First we show that the $\del_{i}$ are linearly independent. To this end consider the linear combination

$$
\mu = \sum\limits_{i} \lambda_{i} \del_{i}
$$

Then we can see that

$$
\mu(\phi_{j}) = \sum\limits_{i} \lambda_{i} \del_{i}(\phi_{j}) = \sum\limits_{i} \lambda_{i} \frac{\del}{}
$$

`\end{proof}`

We now want to justify the assumption that we had in the previous proof. Namely we want to show that however you extend a chart to all of $M$ the value of a tangential vector at a point $p$ doesn’t change.

> [!definition] Support
> We defined the support of a function $f: M \to R$ as the set
> $\supp(f) = \overline{\set{p \in M \midline f(p) \neq 0}}$

If $U \subset M$ and $p \in M$ then there exists a “bump function” $f: M \to \R$ with the following properties:

1. $0 \leq f \leq 1$
2. $\supp f \subset U$
3. $f \cong 1$ in a nbhd of $p$

We will now construct such a function by $h : \R \to \R$.

$$
h(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-\frac{1}{x^{2}}} \quad x > 0 \\ 0 \end{cases}
$$

It is well known that $h$ is smooth. We can use this to define

$$
\tilde{h}(t) := \int\limits_{0}^{t} h(x) h(x - 1) dx
$$

Which can be seen as a smooth roll-up from $0$ to a constant. Now finally construct:

$$
r(t) = \frac{\tilde{h}(1- \frac{t-\epsilon}{\epsilon})}{\tilde{h}(1)}
$$

This function satisfies what we wanted just on the real numbers. $r$ is constant $1$ between $0$ and $\epsilon$ and falls strictly monotonic to 0 between $\epsilon$ and $2 \epsilon$.
If $U$ is open containing $p$ we can choose $\epsilon$ such that all $x \in \R^{n}$ with $\norm{\phi(p) - x}^{2} \leq 3 \epsilon$ lie in $\phi(U)$. Then $f(q) := r(\norm{\phi(p) - \phi(q)}^{2})$ is a function with the desired properties.

Using this we can now show that extension don’t change the previous theorem.

> [!lemma] Locality of Tangential Vectors
> If $f, g \in \mathcal{F}(M)$ coincide on a nbhd $U$ of $p$, then all tangential vectors $v \in T_{p} M$ have $v(f) = v(g)$

`\begin{proof}`
Take a bump function $h$ around $p$ such that $\supp(h) \subset U$. Then we know $h(p) = 1$. Using this we get that

$$
0 = (f-g) h
$$

Now we simply apply
$0 = v((f-g)h) = v(f-g) h(p) + (f-g)(p) v(h) = v(f-g) = v(f) - v(g)$
since $h(p) = 1$ and $f-g$ coincide on $U$

`\end{proof}`
