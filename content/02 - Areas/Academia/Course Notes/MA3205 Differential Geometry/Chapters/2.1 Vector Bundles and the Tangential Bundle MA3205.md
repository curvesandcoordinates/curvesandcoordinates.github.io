# 2. Vector Bundles and the Tangential Bundle

**Previous**: [[1.3 Differential of Functions on Manifolds MA3205]]]
**Next**: [[2.2 Group actions, Flows and Integrale Curves MA3205]]

> [!definition] Vector Bundles
> A $k$-Vector bundle $(E, \pi)$ (or just $E$) over a manifold $M$ is a manifold $E$ together with a smooth map $\pi: E \to M$ such that:
>
> 1. For all $p \in M$, $\pi^{-1}(p)$ is a $k$-dim $\R$-Vector space.
> 2. For all $p \in M$ there exists an open nbhd $U \subset M$ of $p$ and a diffeomorphism $\phi: U \times \R^{k} \to \pi^{-1}(U) \subset E$ such that for all $q \in U: v \mapsto \phi(q, v)$ is a linear isomorphism $\R^{k} \to \pi^{-1}(q)$
>    In this setting we call $M$ the base of the bundle and $E$ the total space, $\pi$ its projection, $\pi^{-1}(p)$ the fiber over $p$ and $\phi$ the bundle chart.

> [!definition] The Tangential Bundle
> Let $M$ be a $n$-dim Manifold. We then define $TM := \biguplus_{p \in M} T_{p} M = \set{(p, v) \midline p \in M, v \in T_{p} M}$ is called the tangential bundle of $M$

> [!lemma] The Tangential Bundle is a Vector Bundle
> In a canonical way, $TM$ as defined above is a $n$-vector bundle over $M$.

`\begin{proof}`
Let $\phi: U \subset M \to R^{n}$ be a chart on $M$. Then set $\Phi: \pi^{-1}(U) \to \R^{n} \times \R^{n}, (p, v) = \left( p, \sum\limits v_{i} \del_{i} \right) \mapsto (\phi(p), v_{1}, \cdots, v_{n})$ then it is simple to see that this map is a bijection which covers $TM$. Now, one can show that asking $V \in TM$ to be open $\iff$ $\Phi(V \cap \pi^{-1}(U))$ is open in $\R ^{n} \times \R^{n}$ for all charts $\Phi$ on $M$, defines a Hausdorff topology with countable basis on $TM$.Moreover the charts $\Phi$ are compatible and are infact giving bundle charts.
`\end{proof}`

> [!definition] Smooth Sections
>
> 1. If $E$ is a $k$-vector bundle over $M$ then a smooth map $X: M \to E$ is called a smooth section in $E$ if $\pi \circ X = id$.
> 2. The set of all smooth secitons on $E$ is denoted by $\Gamma(E)$
> 3. A $k$-vector bundle is called trivial iff there are $k$ linear independent never-vanishing sections. (this is equivalent to saying that $E \cong M \times \R^{k}$)
> 4. A smooth section in $TM$ is called a tangential vector field on $M$
> 5. A vector field along a map $f: M \to N$ is a map $X: M \to TN$ such that $\pi \circ X = f$

A nowhere vanishing section gives us a vector on every point that is not equal to 0. So triviality can be seen as finding a consistenly smoothly varying basis for all elements of the bundle.

> [!remark] Tangential Vector Fields as Derivations
> Tangential vector fields are also called derivations, since $X \in \Gamma(TM)$ can be viewed as a map $X: \mathcal{F}(M) \to \mathcal{F}(M)$
> which is $\R$-linear and Leibniz. This is done by considering the tangential vectors as $T_{p}M \to \R$ so $X$ gives us for every function $f:M \to \R$ that $Xf: M \to \R, \quad p \mapsto X(p)(f)$

> [!example] Vector Field Examples
>
> - For smooth $f: M \to N$ and $v \in \Gamma(TM)$ the map $df(v): M \to TN$ by mapping $p \mapsto d_{p}f(v_{p}) \in T_{f(p)}N$
> - $M \times R^{k}$ is a trivial $k$-bundle
> - $TT^2$ (the tangent bundle of $T^{2}$) is trivial we can explicitly write down the two independent nowhere-vanishing vector fields

> [!example] Non-Triviality of $TS^n$ for even $n$
> For even $n$ the tangential bundle of $S^{n}$ is non-trivial. In general for $S^{n}$ there is not even one nowhere vanishing smooth section.

`\begin{proof}`

> [!idea]
> The idea for this proof is that we will assume that a nowere vanishing smooth section exists and normalize this. This normalised unit vector field can now be seen to move every point outwards[^2], giving us a bigger sphere. In the end we will calculate the volume of the sphere in multiple ways showing that they don’t agree.

Let $n$ be even. It suffices to show the statement for unit vector fields, since we can always normalize a non-vanishing vector field. Now for any compact set $X \in \R^{n+1}$ and any smooth vector field $v$ defined in a neighbourhood[^3], the maps $f_{t}(x) = x + tv(x)$ are smooth and directionally injective for a sufficiently small choice of $t$.[^4] The total differential of $f_{t}$ is a square matrix with entries linear in $t$. thus its determinant is a polynomial of $t$ of degree $n+1$ (and positive for sufficiently small $t$). The volume off $f_{t}(X)$ can be calculated as $V_{t}=\int_{X} |\det(D_{f_{t}}| d\lambda)$
this means that $V_{t}$ is a polynomial of $t$ of degree $n+1$.

Now assume that we have a smooth unit vector field $v$ on $S^{n}$. Then $\norm{p + t v(p)} = \sqrt{1 + t^2}$We set $X := \set{p \in \R^{n+1} \midline a \leq \norm{p} \leq b}$ for some $a < 1 < b$.
Extending $v$ onto $X$ is then simply done by setting $v(rp) := r \cdot v(p)$ for $a \leq r \leq b$. Now $f_{t}$ is defined on all of $X$ and maps $\del K_{r}(0) \mapsto \del K_{r \sqrt{1+t^{2}}}(0)$
For small enough $t$ we know that $D f_{t}$ is invertible. Then, open sets in $X$ are mapped to open sets. Thus $f_{t}(r S^{n}) \subset r \sqrt{1+t^{2}} S^{n}.$ Since it is also compact we can say that $f_{t}(r S^{n}) = \left(r \sqrt{1+t^{2}}\right) S^{n}$
Given all this we know that $f_{t}(X)$ is the set of points between two concentric spheres of radius $a \sqrt{1+ t^{2}}$ and $b \sqrt{1+ t^{2}}$. Therefore its volume is given by $\tilde{V_{t}} = \left(\sqrt{1+ t^{2}}\right)^{n+1} vol(X)$
Which clearly is not a polynomial function in $t$, thus cause a contradiction for $n$ even[^5].
`\end{proof}`

Note that for an odd $n$ it is simple to find such a vector  field by considering $v(p) := (p_{2}, - p_{1}, \cdots, p_{n}, -p_{n-1})$

> [!definition] Cotangential Bundle
> We define the Cotangential bundle as $TM^{\ast} := \bigcup_{p \in M} T_{p}M^{\ast}$ It is again a Vector bundle in the natural way.
> Let $\phi: U \to \R^{n}$ be a coordinate system with $\phi(p) = (x_{1}(p), x_{2}(p), \cdots, x_{n}(p))$. Then the parial derivatives $\del_{i} = \frac{\del}{\del x_{i}}$ form the Gaus basis for $T_{p}M$ in each $p \in U \subset M$. The corresponding dual basis is given by the differentials $dx_{i}$. Where we mean the differential defined for maps between manifolds. We will often denote this dual basis by $dx^{i}$ instead.

The statement of the dual basis is given by a simple calculation
$dx_{i}(\del_{j}) = \frac{\del (x_{i} \circ \phi^{-1})}{\del x_{j}} = \frac{\del x_{i}}{\del x_{j}} = \delta_{ij}$
