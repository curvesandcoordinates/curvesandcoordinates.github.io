# Baby Lie Groups

**Next Chapter**: [[2.4 Sub-bundles, Submersions, Foliations and Frobenius Theorem MA3205]]

> [!definition] Lie Group
> A Lie Group is a group that is a manifold as well and for which the group operations $\cdot: G \times G \to G \quad (g, h) \mapsto gh$ and $(\cdot)^{-1}: G \to G \quad g \mapsto g^{-1}$
> are smooth

> [!example] Examples of Lie Groups
>
> - $(\R^{n}, +)$ is a Lie group.
> - $GL(n, \R) = \set{A \in \R^{n \times n} \midline \det A \neq 0}$ is a group and we already saw that it is an open submanifold of $\R^{n^{2}}$. Matrix multiplication and inversion are smooth maps since they are simply calculated by polynomials thus $GL(n, \R)$.

> [!definition] Left-Invariant Vector Fields
> Let $G$ be a Lie group and $g \in G$. The maps $L_{g}: G \to G \quad h \mapsto gh$ and $R_{g}: G \to G, h \mapsto hg$ are diffeomorphisms and are called left- and right multiplication with respect to $g$.
> A vector field $X$ on $G$ is called left invariand if $\forall h \in G: \quad d_{h}L_{g}(X_{h}) = X_{gh}$

> [!example] Left Invariant Vector Fields on ${} \R^{n}$
> On $(\R^{n}, +)$ the left invariant vector fields are the constant ones.

We now want to use the Lie Bracket of these Left-invariant vector fields and see that this is a Lie Subalgebra.

> [!lemma] Lie Algebra of Left Invariant Vector Fields
> Left Invariant Vector Fields form a Lie Subalgebra

`\begin{proof}`
Obviously they do form a subspace. Moreover, for left invariant $X$ and $Y$ we find that

$$$\begin{align*}
dL_{g}([X, Y])(f) &=  [X, Y](f \circ L_{g}) = X(Y(f \circ L_{g})) - Y(X(f \circ L_{g})) \\
&= X(dL_{g}(Y)(f)) - Y(dL_{g}(X)(f)) \\
&=  X(Y_{ (g, \cdot)}(f)) - Y(X_{(g, \cdot)}(f)) \\
&=  X(Y(f) \circ L_{g}) - Y(X(f) \circ L_{g}) \\
&= dL_{g}X(Y(f)) -  dL_{g}Y(X(f))\\
&= (X(Y(f)) - Y(X(f)))|_{g(\cdot)}
\end{align*}$$
`\end{proof}`


> [!lemma] Isomorphism of Lie Algebras and Tangent Spaces at Identity
> Let $G$ be a Lie group and ${} \mathcal{g} {}$ be the Lie algebra of the left invariant vector fields on $G$. Then the map $$\Phi: \mathcal{g} \to T_{e} G, \quad X \mapsto X_{e}$$is a vector space isomorphism.

`\begin{proof}`
Obsidouly $\Phi$ is a linear map. But $\Phi$ is also injection as well since $$\Phi(X) = X_{e} = 0 \implies X(g) = d_{e}L_{g}(X_{e}) = d_{e}L_{g}(0) = 0 \implies X = 0$$Finally $\Phi$ is surjective since for any $\chi \in T_{e}G$, then we define a vector field $X$ by $$X_{g} = d_{e}L_{g}(\chi)$$ which is is left invariant since
$$d_{h}L_{g}L(X_{h}) = d_{h}L_{g}L(d_{e}L_{g}(\chi)) = d_{L_{h}(e)}L_{g}(d_{e}L_{g}(\chi)) = d_{e}(L_{g} \circ L_{h})(\chi) = d_{e}L_{gh}(\chi) = X_{gh}$$
`\end{proof}`

Asking for $\Phi$ from the above lemma to be an algebra isomorphism makes $T_{e}G$ into a lie algebra of its own and we no longer distinguish between $T_{e}G$ and $g$.[^7]

> [!example] Lie Algebra of $gl(n, \R)$
> We look at $GL(n, \R)$. We already saw that $T_{e}GL(n, \R) = \R^{n \times n} =: gl(n, \R)$. For any $\chi \in T_{e}GL(n, \R)$. The corresponding left invariant vector field is given by $$X_{A} = d_{Id}L_{A}(\chi)$$but $L_{A}(B)= AB$ is a linear map so $d_{Id}L_{A}(\chi) = L_{A}(\chi) = A \chi$
> Now what is the Lie Bracket in this situation? We will write $X_{Id}= \chi$ and $Y_{Id}= \psi$.
> Note that the [[Flow CIT413033]] of $\Phi_{t}(A)$ of $X$ is given by $$\Phi_{t}(A) = A e^{t\chi}$$since if we consider$$\at{\frac{d}{dt}}{t_{0}} \Phi_{t}(A) = \at{\frac{d}{dt}}{t_{0}} A e^{t \chi} = A e^{t_{0} \chi} \chi = d_{Id}L_{A e^{t_{0} \chi}}(\chi) = X_{A e^{t_{0} \chi}} = X_{\Phi_{t_{0}}(A)}$$
> With that we have that $$\begin{align*}
[X, Y]_{A}&= \lim_{t \to 0}\frac{d_{\Phi_{t}(A)}\Phi_{-t}(Y_{\Phi_{t}(A)}) - Y_{A}}{t}\\
&= \lim_{t \to 0}\frac{\Phi_{t}(A) \psi e^{-t \chi} - A \psi}{t}\\
&= \lim_{t \to 0}\frac{A(e^{t \chi} \psi e^{-t \chi} - \psi)}{t}\\
&= A \lim_{t \to 0}\frac{e^{t \chi} \psi e^{-t \chi} - e^{0 \chi}\psi e^{-0 \chi}}{t}\\
&= A \frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}\left( e^{t \chi} \psi e^{-t \chi} \right)\\
&= A \left( \chi e^{0 \chi} \psi e^{0 \chi} - e^{0 \chi} \psi e^{0 \chi} \chi \right)\\
&= A(\chi \psi - \psi \chi)\end{align*}$$
> So the Lie bracket on $gl(n, \R)$ is exactly the commutator: $[\chi, \psi] = \chi \psi - \psi \chi$.


> [!definition] Definitionally Identify $T_{e} G$ with its Lie Algebra
> Given a Lie group $G$ we call the Lie algebra $T_{e}G$ is called the Lie algebra of $G$.


## Some classical groups
We will now look at some more classical Lie groups and compute their Lie alrebgras

> [!definition]
> $H$ is a Lie subgroup of $G$ if
> - $H$ is (abstract) subgroup of $G$
> - $H$ is a manifold (not necessarily with the subspace topology)
> - $H$ is a topological group ( the group operations are continuous maps)
> 
> In particular the topology on $H$ need not be the one induced from $G$ 


> [!example]
> Consider the $2$-Dim torus obtained as $T^{2} = \R^{2} / \Z^{2}$ is a group under addition and a Lie group. Any straight line (through $0$) forms a $1$-dim subgroup. But if the angle of the line with the real axis is irrational it lies dense in $T^{2}$. Thus is cant have the same topology as the square, since for any open nbhd on $T^{2}$ we can not find an open set on $\R$ such that its image under the line is the nbhd intersected with the line.


> [!definition]
> $H$ is a closed subgroup of a Lie group $G$ if $H$ is (abstract) subgroup and closed subset of ${} G$. Closed subgroups are Lie subgroups and the do carry the topology induced by $G$.

The following classical matric groups are all closed subgroups of $GL(n, \R)$ or $GL(n, \C)$.

- **The Orthogonal Group:**  
	- $O(n) = \set{A \in GL(n, \R) \midline A^{T} A = Id}$ and $o(n) := T_{Id}O(n) = \set{A \in gl(n, \R) \midline X^{T} = -X}$  
	- For $A \in O(n)$ the determinant is $\pm 1$. This means that $O(n)$ has two connected components due to the smoothness of $\det$.
	- The connected component of $O(n)$ with determinant $+1$ is called the special orthogonal group $SO(n)$
- **The Special Orthogonal Group**:
	- $SO(n) = \set{A \in O(n) \midline \det A = 1}$
	- For this we can see that since the corresponding [[1.1 Tangent Space MA3205|tangent space]] at Identity has to remain the same since the identity element was in the connected component of $SO(n)$ so we get that $$so(n):= T_{Id}SO(n) \cong T_{Id}O(n) = o(n)$$
- **The Unitary Group**:
	- We see the group $U(n)$ as the isometries of $\C^{n}$ then we define $$U(n) := \set{A \in GL(n, \C) \midline A^{H}A = Id}$$where $A^{H} = \overline{A^{T}}$
	- For the [[1.1 Tangent Space MA3205|tangent space]] at the identity we can calculate as with $o(n)$ and get $$u(n) = \set{A\in gl(n, \C) \midline A^{H} = -A}$$
- **The Special Unitary Group**:
	- As in the real case we can consider the matrices that have determinant $1$ and get $$SU(n) := \set{A \in U(n) \midline \det A = 1}$$
	- The [[1.1 Tangent Space MA3205|tangent space]] now is given by $$su(n) = \set{A \in SU(n) \midline tr(A) = 0}$$

> [!definition]
> Let $G$ be a Lie group with Lie Algebra $\mathcal{G}$. For $g \in G$ the map $I_{g}: G \to G h \mapsto ghg^{-1}$ is an inner automorphism. The adjoint representation of $G$ is the map $Ad: G \to GL(\mathcal{G}), g \mapsto d_{e}I_{g}$.
> For $X \in \mathcal{G}$ the linear map $Y \mapsto [X, Y]$ is denoted by $ad(X)$. Since 
> $$ad([X, Y]) = ad(X) ad(Y) - ad(Y) ad(X)$$
>  the linear mapping $X \mapsto ad(X)$ from $\mathcal{G}$ to $gl(\mathcal{G})$ is a representation of $\mathcal{G}$.

> [!example]
> For previous matrix Lie groups we have in general that for $A \in GL(n, \R)$ and $X \in gl(n, \R)$ $$Ad(A)(X) = AX A^{-1}$$
> This generalizes to any of the matrix groups by replacing the group and its corresponding lie algebra above. This gives us that we can conjugate any element of a matrix Lie algebra with an element of our Lie group and we again get an element of our Lie algebra.

> [!theorem]
> The [[Flow CIT413033]] of $\theta$ of a left invariant vector field $X$ is defined on all of $\R$ and one finds that $$\theta_{t}(g) = g \theta_{t}(e)$$So left invariant vector fields are complete.

`\begin{proof}`
If $\theta_{t}(h)$ is defined then $g \theta_{t}(h) = \theta_{t}(gh)$ since given an integral curve $\gamma: (-\epsilon, \epsilon) \to G$ of $X$ we have that $$\frac{d}{dt} \gamma(t)|_{t_{0}} = X_{\gamma(t_{0})}$$ and also get that
$$g \gamma_{h}(t) = L_{g}(\gamma_{h}(t))$$
then $$\frac{d}{dt}(g \gamma_{h}(t))|_{t_{0}} = dL_{g} \left( \frac{d}{dt} \gamma_{h}(t)|_{t_{0}} \right) = X( g \gamma(t_{0}))$$
Now $\theta_{t}(e)$ exists for some $\epsilon > 0$ and ${} 0<|t| < \epsilon {}$. Then for $k \in \Z$ we have $\theta_{kt}(g) = g \left( \theta_{t}(e) \right)^{k}$.

`\end{proof}`

> [!definition] The Exponential map of Lie algebras
> The map $\exp: \mathcal{G} \to G, X \mapsto \theta_{1}(e)$ is called the exponential map. $t \mapsto \exp(tx) = \theta_{t}(e)$ is a smooth group homomorphism from $\R$ to $G$. Such a homomorphism is called a one-parameter-subgroup.
> 

Note that $\theta_{s}(e)$ is the integral curve of $X$ through $e$ at time $s$. Since $X$ and $tX$ have the same integral curves in a sense, since as point-sets the set of integral curves does not change but the parametrization of an integral curve of $X$ changes when viewed as one of $tX$. This is given by the fact that $X$ and $tX$ are parallel everywhere but parametrized differently.
Let $\delta$ be the integral curve of $tX$, then $\frac{d}{ds}(\gamma(ts)) = tX = \frac{d}{ds} \delta(s)$.
In our above definition this gives us that $\exp(tX) = \delta(1) = \gamma(t) = \theta_{t}(e)$

> [!remark]
> Obviously $\frac{d}{dt} \exp(tX)|_{t = 0}  = X$. Any one parameter-subgroup is generated by the exponential map, since if $\Phi$ is a one-parameter-subgroup then $$\frac{d}{dt} \Phi(t) |_{t= 0} = X(e)$$ defines a left invariant vector field $X$ and $$\frac{d}{dt} \Phi(t) |_{t_{0}} = \frac{d}{dt}\Phi(t + t_{0})|_{ t = 0} = \frac{d}{dt} \left( L_{\Phi(t_{0})}(\Phi(t))\right)|_{t_{0}} = dL_{\Phi(t_{0})}\left( \frac{d}{dt} \Phi(t) |_{t = 0} \right) = dL_{\Phi(t_{0})} X(e) = X(\Phi(t_{0}))$$ This shows that $\Phi$ is in fact integral curve of $X$

> [!example] Explicit Exponential Map on $GL(n, \R)$
> The exponential map of $GL(n, \R)$ is given by $$\exp: gl(n, \R) \to GL(n, \R) \quad X \mapsto \sum\limits_{k = 0}^{\infty} \frac{X^{k}}{k!} = e^{X}$$
> This series converges absolute and since $e^{0} = Id$ and $e^{t + s} X = e^{tX} e^{sX}$hold we find that $e^tX$ is one-parameter-subgroup. 

> [!lemma]
> $$\exp(Ad(g) X) = g \exp(X) g^{-1} = I_{g}(\exp(X))$$

`\begin{proof}`
We prove a slightly more general statement. Let $\phi: G \to G$ be an analytical group homomorphism, then $\phi(\exp(X)) = \exp(d_{e}\phi(X))$ since we can define $X':= d_{e}\phi(X)$. Then the map $t \mapsto \phi(\exp(tX))$ is one-parameter-subgroup with derivative $X'$ in $e$. The uniqueness of this one-parameter-subgroup then gives $\phi(\exp(X)) = \exp(d_{e}\phi(X))$.

The claim of the Lemma follows from this with $\phi = I_{g}$
`\end{proof}`
