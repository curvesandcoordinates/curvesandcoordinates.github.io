We will now concern ouselves with discretizing the theory of surfaces in $\R^{3}$. Similarly to the planar case we can consider the continous surface theory as a blueprint, so for this we will first consider this.

### Continous Surface Theory

For an immersion $f: U \subset \R^{2} \to \R^{3}$ we associate a map $n: U \to S^{2}$ with $n = \frac{f_{x} \times f_{y}}{\norm{f_{x} \times f_{y}}}$ called the normal or Gauss map of $f$. The area of our surface is given by the integral of determinant
$A(f) = \int_{U} \det(f_{x}, f_{y}, n)$
Central to the surface theory are three bilinear forms given by the matrices

$$$\begin{align*}
I &= \begin{pmatrix} \innprod{f_{x}}{f_{x}}  & \innprod{f_{x}}{f_{y}} \\ \innprod{f_{y}}{f_{x}}  & \innprod{f_{y}}{f_{y}}\end{pmatrix} \\
II &= \begin{pmatrix} \innprod{f_{x}}{n_{x}}  & \innprod{f_{x}}{n_{y}} \\ \innprod{f_{y}}{n_{x}}  & \innprod{f_{y}}{n_{y}}\end{pmatrix} \\
III &= \begin{pmatrix} \innprod{n_{x}}{n_{x}}  & \innprod{n_{x}}{n_{y}} \\ \innprod{n_{y}}{n_{x}}  & \innprod{n_{y}}{n_{y}}\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}$$
these are the socalled, first, second and thid fundamental forms of $f$. These act on the local tangent spaces at every point, meaning that $I_{p}$ for some point $p \in U$ is an inner product on the local tangent space.

Associated to this we consider the matrix $S := I^{-1} II$ called the shape operator. It is the representation matrix of $II$ with respect to $I$. Its eigenvalues $\kappa_{1}, \kappa_{2}$ are the principal curvatures of $f$ and one defines from this the mean and Gauss curvature as
$$H = \frac{1}{2}(\kappa_{1} + \kappa_{2})$$
and $$K = \kappa_{1} \kappa_{2}$$
With this definition we get that the characteristic polynomial of $S$ is given by
$$\chi_{S}(t) = (t - \kappa_{1})(t - \kappa_{2}) = t^{2} - 2 t H + K$$
Via the Cayley-Hamilton theorem we input the Shape Operator into its own characteristic polynomial and obtain the relation
$$III - 2 H II + KI = 0$$
which connects and relates all fundamental forms. The first and second forms are also related by the Gauss-Codazzi-Mainardi Equations.

#### Calculating Curvature by Surface Area Offset
Another useful way to calculate curvature is given by considering a family of offsets for our surface, given by
$$f^{t} = f + tn$$
We can calculate that the area of this offset surface is given by
$$\det(f^{t}_{x}, f^{t}_{y}, n) = \det(f_{x}, f_{y}, n)(1 + 2t H + t^{2} K)$$
Identifying the so-called mixed area function where the above formula is often called the **Steiner Formula**
$$A(u, v) := \frac{1}{2}\left( \det(u_{x}, v_{y}, n) + \det(v_{x}, u_{y}, n)\right)$$
this is a bilinear form on surfaces and has the property that $A(u, u) = A(u)$ the usual area for some function $u$.

This now gives us that
$$\begin{align*}
H = \frac{A(f, n)}{A(f)} \\
K = \frac{A(n)}{A(f)}
\end{align*}$$

## Discretizing this Idea
 
To discretize this idea we need to consider what the natural object is that we want to look at. It turns out to be the area of each quadrilateral, which is connected to the above Steiner Formula. We see this and from this define [[Discrete Surfaces and their Curvature]]. We are able to establish nearly alls continous properties, except the symmetry of the second fundamental form. This leads to the definition of the general “well-behaved” class of surfaces called [[Edge-Constraint Nets]].

The next natural object to consider in the theory of surfaces is special types of surfaces. Since we have defined Gauss curvature and Mean curvature in the general theory we can consider objects which have constant mean curvature. We know from continous cases that $H = 0$ is a good place start, which leads us to [[Discrete Minimal Surfaces]] which can be discribed using Weierstraß-Data in a similar theory to the continous case. Connecting [[Edge-Constraint Nets]] to the minimal surface leads us to discussing a special class of nets, called [[Cirular Nets]] which have a nice characterization for minimality.

For a big part of the theory of surfaces in the discrete case it is useful to use [[Quaternions in Differential Geometry]] as a Tool. This basically involves embedding $\R^{3}$ as the imaginary quaternions which allows for multiplication and in general nices operational capacity.

We then want to use this quaternionic theory to generate surfaces of negative Gauss curvature, which here are summarised under the concept of [[Discrete K-Surfaces]].
