## 5.1 Representations of finite Groups

Let $G$ be a group and $V$ a finite dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{K}$. Then we define
$Aut(V) = \set{F: V \to V \midline F \text{ Linear and invertible}}$
We also know that this automorphism set is a group with respect to composition. Notationally we might use
$Aut(V) = GL(V)$

> [!definition|\*] Representation
> A representation of $G$ on $V$ is a group homomorphism
> $\pi:G \to GL(V)$
> The space vector space $V$ is called the representation space of $\pi$. $d_{\pi} = \dim V$ is called the dimension of the representation and we often talk about the representation as a tuple $(\pi, V)$.

Some simple properties are given in the following Proposition

> [!proposition|\*] Simple Properties of Representations
> Given a group $G$ and a representation space $V$ such that $(\pi, V)$ is a representation of $G$ we then have the following properties:
>
> $$$\begin{align*}
> \pi(g g') &= \pi(g)\pi(g') \\
> \pi(g^{-1})&= \pi(g)^{-1} \\
> \pi(e) &= id_{V}
> \end{align*}$$
> $$$

We can also talk about invariance in this context by considering invariance of the resulting operator.

> [!definition|\*] Invariant Subspace and Subrepresentation
> Given a representation $(\pi, V)$ of $G$ we call a subspace $W \subset V$ invariant with respect to $(\pi, V)$ if
> $\forall g \in G: \pi(g) W \subset W$
> Also note that if $W \subset V$ is an invariant subspace with respect to $(\pi, V)$ then we have
> $\pi^{W}: W \to GL(V), \quad g \mapsto \pi(g)$
> is a representation on $G$. Specifically we call such a representation a subrepresentation of $\pi$ and denote it $\pi^{W} \preceq \pi$

This concept of subrepresentations now naturally give rise to the idea of reducing a representation

> [!definition|\*] Irreducible Representations
> We call a representation $(\pi, V)$ is called irreducible if $\pi^{0}$ and $\pi^{V}$ are the only subrepresentations of $\pi$

Now we will also interconnect two representations by linear invertible operators. Consider for that the following definition

> [!definition|\*] Intertwining Operators and Equivalence
> Given two representation $(\pi, V)$ and $(\tilde{\pi}, \tilde{V})$ we consider the set
> $\mathcal{C}(\pi, \tilde{\pi}) = \set{T \pi(g) = \tilde{\pi}(g) T \midline g \in G, T \in GL(V, \tilde{V})}$
> all the intertwining operators between the representations. If $\mathcal{C}(\pi, \tilde{\pi}) \neq \emptyset$ we call the representations equivalent.
>
> We also call $\mathcal{C}(\pi, \pi) =: \mathcal{C}(\pi)$ the commutator of $\pi$

Two representation $(\pi, V)$ and$\left( \tilde \pi, \tilde V \right)$ are called equivalenct if there is a linear invertible operator $T: V \to \tilde V$ with
$T \pi(g) = \tilde \pi(g) T$ for every $g \in G$. We denote this $\pi \sim \tilde \pi$ and $T$ is called the intertwining operator.

Let $\mathcal{C}(\pi, \tilde \pi)$ be the set of all intertwining operators. The set $\mathcal{C}(\pi, \pi) =: \mathcal{C}(\pi)$ is called the commutator of $\pi$.

> [!example|\*] Mother of all Representations
> Let $G$ be a finite group and $V = \C^{G}$. Then
> $\pi_{L}(g)f(u) = f(g^{-1} u)$
> is easily proven to be a representation.called the left-regular representation. Likewise we can define the right regular representation $\pi_{R}$ with
> $\pi_{R}(f)(u) = f(u g)$
> also note that $\pi_{L} \sim \pi_{R}$ which the reader should prove himself

> [!definition|\*] Unitary Representations
> The representation $(\pi, V)$ of $G$ with $(V, \innprod{\cdot}{\cdot})$ a hilbert space is called unitary if
> $\pi: G \to U(V)$
> is a homomorphism.

Hence if $(\pi, V)$ is a unitary representation (unirep. ) then
$\innprod{\pi(g)v}{\pi(g)w} = \innprod{v}{w}$
and also
$\pi(g^{-1}) = \pi(g)^{-1} =\pi(g)^{*}$

In our theory we only need to consider unitary representations. This usefulness is slightly given by the finiteness of our group (this also is case in the compact non-finite case). This means that wlog we can restrict to unireps for the remainder of this. which is justified by the following trick {which also works in compact cases}

> [!proposition|\*] Representations on Hilbert Spaces are Unitary
> Given a representation $(\pi, V)$ of $G$ on the hilbert space $(V, \innprod{\cdot}{\cdot})$ we can define scalar product
> $[v, w] = \sum\limits_{g \in G} \innprod{\pi(g) v}{\pi(g)w}$
> Then we have the following unitary equivalence
> $(\pi, (V, \innprod{\cdot}{\cdot})) \sim (\pi, (V, [ \cdot, \cdot]))$

`\begin{proof}`
Obvious
`\end{proof}`

$[\pi(g)v, \pi(g)w] = [v, w]$
meaning that in general we can only consider unitary representations on hilbert spaces since any representation can be made unitary by choosind a suitable hilbert space.

> [!proposition|\*] Equivalence gives Unitary Equivalence
> Given unireps $(\pi, V), (\tilde \pi, \tilde V)$ of $G$ which are equivalent, then they are unitarly equivalent.

`\begin{proof}`
This statement reduces to finding a unitary transformation $U \in \mathcal{C}({\pi, \tilde \pi})$. By assumption there is a $T \in \mathcal{C}(\pi, \tilde \pi)$ such that
$\pi(g) = T^{-1} \tilde \pi(g) T, \quad \forall g \in G$
Since $\pi$ is unitary we get that  $\pi(g)^{-1} = \pi(g)^{*} = ...$

plug in nicely this implies (somehow)

$\pi(g) = T^{*} \tilde \pi(g) T \implies (\pi(g))^{-1} (T^{*} T) \pi(g) = T^{*}T$
write $T$ in its polar decomposition $T = U |T|$ with $|T| = (T^{*} T)^{\frac{1}{2}}$ and $U \in \mathcal{U}(V)$ then
$\pi(g)^{-1} |T| \pi(g) = |T|$
Now since $|T|$ is invertible aswell we get
$U \pi(g) U^{-1} = T |T|^{-1} \pi(g) |T| T^{-1} = T \pi(g) T = \tilde \pi(g)$
which is that we have to show
`\end{proof}`

> [!definition|\*] Direct Sum of Representations
> Assume we have a collection of representations $(\pi_{i}, V_{i})$ of $G$ with $i \in \set{1, \cdots, M}$. Now alos let
> $V:= V_{1} \oplus V_{2} \oplus \cdots \oplus V_{M}$
> We can now canonically define a representation on $V$ by defining
> $\pi = \pi_{1} \oplus \cdots \oplus \pi_{M}$
> this means that
>
> $\pi(g)(v) = \pi_{1}(g)v_{1} + \cdots + \pi_{M}(g)v_{M}$
> If $W$ is a subspace of $V$ with respect to the representation space $V$ which is invariant (meaning $\pi(g)W \subset W$ for all $g \in G$) then $W^{\perp}$ is also invariant. We can then decompose $V = W \oplus W^{\perp}$ and $\pi = \pi^{W} \oplus \pi^{W^{\perp}}$

> [!proposition|5.6]
> Every representation of $G$ is a direct sum of finitely many irreducible representations.

`\begin{proof}`
Let $(\pi, V)$ be an reducible representation then there exists a subspace $V_{1} \subset V$ such that $\pi(g)V_{1} \subset V_{1}$. We then can write
$V = V_{1} + \oplus V_{1}^{\perp} \quad \pi = \pi^{V_{1}} \oplus \pi^{V_{1}^{\perp}}$
We can now divide up into two cases

1. Case: $(\pi, V_{1})$ and $(\pi, V_{1}^{\perp})$ are both irreducible  then we have proven our statement
2. Case: If either of our component representations are reducible we can repeat the process. But due to finite dimensionality of $V$ this can only happen finitely many times.
   `\end{proof}`

> [!proposition|5.7] Schur’s Lemma
> Let $(\pi, V), (\rho, W)$ be two representations of $G$ and $T \in \mathcal{C}(\pi, \rho)$ then the following holds
>
> 1. $\left( \pi, \ker T \right)$ is a subrepresentation of $(\pi, V)$. Similarly $(\rho, \im T)$ is a subrepresentation of $(\rho, W)$
> 2. If $(\pi, V), (\rho, W)$ are irreducible and $T \in \mathcal{C}(\pi, \rho) \setminus \set{0}$ then $(\pi, V) \sim (\rho, W)$
> 3. If $(\pi, V)$ is irreducible and $T \in \mathcal{C}(\pi)$ then $T = \lambda id_{V}$ for some $\lambda \in \C$. Conversely if $T \in \mathcal{C}(\pi) \implies T = \lambda id_{V}$ then $(\pi, V)$ is irreducible.
> 4. Suppose $(\pi, V), (\rho, W)$ are irreducible representations and that $\dim \mathcal{C}(\pi, \rho) = \begin{cases}1, \quad \pi \sim \rho \\0, \quad \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$

`\begin{proof}`
Since we have assumed that equivalence requires an invertible operator we can see that 1. and 2. are trivial.

For 3. Let us consider the eigenspace $V_{\lambda} = \set{v \in V \midline Tv = \lambda v}$ then we can see that
$T \pi(g) v = \pi(g) T v = \lambda \pi(g) v$
this means in particular that $V_{\lambda}$ is invariant under $\pi$. Then $(\pi^{V_{\lambda}}, V_\lambda)$ has to be a subrepresentation. But since irreducibility was assumed we know that this must either be trivial or the same representation as $(\pi, V)$. It trivially isnt the trivial representation meaning that $V_{\lambda} = V$ and thus
$T v = \lambda v \iff T = \lambda id_{V}$
The Converse here is an exercise.

For 4. suppose $T, R \in \mathcal{C}(\pi, \rho) \setminus \set{0}$. Since $(\pi, V), (\rho, W)$ are irreducible $T$ is an isomorphism. Then consider the map
$P = T^{-1} R: V \to V$
we then get
$P \pi(g) = T^{-1}R \pi(g) = T^{-1}\rho(g) R = \pi(g) T^{-1} R = \pi(g) P$
meaning $P \in \mathcal{C}(\pi) \setminus \set{0}$ then by 3. we get that $P = \lambda id_{V}$ giving us that
$R = \lambda T$
Thus all non-zero elements of $\mathcal{C}(\pi, \rho)$ are multiples of one another meaning that if $\pi \sim \rho$ we have $\dim \mathcal{C}(\pi, \rho) = 1$ and otherwise trivially $\dim \mathcal{C}(\pi, \rho) = 0$

`\end{proof}`

> [!corollary|\*] Irreducible Representation on Finite Abelian Groups
> Every irreducible representation of a finite abelian group $G$ is $1$-Dimensional

`\begin{proof}`
Let $G$ be an abelian group and let $g_{0} \in G$ be a fixed element.Let $(\pi, V)$ be an irreducible representation and define
$T_{g_{0}}: V \to V \quad T_{g_{0}} v = \pi(g_{0})v$
then by definition we get
$T_{g_{0}}\pi(g) = \pi(g_{0} + g) = \pi(g) \pi(g_{0}) = \pi(g) T_{g_{0}}$
thus $T_{g_{0}} \in \mathcal{C}(\pi)$. By shurs lemma we know that $T_{g_{0}} = \lambda id_{V}$.

If $V_{0} \subset V$ defined by $T_{g_{0}} v_{0} = v_{0}$ then

$\pi(g) V_{0} = \pi(g) T_{g_{0}}V_{0} \subset V$
but then $(\pi, V)$ is not irreducible which is a contradiction
`\end{proof}`

## Schur Orthogonality Relations

We want to now connect these representations to functions on our non-abelian groups.

> [!definition|\*] Matrix Coefficients of Representation
> Let $(\pi, V)$ be a unitary representation of $G$ and let $u, v \in V$ and define
> $\Phi_{u,v} (g) = \innprod{\pi(g) u}{v}$
> we call these functions $\Phi_{u,v}$ the **matrix coefficients** of the representation $(\pi, V)$

Now given any function $f \in \C^{G}$ then $f$ is a matrix coefficient since
$f(g) = \sum\limits_{x \in G} f(gx) \delta_{e}(x) = \innprod{\pi_{L} (g) \delta_{e}}{\overline{f}}$
where $\pi_{L}$ is the left-regular representation.

> [!proposition|5.9] Orthogonality
> Let $(\pi, V)(\rho, W)$ be irreducible and non-equivalent representations of $G$. Then the sets
>
> $$$\begin{align*}
> \set{\phi_{v_{1}, v_{2}} \midline v_{1}, v_{2} \in V} \quad \set{\psi_{w_{1}, w_{2}} \midline w_{1}, w_{2} \in W}
> \end{align*}$$
> are orthogonal.
> $$$

`\begin{proof}`
Let $v_{1}, v_{2} \in V$ and $w_{1}, w_{2} \in W$ and define

$$$\begin{align*}
T: V \to W, Tv &= \innprod{v}{v_{2}}w_{2} \\
\tilde T: V \to W, \tilde T w &= \sum\limits_{g \in G} \rho(g^{-1}) T \pi(g)
\end{align*}$$
by definition of $\tilde T$ we get $\tilde T \in \mathcal{C}(\pi, \rho)$. Shurs lemma implies that either $\tilde T = 0$ or $\tilde T$ is invertible since $\pi \nsim \rho$ we get

$$0 = \innprod{\tilde T v_{1}}{w_{1}}_{W} = \cdots = \sum\limits_{g \in G} \phi_{v_{1}, v_{2}}(g) \overline{\psi_{w_{1}, w_{2}}(g)}$$
hence we have orthogonality
`\end{proof}`

Now let $(\pi, V)$ be an irreducible representation of $G$ and $\set{e_{1}, \cdots, e_{d}}$ an ONB of $V$ then we write
$$\phi_{i,j}(g) = \innprod{\pi(g) e_{j}}{e_{i}}_{V}$$

> [!proposition|5.10]
> We have 
> $$\innprod{\phi_{i, j}}{\phi_{k,l}}_{\C^{G}} = \frac{|G|}{d} \delta_{ik} \delta_{jl}$$
> where $\dim V = d$

`\begin{proof}`
For $i,k \in \set{1, \cdots, d}$ define 

$$T_{ik}:V \to V, \quad T_{ik} v = \innprod{v}{e_{i}}e_{k}$$
then $tr(T_{ik}) = \delta_{ik}$
$$\tilde T_{ik}: V \to V, \quad \tilde T_{ik} = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{g \in G} \pi(g^{-1}) T_{ik} \pi(g)$$
as in proof of the previous proposition $\tilde T_{ik} \in \mathcal{C}(\pi)$

Since $(\pi, V)$ is irreducible by shurs lemma we get that
$$\tilde T_{ik} = \lambda id_{V}$$ Now we get that $d \lambda = tr(\tilde T_{ik}) = tr( T_{ik}) = \delta_{ik}$

Hence $\lambda = \frac{1}{d} \delta_{ik}$ and
$$\tilde T_{ik} = \frac{1}{d} \delta_{ik} Id_{V}$$
Now note that
$$\innprod{\tilde T_{ik}e_{j}}{e_{l}} = \frac{1}{d} \delta_{jl} \delta_{ik}$$
and

$$\innprod{\tilde T_{ik}e_{j}}{e_{l}} = \frac{1}{|G|} \innprod{\phi_{ij}}{\phi_{kl}}$$

`\end{proof}`

> [!corollary|5.11]
> Let $(\pi, V)$ be a unitary representation of $G$ and let $\set{e_{1}, \ldots, e_{d}} \subset V$ be an ONB. THen 
> 1. $\phi_{ij}( g^{-1} ) = \overline{\phi_{ji}(g)}$
> 2. $\phi_{ij}(g g') = \sum\limits_{l=1}^{d} \phi_{il}(g) \phi_{lj}(g')$
> 3. $\sum\limits_{j=1}^d \phi_{ji}(g) \overline{\phi_{jk}(g)} = \delta_{ik}$ and $\sum\limits_{i=1}^d \phi_{ji}(g) \overline{\phi_{ki}(g)} = \delta_{jk}$

`\begin{proof}`
The first statement follows from the fact that $\pi(g^-1) = \pi(g)^\ast$.

The second statement follows froom the fact that $\set{e_{i}}$ is an ONB and thus
$$\pi(g)e_{j} = \sum\limits_{i = 1}^d \innprod{\pi(g)e_{j}}{e_{i}} e_{i} = \sum\limits_{i=1}^d \phi_{ij}(g) e_{i}$$
in particular we get that
$$\pi(g g') = \sum\limits_{i} \phi(ij)(g g') e_{i} = \sum\limits_{i} \pi(g) e_{i} \phi_{ij}(g')$$
hence we get that
$$\pi(g g') = \sum\limits \innprod{\pi(g g') e_{j}}{e_{i}} = \sum\limits \phi_{il}(g) \phi_{lj}(g')$$

FOr the thirs sentence we consider that sicne $\pi(g)$ is unitary we can evaluate

$$\sum\limits_{j} \phi_{ji}(g) \overline{\phi_{jk}(g)} = \sum_{j} \innprod{\pi(g) e_{i}}{e_{j}} \overline{\innprod{\pi(g)e_{k}}{e_{j}}} = \ldots = \innprod{\pi(g)e_{i}}{\pi(g)e_{k}} = \innprod{e_{i}}{e_{k}} = \delta_{ik}$$
the remaining follows analogously
`\end{proof}`


> [!definition|5.12] General Dual Group
> The dual $\hat{G}$ of the finite group $G$ is the set of all non-equivalent unitary irreducible representations.

## Characters

For $(\pi, V)$ a representation of $G$ on an inner-product space $(V, \innprod{\cdot}{\cdot})$ the character is
$$\chi_{\pi}(g) = \mathrm{Tr}(\pi(g))$$
Reminder: If we have an inner product space $V$ and a linear operator $A: V \to V$ then
$$\mathrm{Tr}(A) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^d \innprod{Ae_{i}}{e_{i}}$$ where $\set{e_{i}}$ is an ONB for $V$.

For a character we thus get that for some ONB of $V$ that

$$\chi_{\pi}(g) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^d \innprod{Ae_{i}}{e_{i}} = \sum\limits_{i=1}^d \phi_{ii}(g)$$

> [!proposition|5.13] Properties of general Characters
> Let $(\pi, V)$ be a unitary representation on $G$. Then
> 1. $|\chi_{\pi}(g)| \leq d_{\pi}$ and $\chi_{\pi}(e) = d_{\pi}$ where $d_{\pi}$ is the dimension of the representation
> 2. $\chi_{\pi}(g^{-1}) = \overline{\chi_{\pi}(g)}$
> 3. $\chi_{\pi}(u^{-1} g u) = \chi_{\pi}(g)$ (Class function property)
> 4. For another unitary rep. $(\rho, V_{\rho})$ then $\chi_{\pi \oplus \rho} = \chi_{\pi} + \chi_{\rho}$ and $\chi_{\pi \otimes \rho} = \chi_{\pi} \chi_{\rho}$

`\begin{proof}`
**For 1**
Since $\pi$ is unitary we know that $\spec(\pi(g)) = \set{\lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{d}} \subset \mathbb{T}$ and remember that
$$|\chi_{\pi}(g)| = |\sum\limits \lambda_{i}| \leq \sum\limits |\lambda_{i}| \leq \sum\limits1 = d_{\pi}$$
and clearly $\chi_{\pi}(e) = \mathrm{Tr}(\pi(e)) = d_{\pi}$

**For 2**
Since $\chi_{\pi}(g^{-1}) = \mathrm{Tr}(\pi(g^{-1})) = \mathrm{Tr}(\pi(g)^{*}) = \overline{\chi_{\pi}(g)}$ 

**For 3**
Note here that due to invariance of the trace via orthogonal relation
$$\chi(u^{-1} g u) = \mathrm{Tr}(\pi(u^{-1} g u)) = \mathrm{Tr}(\pi(u)^{-1} \pi(g) \pi(u)) = \chi_{\pi}(g)$$

**For 4**
$$\chi_{\pi \oplus \rho}(g) = \mathrm{Tr}(\pi \oplus \rho(g)) = \mathrm{Tr}(\pi(g)) \mathrm{Tr}(\rho(g))$$
also we have that
$$\chi_{\pi \otimes \rho} = \mathrm{Tr}(\pi(g) \otimes \rho(g)) = \mathrm{Tr}(\pi(g)) \mathrm{Tr}(\rho(g)) = \chi_{\pi} \chi_{\rho}$$
`\end{proof}`

Small remark when going back to the proof you can see that some properties never use the unitarity of a representation thus our proposition can be generalised.

## Orthogonality of Characters

> [!proposition|5.14]
> Let $(\pi, V_{\pi})$ and $(\rho, V_{\rho})$ be irreducible representaitons of $G$. Then
> 1. $\innprod{\chi_{\pi}}{\chi_{\rho}} = 0$ if $\pi \nsim \rho$ 
> 2. $\innprod{\chi_{\pi}}{\chi_{\pi}} = |G|$

`\begin{proof}`
Consider two orthonormal bases $\set{e_{i}^\pi}$ and $\set{e_{i}^\rho}$ of $V_{\pi}$ and $V_{\rho}$ respectively. Then we compute

$$\innprod{\chi_{\pi}}{\chi_{\rho}} = \sum_{g \in G} \chi_{\pi}(g) \overline{\chi_{\rho}(g)} = \sum\limits_{g \in G} \mathrm{Tr}(\pi(g)) \mathrm{Tr}(\rho(g)) = \sum\limits_{g \in G} \left( \sum\limits_{i=1} \innprod{\pi(g) e_{i}^\pi}{e_{i}^\pi} \right) \overline{\left( \sum\limits_{j=1} \innprod{\rho(g) e_{j}^\rho}{e_{j}^\rho} \right)} = \sum\limits_{i=1} \sum\limits_{j = 1} \sum\limits_{g \in G} \phi_{i,i}(g) \overline{\psi_{jj}(g)} = 0$$
by proposition 5.10. The rest is the same.
`\end{proof}`

### Multiplicity
Let $(\pi, V)$ and $(\rho, V)$ two representations of $G$ and assume that $(\rho, V)$ is irreducible and $\pi = \pi_{1} \oplus \ldots \oplus \pi_{k}$ then the multiplicity of $\rho$ in $\pi$ is defined to be
$$\mathcal{m}_{\rho} = |\set{j \midline \pi_{j} \sim \rho}|$$

The multiplicity can be expressed  in terms of characters

> [!proposition|5.15]
> Let $(\pi, V)$ and $(\rho, V)$ be representations of $G$ and assume that $(\rho, V)$ is irreducible. Then
> $$\mathcal{m}_{\rho} = \frac{1}{|G|} \innprod{\chi_{\pi}}{\chi_{\rho}}$$

`\begin{proof}`
Let $\pi = \pi_{1} \oplus \ldots \oplus \pi_{k}$ and assume that all $\pi_{j}$ are irreducible. Then we know that
$$\chi_{\pi} = \sum_{j=1}^k \chi_{\pi_{j}}$$
Then by simple calculation we get that

$$\innprod{\chi_{\pi}}{\chi_{\rho}} = \sum\limits_{j = 1}^k \innprod{\chi_{\pi_{j}}}{\chi_{\rho}} = \mathcal{m}_{p} |G|$$

`\end{proof}`

Thus for any representation $(\rho, V)$ of $G$ we have that
$$\rho = \bigoplus_{\pi \in \hat{G}} m_{\pi} \pi$$
and 
$$\chi_{\rho} = \sum\limits_{\pi \in \hat{G}} m_{\pi} \chi_{\pi}$$

> [!corollary|5.16]
> 1. Let $(\pi, V)$ and $(\rho, V)$ be representations of $G$ an suppose that $$\pi = \bigoplus_{i \in I} m_{i} \pi_{i} \quad \rho = \bigoplus_{j \in J} n_{j} \pi_{j}$$ for some set of irreducible $\set{\pi_{i}}$ then we get that $$\frac{1}{|G|} \innprod{\chi_{\pi}}{\chi_{\rho}} = \sum\limits_{i \in I \cap J} m_{i} n_{i}$$
> 2. $(\pi, V)$ is ireducible iff $\innprod{\chi_{\pi}}{\chi_{\pi}} = |G|$
> 3. The representations $(\pi, V)$ and $(\rho, V)$ are equivalent iff $\chi_{\pi} = \chi_{\rho}$

`\begin{proof}`
Easy to prove
`\end{proof}`

> [!example|5.17]
> Consider again the left-regular representation $(\pi_{L}, L^2(G))$ with 
> $$\pi_{L}: G \to GL(L^2(G)), \quad \pi_{L}(g)f(x) = f(g^{-1} x)$$
> We want to first compute
> $$\chi_{\pi_{L}}(g) = \begin{cases} |G|, \quad g = e \\ 0, \quad g \neq e\end{cases}$$
> this is simply since $\dim L^2(G) = |G|$ and also $\set{\delta_{u} \midline u \in G}$ is also an ONB of $L^2 (G)$ then simply
> $$\chi_{\pi_{L}}(g) = \sum\limits_{u \in G} \innprod{\pi_{L}(g) \delta_{u}}{ \delta_{u}} = \sum\limits_{u\in G} \sum\limits_{g' \in G} \delta_{u}(g^{-1} g') \delta_{u}(g')$$
> We can also note that for this representation every irreducible representation $(\pi, V_{\pi})$ is contained in $\left( \pi_{L}, L^2(G) \right)$ with multiplicity $d_{\pi}$ meaning that in some sense the left-regular representation is the best to study since it contains all others.

> $$\innprod{\chi_{\pi_{L}}}{\chi_{\pi_{i}}} = |G| d_{\pi}$$

thus our set is certainly an orthonormal set in$L^2(G)$ but since we have proven that $L^2(G)$ is given as the orthogonal sum of $d_{\pi} V_{\pi}$ we know that it also spans this set thus its an ONB.

now we can actually define the commutator
$$\mathcal{C}(\pi, \rho) \subset S(\pi, \rho)$$
since in the commutator we consider invertible operators.

Note that $S(\pi, \rho)$ is a linear space and $\mathcal{C}(\pi, \rho)$ is not since the $0$ operator is not included.

The Shur Lemma as we have discussed it was formulated for the space $S$. We will reformulate it

> [!lemma|*] Shurs Lemma Properly
> Let $(\pi, V)$ and $(\rho, W)$ be two representations of $G$ and suppose $T \in S(\pi, \rho)$ then we have
> 1. $\pi(g) \ker T \subset \ker T$ and $\rho(g) \im T \subset \im T$ for every $g \in G$
> 2. If $(\pi, V)$ and $(\pi, W)$ are irreducible and $T \neq 0$ then $T: V \to W$ is an isomorphism. In particular the two representation are equivalent.
> 3. If $\pi$ is irreducible then there is $\lambda \in \C$ such that $T = \lambda id_{V}$
> 4. If $(\pi, V)$ and $(\rho, W)$ are reducible then $\dim S(\pi, \rho) = \begin{cases}1, \quad \pi \sim \rho \\ 0, \quad \pi \not \sim \rho \end{cases}$

## The Group Algebra in Non-Abelian Case

Let $G$ be a finite group and consider $f, g \in \C^G$. Then we write the convolution product of the two as
$$f * g (x) = \sum\limits_{y \in G} f(y) g(y^{-1} x) = \sum\limits_{y \in G} f(y) L_{y}g(x)$$
Note as $G$ is not necessarily Abelian, the convolution is not necessarily commutative.

Define an involution
$$f^*(x) = \overline{f(x^{-1})}$$

> [!proposition|5.23]
> $(L^1(G), *, ^*)$ is a normed $*$- Algbera called the **Group Algebra**

`\begin{proof}`
Clearly for $f, g \in L^1(G)$ we get that $f * g \in L^1(G)$ and also $f^* \in L^1(G)$. Moreover 

$$\norm{ f * g }_{1} = \sum\limits_{x \in G} |f * g(x)| \leq \left( \sum\limits |f(y)| \right) \left( \sum\limits |g(x)| \right) = \norm{ f }_{1} \cdot \norm{ g }_{1} $$
also we have that $^*$ satisfies alls properties that we need
`\end{proof}`

Note that the normed $*$-Algebra $L^1(G)$ is not commutative. if $G$ is non-abelian.


In general let $\mathcal{A}$ be a normed $*$-Algebra then a $*$-Representation of $\mathcal{A}$ on a hilbert space $H$ is a $*$-homomorphism 
$$\pi: \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{L}(H)$$
Thus we have
- $\pi$ is linear
- $\pi(a b) = \pi(a) \pi(b)$
- $\pi(a^*) = \pi(a)^*$ where the right star is adjoint 


A $*$-representation of $\mathcal{A}$ is called non-degenerated if there is no $v \in H  \setminus \set{0}$ with $\pi(a)v = 0$ for every $a$

Now bach to our previous setting. Let $\mathcal{A} = L^1(G)$ and $(\pi, V)$ a unitary representation of $G$. Then define 
$$\pi(f) = \sum\limits_{x \in G} f(x) \pi(x)$$
Note that this $\pi$ is a representation of $L^1(G)$ and specifically a non-degenera $*$-homomorphism.

> [!proposition|5.24]
> Let $(\pi, V)$ be a unitary representation of $G$ then
> $$\pi: L^1(G) \to \mathcal{L} (V), f \mapsto \pi(f)$$
> is a non-degenerate $*$-representation of $L^1(G)$.
> Moreover 
> 1. $\pi(x) \pi(f) = \pi\left( L_{x} f \right)$
> 2. $\pi(f) \pi(x) = \pi(R_{x^{-1}}f)$

`\begin{proof}`
Straight forward after proving everything is a valid representation
`\end{proof}`


We can reverse the concept of representation of group giving us a representation of the group algebra by considering

Let $\pi: L^1(G) \to \mathcal{L}(V)$ be a non-degenerate $*$-representation on the hilber space $V$. Then note that the following
$$\span \set{\pi(f) v \midline f \in L^1(G), v \in V} = V$$
since $id \in L^1(G)$. 

For $x \in G$ let 
$$\tilde{\pi}(x): V \to V, \quad \tilde{\pi} (\pi(f) v) = \pi(L_{x} \circ f) v$$
For $x, y \in G, f \in L^1(G)$ we can show that

$$\tilde{\pi}(xy) \pi(f) = \pi(L_{xy} f) = \tilde{\pi}(x) \tilde{\pi}(y) \pi(f)$$
and
$$\tilde{\pi}(e) \pi(f) = \pi(L_{e} f) = \pi(f)$$ hence $\tilde{\pi}(e) = id_{V}$

Further we have that
$$\norm{ \tilde{\pi}(x) \pi(f)v } = \norm{ \pi(L_{x} f) v } = \norm{ \pi(L_{x} \delta_{e} * f) v } = \norm{ \pi(L_{x} \delta_{e}) } \norm{ \pi(f) v }   $$
and since $\pi$ is a $*$-homomorphism we have
$$\norm{ \pi(L_{x} \delta_{e}) } \leq \norm{ L_{x} \delta_{e} } = \norm{ \delta_{e} }=1   $$

We get from here that $\norm{ \tilde{\pi}(x) } \leq 1$ for every $x \in G$ but we also have
$$\norm{ v } = \norm{ \tilde{\pi}(x^{-1}) \tilde{\pi}(x) v } \leq \norm{ \tilde\pi(x) v } \leq \norm{ v }   $$
hence $\norm{ \tilde{\pi}(x) v } = \norm{ v }$ and thus $\tilde{\pi}$ is unitary.

Moreover this construction is the same as the other way around meaning
$$\tilde{\pi}(f) = \pi(f)$$
which is an exercise.

The unitary representation we constructed in this way is unique. (EX)

## Fourier Transform
The fourier transform of a function $f \in L^1(G)$ 
$$\widehat{f} (\pi) = \pi(f) = \sum\limits_{x \in G} f(x) \pi(x)$$ where $\pi \in \widehat{G}$
This means that the fourier transform of a function spits out an operator (operator-valued).

> [!remark|*]
> 1. The formula makes sense for non-unitary representations
> 2. Note that if $G$ is abelian then this formular reduces to the known formula for the fourier transform on abelian groups except that we use the complex conjugate in the abelian case. We can introduce this in this generalization (by $\pi(x^{-1})$) but then the formula for the convolution product is not as nice.

> [!proposition|5.25]
> For $f, g \in L^1(G)$ then we have 
> $$\widehat{f * g}(\pi) = \widehat{f}(\pi) \circ \widehat{g}(\pi)$$

`\begin{proof}`
This follows from the fact that $\pi(f)$ is a $*$-homomorphism
`\end{proof}`

> [!proposition|5.26] Inversion Formula
> Let $f \in L^2(G)$ then 
> $$f(x) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{\pi \in \widehat{G}} d_{\pi} Tr(\pi(x^{-1}) \widehat{f}(x))$$

`\begin{proof}`
We want $f$ ins $L^2$ because we would like to apply Peter-Weyl theorem, which tells us that
$$\set{\sqrt{\frac{d_{\pi}}{|G|}} \phi_{ij}^\pi \midline i,j \in \set{1, ..., d_{\pi}}, \pi \in \widehat{G}}$$ is an ONB of $L^2(G)$. Use the conjugate basis
$$\set{\sqrt{\frac{d_{\pi}}{|G|}} \overline{\phi_{ij}^\pi} \midline i,j \in \set{1, ..., d_{\pi}}, \pi \in \widehat{G}}$$
to get

$$f(x) =  \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{\pi \in \widehat{G}} d_{\pi} \sum\limits_{ij = 1}^{d_{\pi}} \innprod{f}{\overline{\phi_{ij}^\pi}} \overline{\phi_{ij}^\pi}(x)$$
then

$$\innprod{f}{\overline{\phi_{ij}^\pi}} = \ldots = \innprod{\widehat{f}(\pi) e_{j}}{e_{j}}$$
This leads to 
$$\sum\limits_{ij = 1}^{d_{\pi}} \innprod{f}{\overline{\phi_{ij}^\pi}}\overline{\phi_{ij}^\pi}(x) = \sum\limits_{ij=1}^{d_{\pi}} \innprod{\widehat{f}(\pi) e_{j}^\pi}{e_{i}^\pi} \innprod{e_{i}}{\pi(x) e_{j}} = \ldots = Tr(\pi(x^{-1}) \widehat{f}(\pi))$$
`\end{proof}`

> [!proposition|5.26] Plancherel
> Let $f, g \in L^2(G)$ then
> $$\innprod{f}{g} = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{\pi \in \widehat{G}} d_{\pi} Tr(\widehat{f}(\pi) \widehat{g}(\pi)^\ast)$$

`\begin{proof}`
For the given $f, g \in L^2(G)$ we have

$$\innprod{f}{g}= \sum\limits_{\pi \in \widehat{G}} \frac{d_{\pi}}{|G|} \sum\limits_{ij = 1}^{d_{\pi}} \innprod{f}{\overline{\phi_{ij}^\pi}} \innprod{\overline{\phi_{ij}^\pi}}{g}$$
using previous calculations for $\innprod{f}{\overline{\phi_{ij}^\pi}}$ we conclude that
$$\innprod{f}{g} = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum\limits_{\pi \in \widehat{G}} d_{\pi} Tr(\widehat{f}(\pi) \widehat{g}(\pi)^\ast)$$
`\end{proof}`
