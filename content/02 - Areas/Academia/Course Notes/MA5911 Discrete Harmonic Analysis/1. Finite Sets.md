In discrete harmonic analysis we want to consider the situation of finite sets or finite [[Groups|groups]] and develop a theory of harmonic analysis and fourier transformations on them. To do this we first have to create a setup in which we can do general analysis on finite sets. For this we intuitively need some sort of topology, sigma-algebra and a type of measure on our finite set.

In all of the following let $X \neq \emptyset$ be a finite set. For the uniqueness of limits we would like to define a topology that is housedorf. The simplest and most intuitive choice is to pick the topology
$\mathcal{T} = \mathcal{P}(X) := \set{A \midline A \subset X}$
This is trivially a topology.
Since we also require a sigma-algebra, which in the best case is minimal and also related to our topology we can simply generate one using $\mathcal{T}$. Define this minimal or borel sigma-algebra as $\mathcal{B}(X) = \sigma(\mathcal{T})$.
By the simple argument that $\mathcal{T} \subset \mathcal{B}(X) \subset \mathcal{T}$ we can deduce that
$\mathcal{B}(X) = \sigma(\mathcal{T}) = \mathcal{T} = \mathcal{P}(X)$
Meaning our measure space we are dealing with is especially simple, since our topology and sigma-algebra exactly match.

We can now define the basics of [[Measures|measure theory]] for completion. We will only use some of the simplest cases of this in our finite theory. Defining [[Signed Measures]] in this is important since in the finite theory we still need to consider integrals.

The functions we consider and transform in the finite theory also simplify significantly since the function space
$\C^{X} = \set{f: X \to \C}$
is particularly viable as a finite-dimensional vector space. This means that [[Functions on Finite Sets]] and the [[Operators of Finite Function Spaces]] are easy to describe explicitly which we will use later to simplify a lot of our theory.
