### Birational Equivalence

A rational map is a map defined on some open subset of our variety. This is interesting because using the Zariski Topology the open Subset becomes a dense subset. Meaning that in the context of algebraic geometry these maps already define a lot of information up to close subsets (which are points or varieties)

> [!lemma|\*] Morphism Identity Theorem
> For two Varieties $X, Y$ and $\phi, \psi: X \to Y$ with some $U \subset X$ open such that $\at{\phi}{U} = \at{\psi}{U}$ then
> $\phi = \psi$

this allows us to define the following

> [!definition|\*] Rational Maps
> Given two varieties $X, Y$ a rational map $\phi: X \to Y$ is an equivalence class of pairs $(U, \phi_{U})$ where $U$ is a nonempty open subset of $X$ and $\phi_{U}: U \to Y$ is a morphism. The equivalence is given by
> $(U, \phi_{U}) \sim (V, \phi_{V}) \iff \exists W \subset U \cap V: \at{\phi_{U}}{W} = \at{\phi_{V}}{W}$

Keep in mind that this rational map is not really a map of sets, but rather a family of equivalence classes. This gets us to the following same-ness property

> [!definition|\*] Birationally Equivalent
> A birational map $\phi: X \to Y$ is a rational map which admits an inverse, namely a rational map such that it is inverse as rational maps

> [!corollary|\*] Birational Equivalence is an Equivalence of Function Fields
> For any two varieties $X, Y$ the following is equivalent
>
> 1. $X$ is birationally equivalent to $Y$
> 2. There are open subsets $U \subset X$ and $V \subset Y$ with $U \cong V$ isomorphic (topologically)
> 3. $K(X) \cong K(Y)$ as $k$-Algebras

### Arithmetic Genus

The arithmetic genus is described as a form of genus that is purely algebraic and computable. We connect it to the hilbert polynomial and only through view of cohomology can see the connection between this and the geometric genus we know and love.

First a basic definition

> [!definition|\*] Arithmetic Genus
> Given a projective variety $Y$ of dimension $r$ in $\mathbb{P}^{n}$ with hilbert polynomial $P_{Y}$ we define the **arithmetic genus** of $Y$ to be
> $p_{a}(Y) = (-1)^{r}\left(P_{Y}(0) -1\right)$

this is purely computational, we need to get to the hilbert Polynomial of a variety but we know that this is mostly an exercise in counting when it comes to finite dimensional projective varieties.

We can then note the following

> [!theorem|\*] Arithmetic Genus for Projective Curves of general Degree
> Given a projective curve $Y \subset \mathbb{P}^{2}$ of degree $d$ we can calculate the arithmetic genus directly by
> $p_{a}(Y) = \frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}$

`\begin{proof}`

#### Cohomology Groups of Manifolds

We begin by considering the geometric side of the picture. Given some smooth orientable surface a big motivator of the theory of cohomology in geometry is the idea that holes described by the geometry should somehow correspond to an algebraic object. We skim through the definitions in this section with the aim to explain what the geometric picture is behind the studied object is.

Given some smooth orientable surface or smooth curve $M$ we can consider objects called differential forms $\Omega(M)$. These are certain exterior products of elements in the dual-space of the tangent bundle. Equipped with these and a way to “differentiate” a form of order $k-1$ to get a form of order $k$ we can construct a complex.

$$$0 \longrightarrow \Omega^0(M)  
\xrightarrow{d}  
\Omega^1(M)  
\xrightarrow{d}  
\Omega^2(M)  
\xrightarrow{d}  
\cdots$$
this in turn gives us a natural cohomology groups $$H^{k}_{dR}(M) = \faktor{\ker d: \Omega^{k}(M) \to \Omega^{k+1}(M)}{\im d: \Omega^{k-1}(M) \to \Omega^{k}(M)}$$
These groups (which are in fact vector spaces under the right circumstances) cointain a lot of information concerning the geometry of the considered manifold.

#### Genus of Manifolds
We now get to the standard example of the genus, namely the counting of handles on $n$-Tori. These are in general tori with $n$ handles and we naturally say that a $n$-Torus has genus $g = n$.

This is algebraically captured by the previous construction in the dimension of the first cohomology groups which counts exactly these handles

> [!definition|*] Genus of Smoth Orientable Surfaces
> Let $M$ be a smooth orientable surface then the genus is defined as
> $$g = \frac{1}{2} \dim_{k} H^{1}_{dR}(M)$$

#### Geometric Genus in Algebraic Geometry
We can follow a similar construction as for differential geometry in the case of algebraic geomtry. We need to work with a sheaf of differential forms, denoted by $\omega_{X}$ for a given variety $X$ but in the end we obtain a similar looking definition that in its essence should be considered a cousin to the one of differential geometry

 > [!definition|*] 
> For a projective non-singular variety $X$ we call $\omega_{X}$ the sheaf of differential forms and the geometric genus
> $$p_{g} = \dim _{k} H^{0}(X, \omega_{X})$$

One should notice that since here we work with  $H^{0}(X, \omega_{X})$ namely the global sections using the sheaf of differential forms that this is in a sense directly connected to the function field of $X$. This gives us the following result relatively easily (eventhough we have left out a lot of the detail)

> [!theorem|*] Invariance of ther Geometric Genus
> Let $X$ and $X'$ be two birationally equivalent non-singular projective varieties over $k$. Then $$p_{g}(X) = p_{g}(X')$$
