> [!definition|\*] Banach Algebra
> Complex Banach Space (complete normed vector space) with a associative multiplcation such that
> $\norm{x \cdot y} \leq \norm{x} \norm{y}$
> meaning it is submultiplicative

We can work on Banach Algebras with many different properties, similar to rings. The follwwing might be important

1. **Banach Algebra with Uni** meaning there exists $e$ such that $xe = ex = x$ for all $x \in \mathcal{A}$
2. **Commutative Banach Algebra** $x \cdot y = y \cdot x$
3. **$C^*$- Algebra** meaning there exists an involution $*: \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{A}$ with $(x^*)^* = x$, $(x + \alpha y)^* = x^* + \overline{\alpha} y^*$ and $(xy)^* = y^* x^*$ and most importantly is satisfies the $C^*$ identity meaning $\norm{x^* x} = \norm{x}^2$

> [!example|\*]
>
> 1. For any compact Hausdorf Space $S$ the Banach space
>    $C(S) = \set{f: S \to C \midline f cont}$
>    is a commutative $C^*$ algebra using the supremumsnorm and the involution $f^* = \overline{f}$. It has the unit $f = 1$
> 2. Banach Space $X$ then we can consider the bounded linear operators $\mathcal{L}(X)$ which themselves form a Banach Algebra with $\mathbb{1}$ being the unit operator. The norm is the usual operator norm
>    $\norm{T} = \sup_{0 \neq x \in X} \frac{\norm{Tx}}{\norm{x}}$
>    this should be though of as the canonical example for a non-commutative banach algebra. For any Hilbert Space $H$ we can prove that $\mathcal{L}(H)$ is a $C^*$ algebra with the adjoint
>    $*: H \to H, a \mapsto a^*$
>    such that $a^*$ is the unique element such that $\innprod{av}{w} = \innprod{v}{a^* w}$

> [!lemma|\*] Norm of Involutions
> For any $x$ in $C^*$-Algebra we have
> $\norm{x} = \norm{x^*}$

`\begin{proof}`
We know that $\norm{x}^2 = \norm{x^* x} \leq \norm{x^*} \norm{x}$ implying $\norm{x} \leq \norm{x^*}$ but we also know that
$\norm{x} \leq \norm{x^*} \leq \norm{(x^*)^*} = \norm{x}$
`\end{proof}`

## Invertibility

> [!definition] Invertibility in Banach Algebra wih Unit
> Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a banach algebra with unit $e$ then we call $x$ invertivle if and only if there exists $y$
> $xy = yx = e$

Note that this leads to a definition

> [!definition]
> If for a given $x$, $y$ is the unique element such that they are inverses then we denote $y = x^{-1}$
